Ch 3 History of Life

3.1 — Age of the Earth & Radiometric Dating

What it is
Earth is ~4.568 billion years old. Radiometric dating: measure the ratio of radioactive parent isotope to daughter isotope. Each isotope decays at a fixed rate (half-life) unaffected by temperature, pressure, or chemistry — that invariance makes it a reliable clock.
Why it matters
Without dates, no timeline. Lord Kelvin said ~20 million years (wrong — ignored radioactive heat). Darwin said billions from sediment rates (roughly right). Modern physics confirmed 4.568 bya.
How it works
  1. Radioactive parent decays into daughter at a constant rate
  2. Measure current parent:daughter ratio in the rock
  3. Apply known half-life to calculate when rock solidified (reset to 100% parent / 0% daughter)
  4. Multiple isotope systems can cross-check the same sample
Parent → DaughterHalf-LifeBest For
Carbon-14 → Nitrogen-145,730 yrsRecent organic material (<50,000 yrs)
Potassium-40 → Argon-401.3 billion yrsVolcanic rocks; hominin fossils (million-year range)
Uranium-238 → Lead-2064.5 billion yrsAncient rocks (hundreds of millions to billions of years)
Rubidium-87 → Strontium-8747 billion yrsVery ancient formations; solar system age
⚠ Trap — C-14 and Dinosaurs

C-14 CANNOT date dinosaur fossils (~66+ mya). After ~250,000 years all C-14 has decayed. Use U-Pb or K-Ar. Robbins loves this.

The thing to remember is:

Short half-life = use for recent samples. Long half-life = use for ancient samples. Half-life is FIXED, unaffected by environment.

Mnemonic

“Cool Kids Usually Rock” = C-14 (recent), K-Ar (medium), U-Pb (old), Rb-Sr (ancient).

Q1
A paleontologist finds volcanic ash surrounding a ~2 million year old hominin fossil. Which isotope system is most appropriate?
✅ B. K-Ar (half-life 1.3 bya) is ideal for volcanic rock in the million-year range. C-14 only reaches ~50,000 years. Volcanic ash resets the clock when it solidifies — perfect for K-Ar.
Q2
Why was Lord Kelvin's estimate of Earth's age (~20 million years) wrong?
✅ C. Kelvin assumed Earth cooled passively. Radioactive decay generates heat continuously internally — making his cooling model invalid. Darwin's sediment-based estimate was independent and closer to correct.

3.2 — Origin of Life (Abiogenesis)

What it is
Abiogenesis = life from non-living chemistry. The prebiotic soup hypothesis: early Earth's pools/oceans contained organic molecules formed from inorganic chemistry. Energy (lightning, UV, volcanic heat) drove reactions. No O2 in early atmosphere.
Key Experiments
  1. Miller-Urey (1953): Simulated early atmosphere (CH4, H2, NH3, H2O; NO oxygen) + electric sparks → 20+ amino acids in ~1 week. Building blocks form abiotically.
  2. Fox et al. (1977): Heated amino acids at 120°C → peptide-like structures. Polymerization possible.
  3. Murchison meteorite: Purines, pyrimidines, amino acids in a meteorite — extraterrestrial organics may have seeded early Earth.
  4. RNA World Hypothesis: RNA can BOTH store genetic information AND catalyze reactions (ribozymes). May have been the first self-replicating functional molecule before DNA and proteins took specialized roles.
⚠ Trap — Miller-Urey Scope

Produced amino acids — NOT cells, NOT DNA, NOT life. Only proves building blocks can form abiotically.

The thing to remember is:

RNA World = RNA did BOTH jobs (info storage + catalysis) before specialization. Ribosomes still use rRNA as catalytic core today — a molecular fossil of this.

Q3
What makes RNA uniquely suited for the RNA World hypothesis?
✅ D. RNA's bifunctionality = the key. DNA only stores. Proteins only catalyze. RNA does both — hence the hypothetical original molecule of life.

What it is
Biomarkers = molecular evidence of life in ancient rocks (carbon isotope ratios, lipids). Earliest ~3.8 bya. Stromatolites = layered mounds from microbial biofilms; fossils at 3.45 bya. LUCA ~3.5–3.8 bya.

✅ A. Biomarkers 3.8 bya, stromatolites 3.45 bya, eukaryotes 1.8 bya, multicellular eukaryotes (red algae) 1.2 bya.

Ch 4 Phylogenetics

How to read, build, and USE evolutionary family trees.

4.1 — Tree Components

What it is
A phylogenetic tree = hypothesis about evolutionary relationships. Tips = terminal taxa; Branches = lineages through time; Nodes = speciation events; Root = most recent common ancestor of all taxa shown; Clade = node + ALL descendants. Evolution is NOT a ladder — it is a branching bush.
⚠ Trap — Reading Relatedness

Tip ORDER on a tree does NOT show relatedness. Trace back to the SHARED NODE. Two species sharing a more RECENT common node are more closely related regardless of page position. Rotating branches around a node does NOT change tree meaning.

The thing to remember is:

Relatedness = recency of shared ancestor (which node, not position). Trees are hypotheses — can be revised.

Diagram: Phylogenetic Tree Components
A & B are sister taxa (share Node B). C & D are sister taxa (share Node C). Green dashed box = a clade = Node B/C + ALL descendants. Relatedness = recency of shared node, NOT position on page.
Q7
"Sister taxa" in a phylogenetic tree are defined as:
✅ C. Sister taxa share the most recent common node — each other's closest relatives. They diverged from each other more recently than from any other taxon shown.

4.2 — Synapomorphies & Mono/Paraphyletic Groups

What it is
A synapomorphy = shared DERIVED character that evolved in the immediate ancestor of a clade and was inherited by ALL descendants. Defines a clade. Cladistics = building trees using synapomorphies only. Ancestral (plesiomorphic) characters shared by many are NOT informative for relationships within a group.
Group TypeDefinitionValid?Example
MonophyleticCommon ancestor + ALL descendants✓Mammals; Aves; Primates
ParaphyleticCommon ancestor + SOME but not all descendants✗"Reptiles" without birds; "Fish" excluding tetrapods
PolyphyleticGroup NOT including common ancestor of all members✗"Warm-blooded animals" (birds + mammals, separate origins)
⚠ Trap — Reptiles are Paraphyletic

"Reptiles" without birds is PARAPHYLETIC. Birds ARE theropod dinosaurs. Crocodilians are more closely related to birds than to lizards. To be monophyletic must include birds. Classic Robbins question.

Mnemonic

“MONO = All Kids Come Home. PARA = Some Kids Left. POLY = Different Parents Showed Up.”

Diagram: Monophyletic vs Paraphyletic vs Polyphyletic
Q8
A taxonomic group with a common ancestor and SOME but not all of its descendants is:
✅ B. Paraphyletic = ancestor + some descendants. Classic example: "Reptiles" without birds.

4.3 — Parsimony & Homoplasy

What it is
Parsimony: prefer the tree requiring FEWEST evolutionary changes. Homoplasy: similarity NOT due to common descent. Two types: (1) Convergent evolution — same trait independently evolves in separate lineages; (2) Evolutionary reversal — derived character reverts to ancestral state. Both create false groupings.
HomologyHomoplasy
CauseInherited from shared ancestorIndependent evolution OR reversal
Phylo use✓ Useful — defines clades✗ Misleading — false groupings
ExampleHuman arm + whale flipper (same bones)Dolphin + shark body shape (different ancestry, same pressure)
⚠ Trap — Convergent vs. Parallel

Both are homoplasy. Convergent = completely different genetic mechanisms (camera eye in vertebrates vs. octopus). Parallel = same genetic mechanism in related lineages (similar HOX changes). Both mislead phylogenies.

Q9
Dolphins and sharks have similar streamlined bodies despite very different ancestry. BEST described as:
✅ A. Classic convergent evolution/homoplasy. Independent evolution driven by the same selective pressure (hydrodynamic efficiency). Very different developmental genetics so NOT parallel evolution.

4.4 — Exaptation + Case Studies (Tiktaalik, Feathers)

What it is
An exaptation = trait evolved for function A, later co-opted for function B. Evolution tinkers with existing parts; new functions arise from old structures.
Feathers — Key Example
  1. Non-flying theropods had feathers (Velociraptor arm bones show quill nodes)
  2. Arms too short to fly — feathers had other functions first
  3. Original: insulation, species recognition, mate attraction, egg incubation
  4. LATER: feathers co-opted for powered flight in bird lineage = exaptation
Tiktaalik — Phylogenies Generate Predictions
Phylogenetic analysis predicted a fish-tetrapod transitional form in mid-Devonian coastal wetland rocks. Scientists searched northern Canada → found Tiktaalik roseae (375 mya): weight-bearing elbows, bending wrist, neck, no bony operculum. Prediction confirmed by fossil = scientific success.
Swim Bladder — Another Exaptation
Fish swim bladders are homologous to lungs. Ancestral lung (breathing) was co-opted into a swim bladder (buoyancy) in many fish lineages. Tetrapods kept the lung for breathing.
⚠ Trap — Feathers Did NOT Evolve FOR Flight

FALSE. Feathers evolved in non-flying dinosaurs for insulation/display. Flight came later via co-option. Original function ≠ current function = exaptation.

Q10
Swim bladders in ray-finned fish are homologous to tetrapod lungs. The BEST concept describing this is:
✅ D. Classic lecture exaptation example. Same structure (lung), different function (buoyancy) in different lineages after co-option.

✅ D. Reinforcement = selection favoring stronger mate preferences AGAINST interspecies mating because those matings produce low-fitness hybrids. Gamete waste is costly. Step 4 in allopatric speciation model (lecture slide 25).

⚠ Trap — Dispersal vs. Vicariance

Dispersal = organisms MOVE to new area. Vicariance = organisms STAY PUT, BARRIER appears. Hawaiian crickets = dispersal. Panama Isthmus = vicariance. Active vs. passive — the key distinction.

Q14
The Isthmus of Panama rose ~3 mya, separating Atlantic and Pacific shrimp populations that then diverged into distinct species. This is:
✅ D. Vicariance = geological event splits existing range; organisms don't move. The Isthmus rose, splitting Atlantic and Pacific populations. Compare to dispersal: if shrimp swam around the isthmus to colonize the other ocean, THAT would be dispersal.

Ch 14 Biogeography, Speciation & Extinction

Diversity through time and space — adaptive explosions to mass die-offs.

14.1 — Dispersal vs. Vicariance (Biogeography)

What it is
Biogeography = study of spatial and temporal distribution of biodiversity. Initiated by Darwin and Wallace. Two processes explain why related species end up in different places: dispersal (organisms move) and vicariance (barrier forms). Phylogeography = linking phylogenetic relationships to geography to reconstruct historical events.
Marsupial Case Study (Lecture)
Oldest fossils: China 150 mya → dispersed to N. America ~120 mya → S. America → crossed Antarctica (when warm) → arrived in Australia ~55 mya. Australia drifted into isolation for 40+ million years with no placental mammals (until bats/rats arrived ~15 mya). Result: Australian marsupials radiated convergently into ecological roles paralleling placentals elsewhere — marsupial mole, marsupial wolf, etc.

14.2 — Standing Diversity Math

What it is
Standing diversity (D) = number of species present at a given time. λ = origination rate (species/my). μ = extinction rate (species/my). Turnover = total originations + extinctions.
The Formula (Global Scale)
  1. D&sub2; = D&sub1; + originations − extinctions
  2. At global scale: immigration and emigration cancel out — exclude them
  3. If λ > μ: diversity increases. If μ > λ: decreases. If λ ≈ μ: stable diversity + turnover
  4. Worked example (lecture): Start 24 sp. Stage B: +10, -6 = 28. Stage C: +8, -12 = 24.
Diagram: Standing Diversity Through Time (Lecture Example)
⚠ Trap — Global vs. Local Scale

At GLOBAL scale: D&sub2; = D&sub1; + originations − extinctions ONLY. Immigration/emigration cancel globally. At LOCAL scale they matter. Robbins tests the global formula as fill-in-the-blank.

Mnemonic

“Diversity = Species Births minus Species Deaths” at global scale. λ = birth rate of species. μ = death rate of species.

Q15
A global clade starts with 50 species. Over 10 million years, 30 originate and 15 go extinct. Standing diversity at the end?
✅ C. D&sub2; = 50 + 30 − 15 = 65. Turnover = 30 + 15 = 45. λ = 3.0/my > μ = 1.5/my → clade growing.

14.3 — Adaptive Radiation

What it is
Adaptive radiation = λ greatly exceeds μ → rapid diversification into many ecological roles. Triggers: new habitat with no competitors, key innovation opening new resource space, or mass extinction removing competitors.
ExampleTrigger

⚠ Trap — Key Innovation Definition

Key innovation = NOT "the organism got smarter." It is a specific adaptation that grants ACCESS TO previously unavailable habitats or resources by removing a constraint. Eleutherodactylus key = bypasses aquatic larval competition. The removed constraint + available niche = key innovation.

Q16
Eleutherodactylus frogs underwent massive adaptive radiation primarily because:
✅ B. Directly from lecture slide 27. Direct development = eggs hatch as tiny froglets, bypassing the tadpole stage. Tadpoles compete intensely for aquatic resources. Skipping that stage removes a major constraint, opening up enormous ecological space.

14.5 — Island Biogeography (MacArthur & Wilson 1967)

What it is
Island species richness reflects a dynamic equilibrium between immigration (adds species) and extinction (removes species). At equilibrium immigration rate = extinction rate = stable species number. But species COMPOSITION keeps changing (turnover).
Key Predictions
  1. Larger islands → more species (more niches; lower extinction rate)
  2. Islands closer to mainland → more species (higher immigration rate)
  3. Large + near = most species. Small + far = fewest.
  4. Equilibrium number is STABLE; species IDENTITY still turns over
  5. Applies to any isolated habitat: forest fragments, mountaintops, nature reserves
⚠ Trap — Equilibrium = Same Number NOT Same Species

At equilibrium the NUMBER is stable but species COMPOSITION changes continuously through turnover. Equilibrium ≠ same species stay forever.

The thing to remember is:

Island biogeography is foundational for conservation — habitat fragments behave like islands. Larger + more connected reserves = more species preserved.

Q18
According to island biogeography theory, which island has the MOST species at equilibrium?
✅ D. Large = lower extinction rate (more niches, larger populations). Close = higher immigration rate. Both factors push richness up. Large + close = maximum species.

Ch 8 Humans as a Selective Force

Evolution happening right now — because of us.

8.1 — Humans as Selective Agents

What it is
Humans impose intense, consistent, directional selection on other species through hunting, medicine, agriculture, and habitat change. Because selection is strong and repeatable, evolution can be tracked in real time — years to decades, not millions of years.
Human ActivitySelection PressureEvolutionary Response
Trophy huntingRemoves individuals with largest heritable traits (horns, tusks, body size)Populations evolve smaller traits; African elephants evolving tusklessness
Antibiotic useKills susceptible bacteria; pre-existing resistant variants surviveSpread of resistance alleles; MRSA, drug-resistant TB
Pesticide useKills susceptible insects; resistant individuals reproducePesticide resistance in mosquitoes (DDT); Bt resistance in crop pests
Size-selective fishingRemoves large fish; small ones escape nets and reproduceAtlantic cod: smaller body, earlier maturation evolved over decades
Habitat fragmentationIsolates populations; reduces gene flow; increases driftLoss of genetic diversity; inbreeding; local adaptation or extinction
Selective breedingIntentional selection for desired traitsDog breeds; high-yield crops (corn from teosinte); dairy cattle
⚠ Trap — Antibiotics Don't Cause Mutations

Antibiotics do NOT cause resistance mutations. Mutations are random and pre-existing. Antibiotics KILL susceptibles and SELECT FOR pre-existing resistant variants. "Bacteria evolved resistance BECAUSE OF antibiotics" = implies directed mutation = WRONG.

The thing to remember is:

Humans are the most powerful selective force on Earth today. Strong selection = fast evolution. Antibiotic resistance: days. Fish body size: decades. We are running an unplanned global evolution experiment.

Q19
Over decades, Atlantic cod show smaller body size and earlier maturation. Which principle BEST explains this heritable change?
✅ B. Directional selection by humans as a size-selective predator. Nets consistently remove large individuals. Over generations, alleles for small body size + early maturation increase in frequency. Genuine genetic evolution (heritable change), not just plasticity.
Q20
A patient stops antibiotics after 3 days feeling better. The most likely evolutionary outcome:
✅ C. Stopping early kills the most susceptible cells first. Pre-existing resistant variants — always there at low frequency — now have no competition and no antibiotic pressure. They multiply. Antibiotics don't cause new mutations; they select from existing variation.

Ch 17 Human Evolution

~7 million years from a common ancestor with chimps to modern Homo sapiens.

17.1 — Humans as Primates

What it is
Linnaeus (1758) placed humans in Order Primates based on anatomical similarities: forward-facing eyes, gripping hands with nails, large relative brain. Darwin (1871, Descent of Man) explained why: common ancestry with apes. Molecular phylogenetics unequivocally places humans nested within Primates. Most recent common ancestor of all primates: ~66–69 mya (K-T boundary).
Molecular Evidence of Kinship
Human chromosome 2 = fusion of chimp chromosomes 2A + 2B (telomeric sequences in the middle prove fusion — definitive molecular evidence of common descent). ~98.5% DNA similarity with chimpanzees. Coccyx = vestigial tail (homologous to primate tails).
⚠ Trap — "We Evolved FROM Chimps"

FALSE. Humans and chimps share a COMMON ANCESTOR ~7 mya. We are SISTER LINEAGES, not ancestor/descendant. Neither is the ancestor of the other. Both have been evolving since the split. Correct statement: "humans and chimps diverged from a common ancestor ~7 mya."

Q21
Human chromosome 2 contains telomeric sequences in its middle (normally only at chromosome ends). The BEST explanation:
✅ B. Telomeric sequences in the middle of chromosome 2 = molecular signature of end-to-end fusion. The fusion happened in the human lineage after the split from the common ancestor with chimps (who still have two separate chromosomes). Powerful molecular evidence of common descent.

17.3 — Bipedalism

What it is
Habitual upright locomotion on two legs. Evolved ~6–7 mya. Bipedalism was the FIRST major hominin innovation; large brain expansion came LATER.
Fossil Evidence

17.5 — Neanderthals, Modern Humans & Out of Africa

What it is
Out of Africa hypothesis: anatomically modern H. sapiens evolved in Africa (~195 kya) and expanded globally beginning ~70–50 kya, largely replacing other hominin populations but with interbreeding. Svante Pääbo's ancient DNA work (Nobel Prize 2022) provided molecular evidence.
Key Ancient DNA Findings
  1. Neanderthals diverged from H. sapiens lineage ~600–800 kya
  2. Interbreeding after H. sapiens left Africa → non-African humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA; sub-Saharan Africans carry little to none
  3. Denisovans (Siberia; known from a finger bone + DNA) also interbred → some Melanesians carry ~5% Denisovan DNA
  4. Neanderthals extinct ~40 kya — reasons debated: outcompeted, assimilation, disease, climate
  5. Oldest H. sapiens: Omo Kibish, Ethiopia (~195 kya)
⚠ Trap — Out of Africa vs. Multiregional

Multiregional: H. sapiens evolved simultaneously in multiple regions from H. erectus with continuous gene flow. Out of Africa: evolved once in Africa, spread and replaced others (with some interbreeding). Genetic evidence strongly supports Out of Africa. The geographic pattern of Neanderthal DNA (non-Africans only) PROVES it was post-migration interbreeding, not just shared ancestry.

The thing to remember is:

Non-African humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA. Geographic restriction to non-Africans = proof of post-migration interbreeding. All humans trace to African origins. African populations have greatest genetic diversity.

18.2 — Evolving Pathogens & Virulence

What it is
Virulence = degree of harm a pathogen causes its host. Pathogens evolve like any organism. Fitness = within-host replication × transmission to new hosts. This creates a fundamental virulence trade-off: maximizing replication may reduce transmission by killing the host too fast.
Virulence Trade-off Logic
  1. High virulence → more replication → more pathogen output per unit time
  2. But high virulence → kills/incapacitates host faster → fewer total transmission events
  3. Selection favors INTERMEDIATE virulence in most cases
  4. Exception: if transmission doesn't need active host (water-borne = cholera), high virulence can be maintained — bedridden/dead hosts still contaminate water supplies
Antibiotic Resistance Mechanisms
Bacteria evolve resistance through: (1) point mutations altering drug targets; (2) enzymes degrading antibiotics (beta-lactamases destroy penicillin); (3) efflux pumps expelling drugs from the cell; (4) horizontal gene transfer (HGT) — resistance plasmids passed between bacterial species, spreading resistance across phylogenetic lines rapidly.
Myxoma Virus Case Study
Introduced to Australia (1950) to kill invasive rabbits. Initially killed 99.8%. Within years: virus evolved LOWER virulence (rabbits survived longer = more mosquito feeding = more transmission). Rabbits evolved resistance. Classic coevolutionary arms race + virulence trade-off in real time.
⚠ Trap — Virulence Always Decreases?

WRONG. Virulence evolves toward whatever maximizes pathogen FITNESS. Water-borne pathogens (cholera) can maintain HIGH virulence. Evolution has no direction and no "niceness." Transmission mode is the key determinant.

13.4 — Allopolyploidy (Instant Speciation) & Haldane's Rule

What it is
Allopolyploidy = hybridization between two different species + whole-genome duplication. The offspring has a complete chromosome set from BOTH parents. Because it has a different ploidy level from either parent species, it is immediately reproductively isolated from both parents — speciation in ONE GENERATION (instant speciation). Most common in plants; ~50% of all flowering plants have polyploid ancestry.
How Allopolyploidy Creates Instant Speciation
  1. Species A (2n=14) × Species B (2n=14) → hybrid offspring (n=7 from each parent = 14 total, BUT chromosomes can't pair in meiosis — hybrid is sterile)
  2. Accidental genome duplication (e.g., failure of cell division) → allotetraploid offspring (2n=28: two complete sets from A + two from B)
  3. Chromosomes CAN now pair properly in meiosis (each set has a match)
  4. Allotetraploid is fertile but reproductively isolated from both parents — crossing back = ploidy mismatch → sterile offspring
  5. New species formed in a single generation with no geographic isolation required
Real Examples
Tragopogon (goatsbeard): two allopolyploid species formed in the Pacific Northwest within the last ~80 years from European species introduced to North America. Directly observed forming in real time. Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum): hexaploid (6n=42) from three ancestral grass species (AA + BB + DD) via two sequential allopolyploidy events over ~8,000 years.
Haldane's Rule (1922)
When hybrid offspring of one sex are inviable or sterile, it is more often the heterogametic sex (XY males in mammals/flies, ZW females in birds/butterflies). WHY: recessive incompatibility alleles on the X chromosome are exposed (hemizygous) in XY males (only one X) but masked in XX females (two X copies, second can compensate). XY males have no backup copy, so incompatibilities hit them first.
⚠ Trap — Allopolyploidy vs Autopolyploidy

Allopolyploidy = hybridization between TWO DIFFERENT SPECIES + genome duplication. Autopolyploidy = genome duplication within ONE species (extra copies of same genome). Allopolyploidy is far more common and evolutionarily important because the different chromosome sets pair properly in meiosis. ALLO = two different parents. AUTO = copies of yourself.

⚠ Trap — Allopolyploidy Requires Geographic Isolation?

NO. Allopolyploidy creates reproductive isolation IN A SINGLE GENERATION without any geographic separation. It is one of the few routes to sympatric speciation that is well-documented and uncontroversial. Plants are particularly prone to this because they tolerate polyploidy better than animals.

The thing to remember is:

Allopolyploidy = fastest possible speciation: 1 generation, no geography needed, immediately isolated from parents. ~50% of flowering plants have polyploid ancestry. Mule sterility is the FAILURE of this — no duplication step, so you get sterile hybrid not new species.

✅ C. This is allopolyploidy in action. The genome doubling gives each chromosome a pairing partner — meiosis now works correctly, so the offspring is fertile. 14.6 — Wallace Line, Conservation Genetics & 50/500 Rule

Wallace Line
A sharp biogeographic boundary running through the Malay Archipelago (between Bali and Lombok; between Borneo and Sulawesi). West of line: Asian fauna (primates, tigers, elephants). East of line: Australasian fauna (marsupials, cockatoos). The line marks the edge of the continental shelves of Asia and Australia. Even during ice age low sea levels, a deep water channel remained — preventing most organisms from crossing. Discovered by Alfred Russel Wallace in 1859. Classic example of vicariance creating biogeographic patterns.
Conservation Genetics: The 50/500 Rule
Minimum viable population size guideline: (1) Ne ≥ 50 in the short term to prevent rapid inbreeding depression (limits inbreeding rate to ~1% per generation); (2) Ne ≥ 500 in the long term to maintain sufficient genetic variation for adaptive evolution. Ne (effective population size) is always smaller than N (census population) due to unequal sex ratios, variance in reproductive success, and population fluctuations. Some scientists suggest 100/1000 as more realistic.
Inbreeding Depression
Inbreeding increases homozygosity. Mechanisms of harm: (1) deleterious recessive alleles normally masked in heterozygotes become expressed in homozygotes; (2) loss of heterozygote advantage (e.g., MHC immune genes where heterozygotes recognize more pathogens). Classic example: cheetahs have so little genetic diversity that skin grafts between unrelated individuals are accepted without immune rejection (normally rejected due to MHC diversity). Result: ~70% abnormal sperm, high disease susceptibility.
Parapatric Speciation
A third speciation mode (between allopatric and sympatric). Populations have PARTIAL geographic separation — they meet along a narrow contact zone where some gene flow occurs. Strong selection gradients on each side can overcome gene flow, driving divergence. If assortative mating evolves in the contact zone, full speciation can result. Less common than allopatric; harder to document than sympatric.
⚠ Trap — Ne vs. N

Effective population size (Ne) is ALWAYS smaller than census population (N). Ne reflects the number of breeding individuals that contribute equally to the next generation. Most real populations have Ne much smaller than N due to unequal reproductive success, sex ratio imbalances, and population bottlenecks. Conservation policy targets Ne, not N.

Q33 — Wallace Line
The Wallace Line separates Asian and Australasian fauna even though the islands are geographically close. The BEST explanation is:
✅ D. The Wallace Line marks the edge of the continental shelves. Even when sea levels dropped during ice ages (exposing many land bridges), a deep ocean channel remained between Asia and Australia. This water barrier prevented most terrestrial animals from crossing — creating the sharp biogeographic discontinuity Wallace documented in 1859.
Q34 — 50/500 Rule
A wildlife conservation plan proposes maintaining a breeding population of 45 individuals. The 50/500 rule suggests this is:
✅ B. The 50/500 rule: Ne ≥ 50 is the short-term minimum to keep inbreeding rate below ~1% per generation. Ne ≥ 500 is the long-term minimum to maintain adaptive genetic variation. 45 individuals falls below even the short-term threshold. Also note: Ne < N in real populations, so 45 census individuals = even fewer effective breeders.

8.2 — Specific Human-Driven Evolution Examples

Trophy Hunting — Bighorn Sheep
Trophy hunting selectively removes individuals with the LARGEST traits (biggest horns = most desirable trophy). Over 30 years of trophy hunting in Canadian bighorn sheep: average horn size declined approximately 20%. Mechanism: large-horned males killed before they can maximize reproduction → genes for smaller horns increase in frequency. This is directional selection opposing natural selection (which favors larger horns for male–male competition). The evolutionary change may be difficult to reverse because the alleles for larger horns have been depleted.
Tusklessness in African Elephants
In Mozambique, decades of intense ivory poaching during the civil war (1977–1992) drove rapid evolution of tusklessness in elephants. Before the war: ~18% of females were naturally tuskless. After: ~33% tuskless in heavily poached populations. Tusklessness is heritable and linked to a dominant allele. Selection coefficient was extremely strong — tusked females were killed. Classic human-imposed directional selection on a heritable trait.
Antibiotic Resistance — Mechanisms
Four main mechanisms: (1) Target modification — mutations alter the antibiotic binding site (e.g., ribosome changes block tetracycline); (2) Enzymatic degradation — beta-lactamases destroy the beta-lactam ring of penicillin/ampicillin; (3) Efflux pumps — membrane proteins actively pump antibiotics OUT of the cell; (4) Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) — resistance plasmids passed between bacterial species via conjugation, transformation, or transduction — spreads resistance ACROSS species lines.
⚠ Trap — Trophy Hunting Selection Direction

Trophy hunting creates selection in the OPPOSITE direction from natural selection. Natural selection favors LARGE horns (male competition for mates). Trophy hunting removes large-horned individuals before they can fully reproduce, favoring SMALL horns. This is one of the clearest demonstrations that humans impose strong, unnatural selection pressures on wild populations.

✅ C. Trophy hunters preferentially kill the largest-horned males (highest trophy value). Large-horned males that would normally maximize fitness through many matings are killed early, before they can fully pass on their genes. Small-horn alleles increase in frequency over generations — the opposite of what natural selection would produce. This is human-imposed directional selection.

17.6 — Denisovans, Anatomically vs. Behaviorally Modern, Brain Evolution

Denisovans
An archaic hominin group identified primarily through DNA from a finger bone in Denisova Cave, Siberia (2010). Known from very few fossils but extensive genomic data. Diverged from Neanderthals ~400 kya. Interbred with modern humans: Melanesians/Papua New Guineans carry ~3–6% Denisovan DNA; some mainland Asian and Indigenous Australian populations also show traces. Key finding: the EPAS1 gene (high-altitude adaptation) in Tibetans originated from a Denisovan gene variant. Modern humans acquired a Denisovan adaptation through interbreeding — direct fitness benefit from archaic introgression.
Anatomically Modern vs. Behaviorally Modern H. sapiens
Anatomically modern H. sapiens: oldest fossils ~195 kya (Omo Kibish, Ethiopia) / ~315 kya revised (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco). Have modern skeletal features (high domed skull, reduced brow ridges, chin).
Behaviorally modern H. sapiens: appear ~70–100 kya. Show symbolic thought, abstract art, personal ornaments (shell beads), long-distance trade networks, composite tools. There was a ~100,000 year gap between anatomical modernity and full behavioral modernity.
What Drove Brain Expansion? (Multiple Hypotheses)
Social brain hypothesis: tracking complex social relationships in large groups favored larger neocortex. Ecological hypothesis: environmental problem-solving (foraging, tool use) drove brain expansion. Cooking hypothesis (Wrangham): cooking food with fire dramatically increased caloric yield from same raw ingredients → provided energy to support larger, metabolically expensive brains. Cooking may have made brain expansion energetically feasible.
MHC and Mate Choice
Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) = immune gene region with extreme polymorphism. Studies show humans prefer mates with DIFFERENT MHC types (detected via body odor — "sweaty T-shirt" experiments). Benefit: offspring with diverse MHC can recognize more pathogens. This is one of the few documented cases of genetic mate choice in humans driven by immune gene diversity.
⚠ Trap — Anatomical vs. Behavioral Modernity

Anatomically modern H. sapiens (modern skeleton) appeared ~195 kya (or ~315 kya with latest revisions). Behaviorally modern humans (art, symbols, long-distance trade) appeared only ~70–100 kya. There was a gap of ~100,000–200,000 years where humans looked like us but didn't yet act like us. Don't conflate these two milestones.

⚠ Trap — Denisovan DNA Location

Denisovan DNA is found primarily in Melanesians/Pacific Islanders (~3–6%) and some Asian populations — NOT in sub-Saharan Africans or Western Europeans at comparable levels. Compare: Neanderthal DNA is in ALL non-African modern humans (~1–4%). The different distributions reflect different geographic regions where interbreeding occurred.

Q37 — Denisovans
A Tibetan high-altitude adaptation gene (EPAS1) was found to have originated from Denisovans. This demonstrates:
✅ C. Adaptive introgression = when gene flow from an archaic/related population introduces an allele that is then positively selected. Denisovans (who lived in Asia including high-altitude regions) already had an EPAS1 variant adapted for low oxygen. When H. sapiens interbred with Denisovans, this variant entered the human gene pool. At high altitudes in Tibet, it was strongly favored. ✅ B. There is a ~100,000–200,000 year gap between anatomical modernity (modern skeleton at ~195 kya+) and behavioral modernity (symbolic thought, art, ornaments, long-distance trade at ~70–100 kya). These Omo fossils had modern bodies but pre-modern behavior. Modern skulls came first; modern minds came later.

18.4 — Sickle Cell, Cancer as Somatic Evolution & More Trade-offs

Sickle Cell: Balanced Polymorphism
Sickle cell is caused by a single nucleotide change in beta-globin: Glutamic acid → Valine (Glu6Val). HbS/HbS homozygotes: severe sickle cell anemia (sickling of red blood cells, chronic illness, early death without treatment). HbA/HbS heterozygotes: ~25% protection against severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria (parasitized cells sickle and are cleared by spleen; intracellular environment hostile to parasite). In malaria-endemic Africa, HbS maintained at 10–20% frequency — classic heterozygote advantage (balancing selection via overdominance). In non-malarial regions: HbS is purely deleterious (no compensating benefit for heterozygotes).
Cancer as Somatic Evolution
Cancer is evolution happening INSIDE the body within a single lifetime. Key parallels: (1) Heritable variation: somatic mutations create variant cell lineages; (2) Natural selection: cells with mutations allowing faster proliferation, immune evasion, or metastasis are selected; (3) Adaptation: tumors evolve resistance to chemotherapy (same mechanism as antibiotic resistance). Why doesn't selection eliminate cancer-causing alleles? Because most cancers hit late in life — antagonistic pleiotropy: p53 prevents cancer early (fitness benefit) but its high activity may accelerate aging (cost). Also: high cell division rates in early development are good for growth but increase mutation risk later.
p53 — The Guardian of the Genome
p53 = tumor suppressor protein encoded by the TP53 gene. Triggers cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, or apoptosis (programmed cell death) when DNA damage is detected. Early-life benefit: prevents cancer during reproductive years. Late-life cost: excessive p53 activity may deplete stem cells → tissue atrophy, reduced regeneration = accelerated aging. Mouse experiments: enhanced p53 → reduced cancer BUT accelerated aging. Classic antagonistic pleiotropy.
Evolutionary Mismatch: The Back
The human spine evolved from a horizontal quadruped design (~300 million years of evolution) and was co-opted for vertical bipedalism only ~6–7 million years ago. The S-curve compensates for upright posture but creates mechanical vulnerabilities: lumbar disc herniation, vertebral compression fractures, spondylolisthesis, chronic lower back pain. ~80% of humans experience significant back pain. This is evolutionary constraint (Reason #4): selection can only tinker with existing structures, not redesign from scratch.
⚠ Trap — Sickle Cell Heterozygote Fitness

In malaria-endemic regions: fitness ranking is HbA/HbS > HbA/HbA > HbS/HbS. Heterozygotes have the HIGHEST fitness (heterozygote advantage). In non-malarial regions: HbA/HbA > HbA/HbS > HbS/HbS (sickle cell is just deleterious). The SAME genotype has completely different fitness in different environments. This is why the allele is found at high frequency in sub-Saharan Africa but not in non-malarial populations.

⚠ Trap — Cancer Is Evolution

Cancer IS evolution — it is natural selection acting on somatic cell lineages within a body. Cells with mutations enabling faster proliferation, immune evasion, or metastasis are "selected for" within the tumor microenvironment. Tumors evolve drug resistance by the same mechanism bacteria evolve antibiotic resistance: pre-existing variation + selection. Chemotherapy resistance = same logic as antibiotic resistance.

Q39 — Sickle Cell Balanced Polymorphism
In malaria-endemic West Africa, the HbS allele is maintained at ~10–20% frequency despite causing severe anemia in homozygotes. This is BEST explained by:
✅ B. This is the classic heterozygote advantage (overdominance) example. HbA/HbS heterozygotes are protected from severe malaria — parasitized cells sickle and are cleared. This fitness advantage in malaria-endemic regions outweighs the cost of homozygous HbS/HbS disease. Selection maintains both alleles at intermediate frequencies. In non-malarial regions, HbS is purely deleterious (no compensating benefit), explaining its low frequency in non-African populations.
Q40 — Cancer as Somatic Evolution
A tumor initially responds to chemotherapy but after several months becomes drug-resistant. By analogy with antibiotic resistance, the MOST likely explanation is:
✅ C. This is somatic evolution in action — identical mechanism to antibiotic resistance. Tumors are genetically heterogeneous (different cell lineages with different mutations). Chemotherapy kills susceptible cells. Pre-existing resistant variants (random mutations already present before treatment) are selected for and expand. This is why cancer treatment strategies now focus on targeting multiple pathways simultaneously — to prevent resistant clones from expanding.
Q41 — Which is NOT a reason we are vulnerable to disease (Nesse 2005)?
Which of the following is NOT one of Nesse's six evolutionary reasons why humans remain vulnerable to disease?
✅ D. D is the WRONG one — natural selection optimizes REPRODUCTIVE FITNESS, NOT health or longevity. This is actually the central insight of evolutionary medicine. Two populations of plants look morphologically different but readily produce fertile hybrids in the lab. Under the Biological Species Concept they would be considered _____, but under the Morphospecies Concept they would be considered _____.
✅ A. This scenario shows where BSC and morphospecies DISAGREE. Under BSC: fertile hybrids = gene flow possible = SAME species (not reproductively isolated). Q44 — Which is NOT a prezygotic barrier?
Which of the following is NOT a prezygotic reproductive isolating mechanism?
✅ D. D is POSTZYGOTIC — the hybrid is formed (zygote exists) but is sterile. This is hybrid sterility, a postzygotic barrier. A = behavioral isolation (prezygotic, pre-mating). Q45 — Standing Diversity Calculation
A clade has 100 species. Over 4 million years, the origination rate is 5 species/my and extinction rate is 8 species/my. What happens to standing diversity?
✅ B. Net change = λ − μ = 5 − 8 = −3 species/my. Over 4 my: −3 × 4 = −12 species. 100 − 12 = 88 species. The clade is declining but hasn't gone extinct in just 4 million years. Extinction would require μ ≫ λ sustained much longer.

Which of the following is NOT a standard component of a phylogenetic tree?

Q47 — Scenario: Parallel or Convergent?
Two closely related species of salamanders that share ~90% of their DNA both independently evolved reduced limb lengths via changes to the same HOX gene regulatory region. This is BEST described as:
✅ B. Parallel evolution = similar outcome via the SAME genetic mechanism in closely related lineages. Key markers here: (1) closely related (~90% DNA similarity), (2) same molecular mechanism (same HOX gene regulatory change). Convergent evolution typically involves DIFFERENT mechanisms in more distantly related lineages (e.g., camera eye in vertebrates vs. octopus). Both are homoplasy; the distinction is mechanism and relatedness.
Q48 — Great Oxidation Event
The Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 bya) was caused by:
✅ A. Cyanobacteria evolved oxygenic photosynthesis ~2.6 bya, producing O2 as a byproduct. This O2 accumulated in the atmosphere, triggering the Great Oxidation Event ~2.4 bya. Consequences: mass extinction of obligate anaerobes, formation of the ozone layer, and eventual evolution of aerobic respiration. Mitochondria (B) are thought to have originated ~1.8 bya as endosymbionts — AFTER the GOE.
Q49 — Scenario: Adaptive Radiation Trigger
After a mass extinction 66 mya, mammals rapidly diversified into many new ecological roles previously occupied by dinosaurs. The BEST description of this event:
✅ C. This is the classic "extinction-triggered adaptive radiation." Mammals had been small and ecologically limited for ~160 million years due to competitive exclusion by dinosaurs. When the K-T extinction removed this competition, ecological niches became available. Within ~10 million years, all major mammalian orders appeared. This is NOT key innovation — mammals didn't evolve anything new to outcompete dinosaurs; rather, the competitors were removed.
Q50 — Virulence and Transmission Mode
A newly discovered pathogen is transmitted ONLY through direct physical contact with infected individuals. Evolutionary theory predicts this pathogen will evolve toward:
✅ A. For contact-transmitted pathogens, transmission REQUIRES the host to be mobile and socially active (coming into physical contact with others). If the pathogen kills or bedrids the host, transmission stops. Selection therefore strongly favors lower/intermediate virulence. Compare to cholera (water-borne) where the host doesn't need to be active for transmission — cholera can be highly virulent because dying hosts still contaminate water. Transmission mode is the key determinant of optimal virulence.
Q51 — Scenario: Which speciation mode?
Two species of fish live on opposite sides of a coral reef. They can see and smell each other but only mate with members of their own species due to color pattern differences that drive mate choice. No physical barrier separates them. This is BEST classified as:
✅ B. The two fish occupy the same geographic area with no physical barrier between them. The reproductive isolation is BEHAVIORAL (color pattern-based mate choice), not geographic. This is sympatric speciation maintained by assortative mating. D is wrong — sharing a habitat doesn't prevent speciation; sympatric speciation is specifically when new species form WITHOUT geographic isolation.
Q52 — Phylogenetics: Parsimony Application
Two possible trees for 3 species (A, B, C) require 5 and 7 evolutionary steps respectively. Under the principle of parsimony, you should:
✅ A. Parsimony = choose the tree requiring the fewest evolutionary changes as the WORKING HYPOTHESIS. More steps = more homoplasy assumed = less parsimonious. The 5-step tree is preferred. This doesn't mean the 5-step tree is definitely correct — it's a hypothesis that can be revised with new evidence (other methods like Bayesian or maximum likelihood may give different results, especially when homoplasy is common).
Q53 — Fill-in: Hominin Brain Sizes
Arrange these hominins in order of INCREASING brain size (smallest to largest average):
✅ D. Brain sizes: Australopithecus ~450cc (ape-sized), H. erectus ~900–1100cc, H. sapiens ~1350cc average, Neanderthal ~1500cc (LARGEST on average). This is a key numerical anchor — Neanderthals had larger brains than modern humans on average. Brain expansion was gradual through the Homo lineage but Neanderthals exceeded our average.
Q54 — Which is NOT evidence of human-chimp common ancestry?
Which of the following is NOT evidence supporting the common ancestry of humans and chimpanzees?
✅ C. Ecological similarity does NOT demonstrate common ancestry — that would be convergent evolution (organisms in similar environments may evolve similar traits independently). Actual evidence for common ancestry is molecular (chromosome 2 fusion, DNA similarity) and anatomical (homologous structures like coccyx). Ecological similarity alone is irrelevant to phylogenetic relationships.
Q55 — Mass Extinction: Permian Cause
The End-Permian "Great Dying" (~252 mya) is MOST associated with which cause?
✅ B. End-Permian = Siberian Traps. Massive volcanism released enormous CO2 + SO2, causing: runaway greenhouse warming, ocean acidification (CO2 dissolved in seawater), marine anoxia (low oxygen), and ozone depletion. ~96% of marine species and ~70% of terrestrial vertebrates went extinct. Recovery took 5–10 million years. Chicxulub asteroid = K-T (~66 mya), not Permian. Glacial episodes = End-Ordovician.

Early fish evolved lungs to gulp air from the surface, supplementing O2 in low-oxygen water. In many ray-finned fish lineages, this lung was later modified into a swim bladder for buoyancy. This is an example of:
✅ C. Classic exaptation (from lecture). The lung evolved for BREATHING (original function). In many fish lineages, it was co-opted for BUOYANCY (new function). Q61 — Polyploid speciation timeline
How many generations does allopolyploid speciation require?
✅ A. Allopolyploidy = INSTANT SPECIATION. The doubled genome creates immediate reproductive isolation: when the allopolyploid tries to mate with either parent species, the ploidy mismatch (e.g., 4n × 2n = 3n offspring) causes meiotic failure. New species in ONE generation. This is one of the strongest arguments that speciation can be extremely rapid.
Q62 — Rhagoletis key detail
Why is Rhagoletis pomonella considered an example of sympatric speciation IN PROGRESS (not completed)?
✅ B. Rhagoletis is a MODEL of speciation IN PROGRESS — not completed. The apple and hawthorn populations show: genetic divergence at several loci, different emergence timing (temporal isolation developing), and assortative mating (flies mate on their host plant). BUT some interbreeding still occurs. This makes it ideal for studying the early stages of speciation.
Q63 — Adaptive radiation vs. allopatric speciation
Hawaiian honeycreepers (>50 species) evolved from a single ancestor that colonized ~5 mya. This is BEST classified as:
✅ C. This is adaptive radiation triggered by: (1) colonization of a new habitat (Hawaii), (2) no prior competitors (ecological opportunity). Within the archipelago, allopatric speciation between islands also contributes. The key features of adaptive radiation are: single ancestor + rapid diversification + many ecological roles. Calling it purely allopatric misses the ecological opportunity driver.
Q64 — Mass extinction: which caused the most harm to mammals?
Which mass extinction actually BENEFITED the mammalian lineage by creating the conditions for their diversification?
✅ B. The K-T extinction was catastrophic for most groups but was the BEST thing that ever happened to mammals. For ~160 million years, dinosaurs monopolized large-body ecological niches. The asteroid killed them (and ~76% of other species). Within ~10 million years after, ALL major mammalian orders appeared — whales, bats, elephants, primates, etc. Extinction-triggered adaptive radiation.
Q65 — Island Biogeography applied to conservation
A city proposes replacing a large forest with 10 small isolated forest patches of equal total area. Island biogeography theory predicts this will:
✅ C. Island biogeography applied to conservation: habitat fragments = islands. Smaller patches = smaller populations = higher extinction rates per patch. Isolation = lower immigration rates between patches (can't rescue locally extinct species). One large contiguous habitat supports more species than the same area split into many small pieces. This is the SLOSS debate (Single Large Or Several Small) in conservation biology — theory strongly favors single large.
Q66 — Ne vs N practical implication
A wolf pack has 40 animals total (census population N=40), but only 2 breeding pairs that successfully raise offspring each year. The effective population size (Ne) is approximately:
✅ A. Ne is determined by the number of individuals that ACTUALLY CONTRIBUTE genetically to the next generation. With only 2 breeding pairs (4 breeders), Ne ≈ 4 × (4-1) = small, possibly 8-16 depending on how equal their reproductive success is. The 36 non-breeding wolves contribute nothing to the next generation's gene pool. Ne << N when reproductive success is highly unequal. This is why conservation policy targets Ne, not N.

Q67 — Fishing evolution vs plasticity
Atlantic cod in heavily fished populations now mature at smaller sizes and younger ages compared to unfished populations. A critic argues this is just phenotypic plasticity (fish get smaller because of less food). What evidence would BEST demonstrate this is GENETIC evolution?
✅ C. The definitive test: raise fish from both populations in identical, controlled environments. If the difference persists (fished fish still mature earlier/smaller despite same food), the trait is GENETIC, not a plastic response to food availability. This is the common garden experiment approach. Speed (A) and morphological differences (B) are consistent with either genetic or plastic changes.
Q68 — Ardipithecus significance
Ardipithecus (~4.4–5.8 mya) was found in FOREST, not savannah environments. Why does this matter for understanding bipedalism?
✅ D. The traditional explanation was that bipedalism evolved for open grassland/savannah locomotion. Ardipithecus being a FOREST biped challenges this narrative. Bipedalism apparently predated open savannah habitats. This means the savannah hypothesis (freeing hands for tools in open grassland, or heat regulation in the sun) cannot be the original driver. The HOW and WHY of bipedalism origins is still actively debated.
Q69 — Which is NOT an anatomically modern H. sapiens trait?
Which of the following is NOT a characteristic that distinguishes anatomically modern H. sapiens from archaic hominins like H. erectus?
✅ B. Art and personal ornaments are characteristics of BEHAVIORALLY modern H. sapiens (~70–100 kya), not just anatomically modern ones (~195 kya). Anatomically modern = skeletal features (A, C, D). The gap between anatomical modernity and behavioral modernity is ~100,000+ years. You can be anatomically modern but pre-behaviorally-modern.
Q70 — Fever: adaptation or disease?
Some doctors advocate routinely suppressing fever with antipyretics (fever-reducing drugs). Evolutionary medicine's perspective on this practice would be:
✅ C. This is Nesse's Reason #6: apparent disease may be adaptation. Fever raises body temperature, which: slows bacterial/viral replication (many pathogens are optimized for normal body temp), activates immune cells, and stimulates antibody production. Studies show that suppressing fever can prolong some infections. Evolutionary medicine doesn't say 'never treat fever' — it says evaluate whether the symptom is a defense before automatically suppressing it.
Q71 — Drug resistance as somatic evolution
A patient with HIV is treated with a single antiretroviral drug. After 6 months the drug becomes ineffective. By analogy with antibiotic resistance, what MOST LIKELY happened?
✅ B. HIV generates ~1 billion descendants per day with a high mutation rate (no repair enzymes). A diverse viral population already exists when treatment starts. The drug kills the susceptible majority, but pre-existing resistant variants (already present at low frequency) now have a massive competitive advantage. They expand. Same mechanism as antibiotic resistance. This is why combination therapy (multiple drugs simultaneously) is used — much harder for a single viral strain to have pre-existing resistance to 3+ drugs simultaneously.
Q72 — Mismatch disease identification
Which of the following is BEST explained as an evolutionary mismatch disease (not antagonistic pleiotropy or genetic constraint)?
✅ D. D is the clearest evolutionary mismatch: the evolved trait (efficient fat storage for feast/famine cycles) is now maladaptive in the modern constant-feast environment. A is evolutionary CONSTRAINT (Reason 4 — can't redesign spine from scratch). B is ANTAGONISTIC PLEIOTROPY (Reason 5 — early benefit + late cost). C is not a human disease example at all. Mismatch = evolved for environment X, now living in environment Y.
Q73 — Which is NOT one of Nesse's 6 reasons?
Which of the following is NOT one of Nesse's (2005) six evolutionary reasons why humans remain vulnerable to disease?
✅ C. C is the WRONG one — it contradicts the entire premise of evolutionary medicine. Natural selection optimizes REPRODUCTIVE FITNESS, not health. Health and longevity are only selected for to the extent they increase fitness. Selection can maintain alleles that harm health if they increase reproduction (antagonistic pleiotropy). The other options (A, B, D) are all genuine Nesse reasons. The 6th reason not shown is: selection lags behind environmental change (mismatch).
Q74 — CFTR heterozygote advantage
Cystic fibrosis (CF) alleles persist at ~4% frequency in European populations — higher than expected from mutation alone. The BEST evolutionary explanation is:
✅ B. Same logic as sickle cell heterozygote advantage. CF homozygotes (two copies) have severe lung/digestive disease. But ONE copy may reduce CFTR function just enough that cholera toxin causes less deadly diarrhea — carriers had higher survival during cholera/typhoid epidemics that historically ravaged Europe. The 4% frequency is too high for mutation alone to maintain. Heterozygote advantage (balancing selection) is the most parsimonious explanation.
Q75 — Scenario: All-of-the-above reproductive barriers
Two bird species have: different habitat preferences (marshes vs. forest), mate attraction songs that species only respond to their own, and any hybrid offspring have abnormal development and die as nestlings. How many TYPES of reproductive barriers are present?
✅ D. Multiple reproductive barriers can and often do reinforce each other. Here we have: (1) HABITAT isolation — different microhabitats mean populations rarely encounter each other (prezygotic, pre-mating); (2) BEHAVIORAL isolation — species-specific songs prevent mating even if individuals meet (prezygotic, pre-mating); (3) HYBRID INVIABILITY — the rare hybrids that form die as nestlings (postzygotic). All three act as sequential filters. This is actually the norm for well-established species — they have multiple layers of isolation.
Q76 — Comparative anatomy: coccyx function
The human coccyx (tailbone) anchors muscles used for upright posture, while in other primates it is part of a functional tail. Darwin used this as evidence for common ancestry. This is an example of:
✅ A. The coccyx is a vestigial structure: it retains its homology to primate tails (same bones, same developmental origin, same genetic basis) but is greatly reduced in size and has changed function (from tail movement to posture muscle attachment). It is evidence of common ancestry because the most parsimonious explanation for sharing this structure is descent from a common tailed ancestor. Darwin used it explicitly in Descent of Man (1871).
Q77 — Which evidence type is WEAKEST for phylogenetics?
Which type of similarity is LEAST reliable as evidence for inferring phylogenetic relationships?
✅ B. Analogous structures (homoplasy) are the LEAST reliable for phylogenetics because they arise from convergent evolution — independent evolution in unrelated lineages due to similar selective pressures. Using them as evidence for relationship gives false groupings. Synapomorphies (A), molecular data (C), and developmental homologies (D) all reflect shared ancestry and are reliable. Cladistics specifically excludes analogous traits and focuses only on synapomorphies.
Q78 — Scenario: Gene flow and species status
Two populations of oak trees produce fertile hybrids in the narrow zone where their ranges overlap. However, morphologists classify them as separate species based on leaf shape differences. Which statement BEST represents the situation?
✅ C. Oaks are famous for hybridizing across apparent species boundaries — a classic problem for species concepts. BSC: fertile hybrids = some gene flow = possibly one species. Morphospecies: different enough morphologically = different species. The general lineage concept might consider them different if the bulk of each population is evolving independently despite rare hybridization. This illustrates WHY no single species concept works universally — hybridizing plants regularly break BSC assumptions.
Q79 — Turnover at equilibrium
Two islands have the same equilibrium species number (50 species each). Island A is far from the mainland; Island B is close. At equilibrium, which island has higher species turnover?
✅ B. Key MacArthur-Wilson insight: equilibrium species number is the same, but the RATES can differ. Island B (close) has a HIGH immigration rate. To reach the same equilibrium, it must also have a high extinction rate (more colonizers arriving = more competition = more local extinctions). Higher immigration + higher extinction = HIGHER TURNOVER at the same equilibrium number. Island A has LOW immigration and LOW extinction = same number but lower turnover. Species identity changes faster on B.
Q80 — Final integrative question
A population of bacteria is treated with antibiotic X for one week. Resistance evolves. The bacteria are then treated with a COMBINATION of antibiotics X+Y simultaneously. Why is combination therapy much harder for bacteria to overcome?
✅ D. This is evolutionary logic applied to medicine. Single-drug resistance: the bacterial population already contains some cells with pre-existing resistance to drug X (frequent mutation). Combination therapy: a cell needs SIMULTANEOUS pre-existing resistance to BOTH X and Y. If resistance frequency to X is 10⁻⁶ and to Y is 10⁻⁶, the probability of one cell having both is ~10⁻¹² — vanishingly rare in a normal infection. This is why HIV triple therapy revolutionized AIDS treatment and why TB treatment uses 4+ drugs.

Hybrid STERILITY. Mules are healthy and vigorous but CANNOT REPRODUCE — 63 chromosomes can't pair in meiosis. Inviability = can't survive. Sterility = survives, can't reproduce.
⚠ Island equilibrium = same NUMBER or same SPECIES?
Same NUMBER but species IDENTITY changes (turnover). Some go extinct, new ones colonize. Equilibrium diversity is stable; which species are present is not.
⚠ Do pathogens always evolve LOWER virulence?
NO. Virulence evolves to maximize pathogen FITNESS. Water-borne pathogens (cholera) can maintain HIGH virulence — transmission doesn't need an active host. Evolution has no direction.
⚠ Is FOXP2 "the language gene"?
Oversimplification. FOXP2 = transcription factor for fine orofacial motor control. Functions in mice + birds too. Necessary but NOT sufficient for language. 2 human-specific amino acid changes after chimp split.
⚠ Global diversity formula — include immigration?
NO. Global: D2 = D1 + originations − extinctions ONLY. Immigration + emigration cancel globally. Only matter at local/regional scale. Robbins fill-in-the-blank target.
⚠ Dispersal vs vicariance — who moves?
DISPERSAL = organisms MOVE to new area. VICARIANCE = organisms STAY, BARRIER appears. Hawaiian crickets = dispersal. Panama Isthmus = vicariance. Active vs passive movement.
⚠ Neanderthal brain vs H. sapiens — who has bigger?

⚠ Miller-Urey produced what exactly?
Amino acids (20+ kinds) from inorganic material + simulated lightning. NOT cells. NOT DNA. NOT life. Only proves building blocks can form abiotically. Don't overstate.
⚠ Feathers evolved FOR flight — true or false?
FALSE. Feathers evolved in non-flying theropods for insulation/display. Arms too short to fly early on. Flight evolved LATER when feathers were co-opted = EXAPTATION. Original function ≠ current function.
⚠ Antagonistic pleiotropy vs mutation accumulation?
Antagonistic pleiotropy = one allele with BOTH early benefits AND late costs (testosterone). Mutation accumulation = late mutations with NO early benefit accumulate because selection is weak. Does it HELP early? Yes = antagonistic pleiotropy.
⚠ Synapomorphy vs ancestral character — which defines a clade?
SYNAPOMORPHY (shared DERIVED character) only. Ancestral characters shared by many groups tell you NOTHING about relationships within those groups. Only derived characters evolved in the immediate ancestor of a specific clade are informative.
⚠ Allopolyploidy needs geographic isolation to form new species?
FALSE. Allopolyploidy creates immediate reproductive isolation (different ploidy level) in a SINGLE GENERATION with no geographic separation needed. One of the only true routes to instant sympatric speciation. ~50% of flowering plants have polyploid ancestry.
⚠ What causes hybrid male sterility before female sterility? (Haldane's Rule)
Heterogametic sex (XY males) has recessive incompatibility alleles on the single X EXPOSED (no second X to mask them). Homogametic (XX females) have a backup. In birds, females are ZW (heterogametic) so FEMALES are sterile first. Rule = heterogametic sex goes first.
⚠ Sickle cell in malaria region — which genotype has HIGHEST fitness?
HbA/HbS HETEROZYGOTE has HIGHEST fitness in malaria-endemic regions. HbA/HbA = susceptible to malaria. HbS/HbS = severe anemia. HbA/HbS = protected from malaria WITHOUT anemia. Classic heterozygote advantage (overdominance).
⚠ Where is Denisovan DNA found (vs. Neanderthal DNA)?
Denisovan DNA: primarily Melanesians/Pacific Islanders (~3–6%) + some Asians. Neanderthal DNA: ALL non-African modern humans (~1–4%). Denisovan = Pacific/Asian region. Neanderthal = global non-African. Africans have neither (never encountered either species).
⚠ Great Oxidation Event — who caused it and when?
Cyanobacteria evolving oxygenic photosynthesis (~2.6 bya) → O2 accumulated → Great Oxidation Event ~2.4 bya. Consequence: mass extinction of anaerobes; ozone layer formed; aerobic respiration became possible. NOT from eukaryotes (they came AFTER GOE).
⚠ Ne vs. N — which is always smaller?
Ne (effective population size) is ALWAYS smaller than N (census population). Ne reflects actual breeding individuals contributing equally. Unequal sex ratios, variance in reproductive success, and bottlenecks reduce Ne below N. Conservation targets Ne ≥ 50 (short-term) and Ne ≥ 500 (long-term).
⚠ Cancer = evolution? What does that mean?
YES. Cancer IS somatic evolution: heritable variation (somatic mutations) + natural selection (cells with proliferation advantages replicate more) = adaptation of tumor. Drug resistance in cancer = same mechanism as antibiotic resistance: pre-existing resistant variants selected for when drug kills susceptibles.
⚠ Anatomically modern vs. behaviorally modern H. sapiens — which came first and by how long?
ANATOMICAL modernity (~195 kya) came ~100,000 years BEFORE BEHAVIORAL modernity (~70–100 kya). Modern skeleton first; art, symbols, long-distance trade, ornaments came later. Don't equate looking modern with acting modern.
⚠ Wallace Line: what's on each side?
WEST: Asian fauna (primates, tigers, elephants, Asian deer). EAST: Australasian fauna (marsupials, cockatoos, birds of paradise). A deep ocean channel persisted even during ice ages — discovered by Alfred Russel Wallace 1859. Classic vicariance in biogeography.
⚠ Trophy hunting: which direction does it drive evolution vs. natural selection?
OPPOSITE directions. Natural selection favors LARGE horns/tusks (male competition, mate attraction). Trophy hunting removes LARGE-horned/tusked individuals → selects for SMALL traits. Bighorn sheep: 20% horn size decline over 30 years. Elephants: tusklessness increasing in poached populations.

Answer
5,730 years. Only works on recent organic material <50,000 years old. CANNOT date dinosaurs.
CH3
K-Ar: half-life + what it dates
Answer
1.3 billion years. Works on VOLCANIC ROCKS in the million-year range. Ideal for dating hominin fossils.
CH3
U-Pb: half-life + what it dates
Answer
4.5 billion years. Ancient rocks (hundreds of millions to billions of years).
CH3
Why Kelvin was wrong about Earth's age
Answer
He assumed passive cooling. Didn't know about radioactive decay generating heat continuously inside Earth.
CH3
Prebiotic soup hypothesis
Answer
Before life, Earth's pools/oceans contained organic molecules formed from inorganic chemistry (CH4, H2, NH3, H2O; no O2). Energy = lightning, UV, volcanic heat.
CH3
Miller-Urey experiment (1953)
Answer
Simulated early Earth atmosphere + electric sparks → produced 20+ amino acids in ~1 week. Shows building blocks form abiotically. NOT cells, NOT DNA, NOT life.
CH3
RNA World Hypothesis
Answer
RNA can BOTH store information (like DNA) AND catalyze reactions (like enzymes/ribozymes). Hypothesized as the first self-replicating functional molecule. Bifunctional = original 'living' molecule.
CH3
Biomarkers — what + when
Answer
Molecular evidence of life in ancient rocks (carbon isotope ratios, lipids). Earliest ~3.8 bya. Indicates life but not fossils.
CH3
Stromatolites — what + when
Answer
Layered mounds formed by microbial (cyanobacterial) biofilms trapping sediment. Fossil stromatolites date to 3.45 bya. Still exist today in shallow seas.
CH3
LUCA — what + when
Answer
Last Universal Common Ancestor. ~3.5–3.8 bya. Probably a single-celled prokaryote with DNA, RNA, ribosomes.
CH3
Three domains of life — first appearances
Answer
Bacteria ~3.5 bya (cyanobacteria + first O2). Archaea: biologically produced methane in Australian rocks. Eukaryotes: ~1.8 bya fossil eukaryotes.
CH3
Multicellularity: how many times evolved?
Answer
At LEAST 25 independent origins: plants, animals, fungi, algae, slime molds. NOT a single event. Convergent evolution of multicellularity.
CH3
Great Oxidation Event
Answer
~2.4 bya. Cyanobacteria (evolving ~2.6 bya) pumped O2 into an atmosphere that had essentially none. Killed anaerobes; enabled aerobic metabolism; formed ozone layer.
CH3
Cambrian Explosion — when + what
Answer
542–488 mya. Rapid appearance of most major animal body plans: arthropods, chordates, trilobites. Burgess Shale (505 mya) = snapshot of 93 species.
CH3
Devonian Period highlights
Answer
416–359 mya. 'Age of Fishes.' Dunkleosteus (6m predatory fish). TIKTAALIK (375 mya) transitional fish-tetrapod. Earliest tetrapods ~370 mya. First trees 385 mya.
CH3
Permian extinction: when + severity + cause
Answer
~252 mya. ~96% species loss — LARGEST mass extinction ('Great Dying'). Cause: Siberian Traps volcanism → CO2 spike → global warming + ocean acidification + anoxia.
CH3
K-T extinction: when + severity + cause
Answer
~66 mya. ~76% species loss. Cause: Chicxulub asteroid (~10 km) + elevated CO2. Killed non-avian dinosaurs. Evidence: global iridium layer + Chicxulub crater.
CH3
Geologic period mnemonic
Answer
Old Salamanders Don't Climb Poorly Traveled Jungle Crags = Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian, Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous
CH3
What is a Lagerstätte?
Answer
Exceptionally preserved fossil deposit with soft tissues. Burgess Shale (505 mya, Canada) = classic Lagerstätte. Anoxic conditions prevented decay. Snapshot of entire ecosystem.
CH3
Tiktaalik significance
Answer
375 mya. Transitional fossil between fish and tetrapods. Had: weight-bearing elbows, bending wrist, neck, no bony operculum. Predicted by phylogenetic analysis of where transitional form should be.

Ch 4 • Phy Ch 4 • Phylogenetics

CH4
Phylogenetic tree — 5 components
Answer
Tips = living taxa. Branches = lineages through time. Nodes = speciation events (splits). Root = most recent common ancestor of all taxa shown. Clade = node + ALL descendants.
CH4
Sister taxa definition
Answer
Lineages that diverged from the SAME IMMEDIATE ancestral node — each other's closest relatives. Diverged most recently from each other.
CH4
Synapomorphy definition
Answer
A SHARED DERIVED character that evolved in the immediate ancestor of a specific clade and was inherited by ALL descendants. Only synapomorphies (not ancestral characters) define clades.
CH4
Monophyletic group
Answer
Common ancestor + ALL of its descendants. The only valid taxonomic grouping in cladistics. Also called a 'clade.'
CH4
Paraphyletic group
Answer
Common ancestor + SOME but not ALL descendants. INVALID as a clade. Example: 'Reptiles' without birds (excludes birds which ARE theropod dinosaurs).
CH4
Polyphyletic group
Answer
Group that DOES NOT include the most recent common ancestor of all its members. INVALID. Example: 'warm-blooded animals' (birds + mammals have different origins).
CH4
Are 'Reptiles' monophyletic?
Answer
NO — paraphyletic. Excludes birds which are avian theropod dinosaurs. Crocodilians are more closely related to birds than to lizards. To be monophyletic must include birds.
CH4
Principle of parsimony in phylogenetics
Answer
Choose the tree requiring the FEWEST evolutionary changes (steps) as the working hypothesis. More steps = more homoplasy assumed = less parsimonious.
CH4
Homoplasy — definition + 2 types
Answer
Similarity NOT due to shared ancestry. Types: (1) Convergent evolution — same trait evolves independently in separate lineages via different mechanisms. (2) Evolutionary reversal — derived character reverts to ancestral state.
CH4
Convergent vs. parallel evolution
Answer
Convergent = different genetic/developmental mechanisms, more distantly related (e.g., camera eye in vertebrates vs. octopus). Parallel = SAME genetic mechanism in more closely related lineages (e.g., limblessness via HOX changes in snake lineages).
CH4
Homology vs. homoplasy
Answer
Homology = similarity due to SHARED ANCESTRY. Homoplasy = similarity NOT due to shared ancestry (independent evolution). Human arm + whale flipper = homology. Dolphin + shark streamlined body = homoplasy.
CH4
Exaptation definition + example
Answer
Trait evolved for function A, later co-opted for function B. Feathers: evolved in non-flying theropods for insulation/display → LATER co-opted for flight in birds. Swim bladder: ancestral lung co-opted for buoyancy.
CH4
Did feathers evolve for flight?
Answer
NO. Feathers evolved in non-flying theropod dinosaurs (Velociraptor had feather quill nodes, arms too short to fly). Original functions: insulation, display, species recognition, egg incubation. Flight co-opted feathers LATER = exaptation.
CH4
Cladistics definition
Answer
Phylogenetic method that builds trees using ONLY shared derived characters (synapomorphies). Developed by Willi Hennig. Forms basis of modern phylogenetic taxonomy.
CH4
What does phylogeography study?
Answer
Study of historical processes (vicariance and dispersal) responsible for the current geographic distributions of genealogical lineages. Ties phylogenetic relationships to geography.
CH4
What are the components of a clade?
Answer
A node (the most recent common ancestor) + ALL of its descendants. Must be complete — leaving out any descendant makes it paraphyletic.

Ch 13 • Sp Ch 13 • Speciation

CH13
Biological Species Concept (BSC) — definition + 3 failures
Answer
Groups of interbreeding natural populations, reproductively isolated from others (Mayr). FAILS for: (1) fossils (can't test gene flow), (2) asexual species (bacteria, archaea), (3) hybridizing species (fertile hybrids exist).
CH13
Morphospecies Concept — definition + failure
Answer
Species defined by measurable morphological differences. Useful for fossils and field ID. FAILS for: cryptic species (look identical but genetically distinct), phenotypically plastic species (environment changes morphology).
CH13
Phylogenetic Species Concept — definition + failure
Answer
Smallest monophyletic group with defining synapomorphies. Works for any reproducing organism. FAILS: requires established phylogeny; often over-splits taxa into very fine categories.
CH13
General Lineage Species Concept
Answer
Metapopulations exchanging alleles frequently enough to be the same evolutionary lineage. Unifies other concepts. Hard to quantify allele exchange threshold in practice.
CH13
3 steps of speciation
Answer
1. Creation of barrier to gene flow. 2. Genetic and ecological divergence. 3. Reproductive isolation evolves. (+4: Reinforcement if secondary contact occurs.)
CH13
Habitat isolation (prezygotic, pre-mating)
Answer
Same geographic area but different microhabitats → populations don't encounter each other. Example: Hawthorn vs. apple maggot flies (same region, different host plants).
CH13
Temporal isolation (prezygotic, pre-mating)
Answer
Different breeding/flowering/mating times prevent encounters. Example: hummingbird-pollinated plants with staggered flowering phenology (Stiles 1977).
CH13
Gametic incompatibility (prezygotic, post-mating, pre-fert)
Answer
Sperm/pollen from one species fails to penetrate egg of another due to species-specific surface proteins. Mating occurred but fertilization blocked.
CH13
Hybrid inviability (postzygotic)
Answer
Hybrid zygote forms but embryo fails to develop normally or offspring die before reproductive age. Incompatible allele interactions cause developmental failure.
CH13
Hybrid sterility (postzygotic) — mule example
Answer
Hybrids survive but CANNOT REPRODUCE. Mule: horse (64 chr) × donkey (62 chr) = mule (63 chr). Chromosomes can't pair in meiosis. Healthy but sterile. Horse and donkey genes cannot flow into each other's populations.
CH13
Reinforcement — definition
Answer
Natural selection STRENGTHENING prezygotic barriers when populations that meet in secondary contact produce low-fitness hybrids. Individuals who avoid mating with the other species have higher fitness (don't waste gametes on failed offspring). Prezygotic isolation INCREASES.
CH13
Allopatric speciation — steps
Answer
1. Geographic barrier forms (vicariance) OR one population disperses (dispersal). 2. Gene flow stops. 3. Populations diverge genetically and ecologically. 4. Reproductive isolation evolves. 5. Reinforcement if secondary contact.
CH13
Sympatric speciation — definition + example
Answer
New species evolves WITHIN the same geographic range as ancestor, without geographic isolation. Example: Rhagoletis apple maggot fly — shifted from hawthorn to apple trees (~140 ya) within same range. Host shift = mating isolation.
CH13
Vicariance vs. dispersal
Answer
Vicariance = organisms STAY PUT, BARRIER appears (e.g., Panama Isthmus splits Atlantic/Pacific populations). Dispersal = organisms MOVE to new area (e.g., Hawaiian cricket colonizing new island). Both produce allopatric speciation.
CH13
Allopolyploidy = instant speciation
Answer
Hybridization between two species + whole-genome duplication = new allopolyploid organism. Immediately reproductively isolated from both parents (different ploidy → meiotic failure when crossed back). NO geographic isolation needed. One generation.
CH13
Allopolyploidy examples
Answer
Tragopogon (goatsbeard): two allopolyploid species formed in Pacific Northwest within ~80 years — observed in real time. Bread wheat: hexaploid (6n=42) from three ancestral grass species via two sequential allopolyploidy events.
CH13
Haldane's Rule
Answer
When one sex of hybrid offspring is inviable or sterile, it is more often the HETEROGAMETIC sex (XY males in mammals; ZW females in birds). Reason: recessive incompatibility alleles on X are EXPOSED in hemizygous XY males (no backup X). XX females have backup copy.
CH13
Parapatric speciation
Answer
Populations have PARTIAL geographic separation; meet in a narrow contact zone where some gene flow occurs. Selection gradients stronger than gene flow can drive divergence. Intermediate between allopatric and sympatric.
CH13
Hawaiian Laupala crickets — what they demonstrate
Answer
37 endemic cricket species on Hawaii. Colonized from older to younger volcanic islands (dispersal). Phylogeny mirrors island age sequence — confirms dispersal model. Volcanic islands formed by crust moving west over a hotspot.
CH13
What is ecological speciation?
Answer
Speciation driven by natural selection due to adaptation to different ecological environments/resources — even without geographic isolation. Rhagoletis is the textbook example (different host plant = different selective environment = mating isolation).

Ch 14 • Bi Ch 14 • Biogeography

CH14
Biogeography — definition
Answer
Study of spatial and temporal distribution of biodiversity. Integrates biology, geology, geography, paleontology. Initiated by Darwin and Wallace.
CH14
Standing diversity formula (global)
Answer
D₂ = D₁ + originations − extinctions. At global scale ONLY: immigration and emigration cancel out and are excluded.
CH14
λ (lambda) vs μ (mu)
Answer
λ = rate of species origination (species/million years). μ = rate of species extinction (species/million years). If λ > μ: diversity increases. If μ > λ: decreases. If ≈ equal: stable with turnover.
CH14
Turnover rate definition
Answer
Total originations + extinctions over a time period. A clade can have stable standing diversity BUT high turnover (species constantly replacing each other). Dinosaurs had very high turnover rates.
CH14
Adaptive radiation — definition + 3 triggers
Answer
Rapid diversification of one lineage into many ecological roles (λ >> μ sustained). Triggers: (1) colonization of new habitat with no competitors, (2) KEY INNOVATION opens new resource space, (3) mass extinction removes competitors.
CH14
Key innovation — definition + example
Answer
Specific adaptation that grants access to previously unavailable habitats/resources by removing a constraint. Example: Eleutherodactylus frogs — direct egg development on land skips aquatic larval competition → >400 species radiated.
CH14
African cichlids adaptive radiation
Answer
~500 species in Lake Victoria (~15,000 years!). Fastest known vertebrate adaptive radiation. Colonization of Great Lakes + no competitors + jaw diversity as key innovation. Shows speciation can be extremely rapid.
CH14
Cenozoic mammal radiation trigger
Answer
K-T extinction (66 mya) killed non-avian dinosaurs that had monopolized ecological niches for ~160 million years. Released mammals into vacant adaptive space. Within ~10 million years all major mammalian orders appeared.
CH14
The Big 5 mass extinctions in order
Answer
(1) End-Ordovician 444 mya ~86%. (2) Late Devonian 375-359 mya ~75%. (3) End-Permian 252 mya ~96% LARGEST. (4) End-Triassic 202 mya ~80%. (5) K-T 66 mya ~76% most famous.
CH14
Background extinction rate
Answer
The normal, baseline rate of extinction for a taxon. The standard against which 'mass extinction' departures are measured. Mass extinction = statistically significant departure from this baseline.
CH14
6th mass extinction
Answer
Currently happening. Caused by HUMANS. Extinction rates 100–1000× background rate. Causes: habitat destruction, invasive species, pollution, climate change, overexploitation (HIPCO). If current rates continue, could rival Big 5 within centuries.
CH14
Dispersal in biogeography
Answer
Movement of organisms from one geographic location to another. Active mechanism. Splits species distribution. Example: Hawaiian crickets hopping between volcanic islands. Produces allopatric speciation.
CH14
Vicariance in biogeography
Answer
Formation of geographic barriers splitting existing range. Passive mechanism — organisms stay put, geology moves. Example: Isthmus of Panama rising split Atlantic/Pacific shrimp. Continent drift produces vicariance.
CH14
Marsupial biogeography summary
Answer
Oldest fossils: China 150 mya → N. America ~120 mya → S. America → crossed Antarctica → Australia ~55 mya. Australia drifted into isolation for >40 million years. No placentals until bats/rats ~15 mya. Result: convergent evolution of ecological counterparts.
CH14
Wallace Line — what + significance
Answer
Sharp biogeographic boundary through Malay Archipelago (Bali/Lombok; Borneo/Sulawesi). WEST: Asian fauna (primates, tigers). EAST: Australasian fauna (marsupials, cockatoos). Deep ocean channel persisted even during ice ages — organisms couldn't cross. Discovered by Alfred Russel Wallace 1859.
CH14
Island Biogeography theory (MacArthur & Wilson 1967)
Answer
Island species richness = dynamic equilibrium between immigration (adds species) and extinction (removes species). Equilibrium number is STABLE; species COMPOSITION turns over. Large + near = more species. Small + far = fewer species.
CH14
Island biogeography equilibrium — important distinction
Answer
At equilibrium: species NUMBER is stable. But species COMPOSITION keeps changing (turnover). Same number ≠ same species. New species colonize as others go extinct.
CH14
Conservation genetics: 50/500 rule
Answer
Ne ≥ 50 short-term (prevents rapid inbreeding, keeps inbreeding <1%/generation). Ne ≥ 500 long-term (maintains adaptive genetic variation). Ne (effective population size) < N (census population) always.
CH14
Inbreeding depression — mechanism
Answer
Inbreeding increases homozygosity → (1) deleterious recessives normally masked in heterozygotes become expressed, (2) loss of heterozygote advantage (e.g., MHC immune genes). Cheetahs: so inbred that skin grafts are accepted between unrelated individuals.
CH14
Area phylogeny — what is it?
Answer
A phylogenetic comparison of land masses or locations. When land masses break apart, lineages become separated. Phylogeny of lineages should reflect the history of the land masses (vicariance). Used to approximate when lineages separated.
CH14
Phylogeography — definition
Answer
Study of historical processes (vicariance & dispersal) responsible for current geographic distributions. Uses molecular phylogeny + geographic distribution to reconstruct history of where populations came from.

Ch 8 • Hum Ch 8 • Humans & Selection

CH8
Humans as selective force — key examples
Answer
Trophy hunting (smaller horns/tusks), antibiotic use (resistance), pesticide use (resistance), size-selective fishing (smaller body, earlier maturation), habitat fragmentation (isolation), selective breeding (crops, livestock, dogs).
CH8
Trophy hunting evolution: bighorn sheep
Answer
30 years of trophy hunting → ~20% decline in average horn size. Large-horned males killed before full reproduction → small-horn alleles increase. Directional selection OPPOSING natural selection (which favors large horns for male competition).
CH8
Tusklessness in African elephants
Answer
Mozambique: intense ivory poaching during civil war 1977–1992. Before: ~18% females tuskless. After: ~33%. Tusklessness is heritable. Strong directional selection removed tusked females. Human-imposed selection on heritable trait.
CH8
Antibiotic resistance: how it develops
Answer
RANDOM pre-existing mutations create some resistant bacteria. Antibiotics KILL susceptibles. Resistant variants survive and multiply. NOT directed mutation. Stopping antibiotics early leaves resistant survivors to flourish without competition.
CH8
4 mechanisms of antibiotic resistance
Answer
(1) Target modification (mutation alters drug binding site). (2) Enzymatic degradation (beta-lactamases destroy penicillin). (3) Efflux pumps (expel antibiotic from cell). (4) Horizontal gene transfer — resistance plasmids passed between species.
CH8
Atlantic cod fishing evolution
Answer
Decades of size-selective fishing (nets catch large fish, small escape) → heritable decline in body size and earlier sexual maturation. Genuine genetic evolution (not phenotypic plasticity). Alleles for small size + early maturation increased in frequency.
CH8
Can antibiotics cause mutations?
Answer
NO. Mutations are RANDOM and PRE-EXISTING. Antibiotics REVEAL and SELECT FOR pre-existing resistant variants. Saying 'bacteria evolved resistance BECAUSE OF antibiotics' implies directed mutation = WRONG.
CH8
What makes fishing-driven evolution real evolution (not plasticity)?
Answer
The changes are HERITABLE — cod in different environments still show the evolved smaller size and earlier maturation. The allele frequencies have genuinely changed. Not just phenotypic response to food scarcity.
CH8
Habitat fragmentation + genetics
Answer
Fragments behave like islands (island biogeography applies). Small fragments = smaller populations = more genetic drift = inbreeding depression = loss of adaptive variation. Fragmentation reduces Ne, which compounds all genetic problems.
CH8
Selective breeding vs. natural selection
Answer
Both are evolution. Selective breeding = ARTIFICIAL selection by humans for desired traits. Natural selection = differential reproduction due to environmental pressures. Dog breeds from wolves via artificial selection = remarkably fast phenotypic evolution.

Ch 17 • Hu Ch 17 • Human Evolution

CH17
Why Linnaeus classified humans as primates
Answer
Forward-facing eyes, gripping hands with nails, large relative brain, complex social behavior — anatomical similarities. Darwin (1871, Descent of Man) explained WHY: common ancestry with apes.
CH17
Molecular evidence of human-chimp kinship
Answer
~98.5% DNA similarity. Human chromosome 2 = fusion of chimp 2A + 2B (telomeric sequences in middle prove this). Same blood types (ABO). Coccyx = vestigial tail. Shared embryonic homologies.
CH17
Did humans evolve FROM chimpanzees?
Answer
NO. Humans and chimps share a COMMON ANCESTOR ~7 mya. We are SISTER LINEAGES. Neither is the ancestor of the other. Both have been evolving since the split. Correct: 'diverged from a common ancestor.'
CH17
Human chromosome 2 evidence
Answer
Contains telomeric sequences in its MIDDLE (normally only at chromosome ends). Signature of END-TO-END FUSION of two ancestral chromosomes. Chimp has 2A + 2B separate; humans fused them into one. Powerful molecular evidence of common descent.
CH17
Sahelanthropus tchadensis — key features
Answer
~7 mya, Chad. Earliest potential hominin. Small canines. Foramen magnum position suggests upright posture. Brain ~320–380cc. Debated status but currently earliest candidate.
CH17
Australopithecus — key facts
Answer
4–1.2 mya. Obligate bipeds but ape-sized brain (~450cc). Lucy (Au. afarensis, 3.2 mya, Ethiopia). Laetoli footprints (3.5 mya, Tanzania) = direct bipedal evidence. Used simple tools.
CH17
Homo erectus — key facts
Answer
1.9–0.07 mya. FIRST hominin to leave Africa (~1.9 mya; fossils in Java and China). Brain ~900–1100cc. Acheulean hand axes. First regular use of fire. Long legs + short arms = modern body proportions.
CH17
Homo neanderthalensis — key facts
Answer
400–40 kya. Europe + W. Asia. Brain ~1500cc (LARGER than average H. sapiens ~1350cc). Buried dead. Mousterian tools. Interbred with H. sapiens. Non-Africans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA. Extinct ~40 kya.
CH17
Homo sapiens — origin + timeline
Answer
Anatomically modern: ~195 kya, East Africa (Omo Kibish, Ethiopia) / ~315 kya revised (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco). Behaviorally modern (art, symbols, long-distance trade): ~70–100 kya. Spread globally ~70–50 kya.
CH17
Bipedalism: when did it evolve?
Answer
~6–7 mya. FIRST major hominin innovation. Preceded brain expansion by millions of years. Australopithecus walked fully upright with ~450cc brain. Bipedalism first → big brain later (not vice versa).
CH17
4 anatomical markers of bipedalism
Answer
(1) Foramen magnum: bottom center in humans (upright), rear in apes. (2) Wide bowl-shaped pelvis vs. elongated ape pelvis. (3) Valgus femur angle (inward toward knee) vs. vertical ape femur. (4) Arched foot + aligned big toe (Laetoli footprints).
CH17
Why did bipedalism evolve? (2 hypotheses)
Answer
(1) Thermoregulation: upright posture reduces direct sun exposure; tall body dissipates heat in hot savannah. (2) Freeing hands: frees forelimbs for carrying food, tools, and infants. Both likely contributed.
CH17
Bipedalism costs (maladaptations)
Answer
~80% of humans have lower back pain (spine retrofitted from horizontal to vertical without redesign). Difficult childbirth (narrow bipedal pelvis + large infant head = obstetric dilemma). Knee injuries from increased joint stress.
CH17
Hominin tool traditions in order
Answer
Oldowan (~2.6 mya, H. habilis, simple flaked stones) → Acheulean (1.7 mya, H. erectus, symmetric hand axes) → Mousterian (300–30 kya, Neanderthals, prepared core) → Upper Paleolithic (~45 kya, modern H. sapiens, blades + art).
CH17
FOXP2 — what it is + why it matters
Answer
Transcription factor regulating orofacial FINE MOTOR CONTROL. Mutations cause severe speech/language disorders. Human version has 2 unique amino acid changes after chimp split. Neanderthals have the human version (mutations predated divergence ~600 kya). NOT 'the language gene.'
CH17
Out of Africa hypothesis
Answer
Anatomically modern H. sapiens evolved in Africa (~195 kya) and expanded globally ~70–50 kya, largely replacing other hominins but with some interbreeding. Supported by: lower genetic diversity outside Africa (bottleneck), geographic pattern of Neanderthal DNA.
CH17
Neanderthal DNA in modern humans — pattern
Answer
Non-African modern humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA. Sub-Saharan Africans carry little to none. Geographic restriction = PROOF of post-migration interbreeding (not just shared ancestry). If shared ancestry alone, all humans would have equal amounts.
CH17
Denisovans — who + where + legacy
Answer
Archaic hominin known mainly from DNA extracted from finger bone in Denisova Cave, Siberia. Diverged from Neanderthals ~400 kya. Interbred with H. sapiens. MELANESIANS carry ~3–6% Denisovan DNA. EPAS1 gene (high-altitude adaptation in Tibetans) came from Denisovans via adaptive introgression.
CH17
Anatomically vs. behaviorally modern H. sapiens
Answer
Anatomically modern = modern skeleton (~195 kya or earlier). Behaviorally modern = symbolic thought, art, personal ornaments, long-distance trade (~70–100 kya). GAP of ~100,000–200,000 years. Looked modern before acting modern.
CH17
Adaptive introgression — what is it?
Answer
Gene flow from an archaic/related population introduces an allele that is then POSITIVELY SELECTED in the recipient population. Example: Denisovan EPAS1 allele (high-altitude adaptation) introgressed into Tibetan ancestors and was then strongly selected.
CH17
MHC and human mate choice
Answer
Major Histocompatibility Complex = immune gene region. Studies show humans prefer mates with DIFFERENT MHC types (detected via body odor — 'sweaty T-shirt' experiments). Benefit: offspring with diverse MHC recognize more pathogens. Genetic mate choice driven by immune diversity.
CH17
Cooking hypothesis (Wrangham)
Answer
Cooking food with fire dramatically increases caloric yield from same raw ingredients → provided energy to support larger, metabolically expensive brains. May have made brain expansion energetically feasible in H. erectus lineage.

Ch 18 • Ev Ch 18 • Evolutionary Medicine

CH18
Evolutionary medicine — definition
Answer
Applying evolutionary biology to understand why humans remain vulnerable to disease. Natural selection maximizes REPRODUCTIVE FITNESS, not health or longevity. Our bodies are modified ancestral structures.
CH18
Nesse's 6 reasons we get sick
Answer
(1) Pathogens evolve faster. (2) Selection lags behind environment (mismatch). (3) Trade-offs. (4) Evolutionary history constrains solutions. (5) Antagonistic pleiotropy. (6) Apparent disease is actually adaptation.
CH18
Reason 1: Pathogens evolve faster
Answer
Short generation times + high mutation rates = pathogens outpace host defenses. HIV: ~1 billion descendants/day; no repair enzymes → high mutation rate. New flu strains emerge each season. We can't keep up.
CH18
Reason 2: Evolutionary mismatch
Answer
Modern environment changed faster than evolution can track. Our bodies evolved for ancestral conditions (feast/famine, high activity, high pathogen load). Modern perpetual feast → obesity, diabetes. Sedentary lifestyle → cardiovascular disease.
CH18
Reason 3: Trade-offs
Answer
Selection can't solve some problems without creating others. Sickle cell: protects against malaria (HbA/HbS heterozygotes) but causes disease in HbS/HbS homozygotes. Narrow bipedal pelvis + large infant head = difficult childbirth.
CH18
Reason 4: Evolutionary constraints
Answer
Selection can only TINKER with existing structures — can't redesign from scratch. Human spine: 300 million years as horizontal quadruped → ~7 million years retrofitted for bipedalism → back pain in ~80% of humans. Eye blind spot from inverted retina.
CH18
Reason 5: Antagonistic pleiotropy
Answer
Single allele with BOTH early reproductive benefits AND late-life costs. Selection maintains it because early benefits outweigh late costs (most ancestors died before costs manifested). Testosterone: early mating success → late prostate cancer/heart disease.
CH18
Reason 6: Apparent disease = adaptation
Answer
Some symptoms ARE defenses. Fever: raises body temperature to kill pathogens (blocking fever may worsen outcomes). Morning sickness: peaks at 6–18 weeks (organogenesis) — aversion to contaminated foods protects fetus. Women with morning sickness have lower miscarriage rates.
CH18
Antagonistic pleiotropy vs. mutation accumulation
Answer
AP = allele with EARLY BENEFITS + late costs (e.g., testosterone, p53). Maintained by selection for early benefits. MutAccum = late deleterious mutations with NO early benefit — accumulate because selection is too weak late in life to remove them.
CH18
p53 — antagonistic pleiotropy example
Answer
p53 = 'guardian of the genome.' Triggers cell cycle arrest/apoptosis when DNA damage detected. Early benefit: prevents cancer during reproductive years. Late cost: excessive p53 activity depletes stem cells → accelerated aging. Mouse studies confirm: more p53 = less cancer BUT faster aging.
CH18
Virulence — definition + trade-off
Answer
Degree of harm a pathogen causes its host. Pathogen fitness = within-host replication × transmission to new hosts. HIGH virulence → more replication but kills host faster → less total transmission. INTERMEDIATE virulence is usually optimal.
CH18
Why cholera can maintain high virulence
Answer
Cholera spreads via CONTAMINATED WATER — transmission does NOT require an active, mobile host. Bedridden or dead hosts still contaminate water supplies. High virulence (explosive diarrhea) actually INCREASES pathogen output. Different transmission mode = different optimal virulence.
CH18
Antibiotic resistance evolution (evolutionary medicine angle)
Answer
Bacteria evolve resistance by same mechanism as any evolution: random mutations exist → antibiotics kill susceptibles → resistant variants selected for. Stopping antibiotics early = strongest selection for resistance (killing easy targets, leaving resistant ones to multiply).
CH18
Cancer as somatic evolution
Answer
Cancer IS evolution occurring within a single body. Somatic mutations create variant cell lineages → natural selection favors cells with faster proliferation, immune evasion, metastasis → tumor 'adapts.' Chemo resistance = same mechanism as antibiotic resistance: pre-existing resistant cells selected for.
CH18
Sickle cell: genotype fitness ranking in malaria-endemic Africa
Answer
HbA/HbS heterozygote > HbA/HbA > HbS/HbS. Heterozygote has HIGHEST fitness because ~25% protection against severe malaria. Classic heterozygote advantage (overdominance/balancing selection). HbS maintained at ~10–20% frequency.
CH18
Sickle cell: fitness ranking in NON-malarial regions
Answer
HbA/HbA > HbA/HbS > HbS/HbS. No malaria = no benefit for carrying HbS. Heterozygote no better than normal homozygote. Homozygote still has severe anemia. SAME genotype, DIFFERENT fitness in different environments.
CH18
Hygiene hypothesis
Answer
Reduced exposure to parasites and pathogens in clean environments creates a mismatch. Immune system evolved calibrated to high pathogen/parasite load. Without those targets, it may attack harmless antigens (ALLERGIES) or self-antigens (AUTOIMMUNITY). Farm children have significantly LOWER allergy rates.
CH18
Conserved gene networks in evolutionary medicine
Answer
Humans and yeast share common ancestor >1 billion years ago. Many gene networks preserved. Marcotte et al.: yeast cell-wall repair genes homologous to human angiogenesis genes → identified 8 new human blood vessel growth genes = potential cancer drug targets. Ancient conservation enables medicine.
CH18
Myxoma virus — evolutionary case study
Answer
Introduced to Australia 1950 to kill invasive rabbits. Initially killed 99.8%. Within years: virus evolved LOWER virulence (rabbits survived longer = more mosquito feeding opportunities = more transmission). Rabbits evolved resistance. Virulence trade-off + coevolution in real time.
CH18
Mismatch disease examples
Answer
Obesity (fat storage evolved for feast/famine, now perpetual feast). CVD (sedentary vs. hunter-gatherer activity level). Autoimmunity/allergies (clean environments vs. evolved high-parasite-load immune calibration). Back pain (horizontal spine retrofitted for bipedalism). Increased breast/ovarian cancer (fewer pregnancies → more lifetime ovulatory cycles → more estrogen).
CH18
Evolutionary mismatch: back pain
Answer
Human spine evolved as horizontal load-bearing column (~300 million years). Retrofitted for vertical bipedalism only ~7 million years ago. S-curve compensates but creates vulnerabilities: herniated discs, lumbar compression, spondylolisthesis. ~80% of humans experience significant back pain. Classic evolutionary constraint (Reason 4).

TRAP
TRAP: C-14 dating ancient fossils
Answer
C-14 CANNOT date fossils >~50,000 years old. Half-life = 5,730 yrs — after ~250,000 yrs all C-14 is gone. NEVER use C-14 for dinosaurs, ancient hominins, or geological formations. Use K-Ar or U-Pb.
TRAP
TRAP: 'Reptiles' taxonomic status
Answer
Traditional 'Reptiles' (turtles, snakes, lizards, crocodilians) is PARAPHYLETIC — excludes birds. Birds ARE avian theropod dinosaurs. Crocodilians are more closely related to birds than to lizards. Must include birds to be monophyletic.
TRAP
TRAP: Largest vs. most famous extinction
Answer
End-PERMIAN = LARGEST (~96% loss, 'Great Dying'). K-T = MOST FAMOUS (~76% loss, killed non-avian dinosaurs). These are DIFFERENT events. 'Most severe?' = Permian. 'Killed dinosaurs?' = K-T asteroid.
TRAP
TRAP: Antibiotics cause mutations
Answer
NO. Mutations are RANDOM and PRE-EXISTING. Antibiotics KILL susceptibles and SELECT FOR pre-existing variants. No directed mutation. Saying 'evolved resistance BECAUSE OF antibiotics' = implies directed mutation = WRONG.
TRAP
TRAP: Humans evolved from chimps
Answer
NO. Humans and chimps share a COMMON ANCESTOR ~7 mya. We are SISTER LINEAGES. Neither is ancestor of the other. Both evolved since the split. Correct: 'diverged from common ancestor.'
TRAP
TRAP: Bipedalism before or after large brains?
Answer
BIPEDALISM FIRST (~6–7 mya). Brain expansion came LATER. Australopithecus walked fully upright with ape-sized brain (~450cc). Don't say brain drove bipedalism. Sequence: bipedalism → tools → brain expansion.
TRAP
TRAP: BSC fails in these 3 scenarios
Answer
(1) Fossils (can't test gene flow). (2) Asexual species (bacteria, archaea). (3) Hybridizing species (fertile hybrids exist). Know all 3 for Robbins' scenario questions.
TRAP
TRAP: Mule = which postzygotic barrier?
Answer
Hybrid STERILITY (postzygotic). NOT hybrid inviability. Mules are HEALTHY and vigorous — they survive. They CANNOT REPRODUCE because 63 chromosomes can't pair in meiosis. Inviability = can't survive. Sterility = survives, can't reproduce.
TRAP
TRAP: Island biogeography equilibrium
Answer
At equilibrium: species NUMBER is stable. Species IDENTITY (composition) keeps CHANGING (turnover). Equilibrium ≠ same species stay forever. New species colonize as others go extinct. Turnover is an active prediction.
TRAP
TRAP: Virulence always evolves lower?
Answer
NO. Virulence evolves to maximize pathogen FITNESS, not to be 'nice.' Water-borne pathogens (cholera) can maintain HIGH virulence — transmission doesn't require active host. Evolution has no direction, no foresight.
TRAP
TRAP: FOXP2 = 'the language gene'?
Answer
Oversimplification. FOXP2 = transcription factor for fine orofacial MOTOR CONTROL. Also functions in mice (ultrasonic calls) and songbirds (vocal learning). Necessary but NOT sufficient for language. 2 human-specific amino acid changes after chimp split.
TRAP
TRAP: Global diversity formula includes immigration?
Answer
NO. Global scale: D₂ = D₁ + originations − extinctions ONLY. Immigration and emigration cancel globally. They only matter at LOCAL/REGIONAL scale. Robbins tests this as fill-in-the-blank.
TRAP
TRAP: Dispersal vs. vicariance — who moves?
Answer
DISPERSAL = organisms MOVE to new area (active). VICARIANCE = organisms STAY PUT, BARRIER appears (passive). Hawaiian crickets = dispersal. Panama Isthmus separating shrimp = vicariance. The distinction is what moves: organism or geology.
TRAP
TRAP: Neanderthal brain size
Answer
Neanderthals had EQUAL TO or LARGER brains than modern humans (~1500cc vs ~1350cc average). Brain size ≠ cognitive superiority ≠ survival advantage. They went extinct despite bigger brains. Went extinct ~40 kya — likely due to competition, climate, assimilation.
TRAP
TRAP: Miller-Urey produced what?
Answer
Amino acids (20+ kinds) from inorganic material + simulated lightning. NOT cells. NOT DNA. NOT life. Only proves building blocks can form abiotically. Do NOT say it proved how life originated or that it created life.
TRAP
TRAP: Feathers evolved FOR flight?
Answer
FALSE. Feathers evolved in NON-FLYING theropods for insulation/display (Velociraptor had feather quill nodes, arms too short to fly). Flight co-opted feathers LATER = EXAPTATION. Original function ≠ current function.
TRAP
TRAP: Antagonistic pleiotropy vs. mutation accumulation
Answer
AP = allele with BOTH early benefits AND late costs (testosterone). Maintained by selection for early benefits. MutAccum = late deleterious mutations with NO early benefit. Key test: does the allele HELP you reproduce early? Yes = AP. No = MutAccum.
TRAP
TRAP: Synapomorphy vs. ancestral character
Answer
SYNAPOMORPHY (SHARED DERIVED character) defines clades. Ancestral (plesiomorphic) characters shared by many groups are NOT informative for relationships within those groups. 'Has a backbone' can't diagnose subgroups of vertebrates — ALL vertebrates have it.
TRAP
TRAP: Allopolyploidy needs geographic isolation?
Answer
NO. Allopolyploidy creates reproductive isolation IN ONE GENERATION with NO geographic separation. One of the only truly sympatric speciation routes. ~50% of flowering plants have polyploid ancestry. Instant speciation.
TRAP
TRAP: Sickle cell heterozygote fitness in malaria
Answer
HbA/HbS heterozygote has HIGHEST fitness in malaria-endemic regions (not HbA/HbA). Fitness ranking: HbA/HbS > HbA/HbA > HbS/HbS. Classic heterozygote advantage (overdominance). In non-malarial regions: HbA/HbA > HbA/HbS > HbS/HbS.
TRAP
TRAP: Cancer is evolution
Answer
YES. Cancer IS somatic evolution: heritable variation (somatic mutations) + selection (cells with proliferation advantage replicate more) = adaptation of tumor. Drug resistance in cancer = same mechanism as antibiotic resistance. Pre-existing resistant cells selected for.
TRAP
TRAP: Anatomically vs. behaviorally modern
Answer
Anatomically modern H. sapiens (~195 kya) appeared ~100,000–200,000 years BEFORE behaviorally modern humans (~70–100 kya). Modern skeleton came first; art, symbols, long-distance trade came later. Don't equate looking modern with acting modern.
TRAP
TRAP: Denisovan vs. Neanderthal DNA distribution
Answer
Neanderthal DNA: in ALL non-African modern humans (~1–4%). Denisovan DNA: primarily MELANESIANS/Pacific Islanders (~3–6%) + some mainland Asians. Sub-Saharan Africans have neither. Different regions of interbreeding.
