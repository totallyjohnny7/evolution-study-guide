3.1 — Age of the Earth & Radiometric Dating

What it is
Earth is ~4.568 billion years old. Radiometric dating: measure the ratio of radioactive parent isotope to daughter isotope. Each isotope decays at a fixed rate (half-life) unaffected by temperature, pressure, or chemistry — that invariance makes it a reliable clock.
Why it matters
Without dates, no timeline. Lord Kelvin said ~20 million years (wrong — ignored radioactive heat). Darwin said billions from sediment rates (roughly right). Modern physics confirmed 4.568 bya.
How it works
  1. Radioactive parent decays into daughter at a constant rate
  2. Measure current parent:daughter ratio in the rock
  3. Apply known half-life to calculate when rock solidified (reset to 100% parent / 0% daughter)
  4. Multiple isotope systems can cross-check the same sample
Parent → DaughterHalf-LifeBest For
Carbon-14 → Nitrogen-145,730 yrsRecent organic material (<50,000 yrs)
Potassium-40 → Argon-401.3 billion yrsVolcanic rocks; hominin fossils (million-year range)
Uranium-238 → Lead-2064.5 billion yrsAncient rocks (hundreds of millions to billions of years)
Rubidium-87 → Strontium-8747 billion yrsVery ancient formations; solar system age
⚠ Trap — C-14 and Dinosaurs

C-14 CANNOT date dinosaur fossils (~66+ mya). After ~250,000 years all C-14 has decayed. Use U-Pb or K-Ar. Robbins loves this.

The thing to remember is:

Short half-life = use for recent samples. Long half-life = use for ancient samples. Half-life is FIXED, unaffected by environment.

Gold = parent isotope remaining. Blue dashed = daughter isotope accumulating. At each half-life, parent drops by half.
Mnemonic

“Cool Kids Usually Rock” = C-14 (recent), K-Ar (medium), U-Pb (old), Rb-Sr (ancient).

3.2 — Origin of Life (Abiogenesis)

What it is
Abiogenesis = life from non-living chemistry. The prebiotic soup hypothesis: early Earth's pools/oceans contained organic molecules formed from inorganic chemistry. Energy (lightning, UV, volcanic heat) drove reactions. No O2 in early atmosphere.
Key Experiments
  1. Miller-Urey (1953): Simulated early atmosphere (CH4, H2, NH3, H2O; NO oxygen) + electric sparks → 20+ amino acids in ~1 week. Building blocks form abiotically.
  2. Fox et al. (1977): Heated amino acids at 120°C → peptide-like structures. Polymerization possible.
  3. Murchison meteorite: Purines, pyrimidines, amino acids in a meteorite — extraterrestrial organics may have seeded early Earth.
  4. RNA World Hypothesis: RNA can BOTH store genetic information AND catalyze reactions (ribozymes). May have been the first self-replicating functional molecule before DNA and proteins took specialized roles.
⚠ Trap — Miller-Urey Scope

Produced amino acids — NOT cells, NOT DNA, NOT life. Only proves building blocks can form abiotically.

The thing to remember is:

RNA World = RNA did BOTH jobs (info storage + catalysis) before specialization. Ribosomes still use rRNA as catalytic core today — a molecular fossil of this.

3.3 — Early Life Milestones

What it is
Biomarkers = molecular evidence of life in ancient rocks (carbon isotope ratios, lipids). Earliest ~3.8 bya. Stromatolites = layered mounds from microbial biofilms; fossils at 3.45 bya. LUCA ~3.5–3.8 bya.
DomainKey FeaturesEarliest Evidence
BacteriaProkaryote; peptidoglycan wall; 70S ribosomes; circular DNA~3.5 bya photosynthetic cyanobacteria; first atmospheric O2
ArchaeaProkaryote; unique membrane lipids; extremophiles; distinct biochemistryBiologically-produced methane in Australian rocks
EukaryaNuclear envelope; 80S ribosomes; organelles; ~100x larger than bacteria~1.8 bya fossil eukaryotes
Multicellularity
Evolved independently many times (≥25 origins): plants, animals, fungi, algae, slime molds. NOT a single event. Oldest multicellular eukaryote: red algae ~1.2 bya. Slime molds (Dictyostelium) show how: starved cells aggregate → slug → stalk + spores; some cells sacrifice themselves.
⚠ Trap — Multicellularity Origin

Did NOT evolve once. Convergently in many lineages. Never say "multicellularity evolved in [one event]."

The thing to remember is:

Biomarkers (3.8 bya) → Stromatolites (3.45 bya) → Eukaryotes (1.8 bya) → Multicellular eukaryotes (1.2 bya). Simpler comes first.

4.1 — Tree Components

What it is
A phylogenetic tree = hypothesis about evolutionary relationships. Tips = terminal taxa; Branches = lineages through time; Nodes = speciation events; Root = most recent common ancestor of all taxa shown; Clade = node + ALL descendants. Evolution is NOT a ladder — it is a branching bush.
⚠ Trap — Reading Relatedness

Tip ORDER on a tree does NOT show relatedness. Trace back to the SHARED NODE. Two species sharing a more RECENT common node are more closely related regardless of page position. Rotating branches around a node does NOT change tree meaning.

The thing to remember is:

Relatedness = recency of shared ancestor (which node, not position). Trees are hypotheses — can be revised.

A & B are sister taxa (share Node B). C & D are sister taxa (share Node C). Green dashed box = a clade = Node B/C + ALL descendants. Relatedness = recency of shared node, NOT position on page.

Cladistics = building trees using synapomorphies only. Ancestral (plesiomorphic) characters shared by many are NOT informative for relationships within a group.

4.3 — Parsimony & Homoplasy

What it is
Parsimony: prefer the tree requiring FEWEST evolutionary changes. Homoplasy: similarity NOT due to common descent. Two types: (1) Convergent evolution — same trait independently evolves in separate lineages; (2) Evolutionary reversal — derived character reverts to ancestral state. Both create false groupings.
HomologyHomoplasy
CauseInherited from shared ancestorIndependent evolution OR reversal
Phylo use✓ Useful — defines clades✗ Misleading — false groupings

⚠ Trap — Convergent vs. Parallel

Both are homoplasy. Convergent = completely different genetic mechanisms (camera eye in vertebrates vs. octopus). Parallel = same genetic mechanism in related lineages (similar HOX changes). Both mislead phylogenies.

4.4 — Exaptation + Case Studies (Tiktaalik, Feathers)

What it is
An exaptation = trait evolved for function A, later co-opted for function B. Evolution tinkers with existing parts; new functions arise from old structures.
Feathers — Key Example
  1. Non-flying theropods had feathers (Velociraptor arm bones show quill nodes)
  2. Arms too short to fly — feathers had other functions first
  3. Original: insulation, species recognition, mate attraction, egg incubation
  4. LATER: feathers co-opted for powered flight in bird lineage = exaptation
Tiktaalik — Phylogenies Generate Predictions
Phylogenetic analysis predicted a fish-tetrapod transitional form in mid-Devonian coastal wetland rocks. Scientists searched northern Canada → found Tiktaalik roseae (375 mya): weight-bearing elbows, bending wrist, neck, no bony operculum. Prediction confirmed by fossil = scientific success.
Swim Bladder — Another Exaptation
Fish swim bladders are homologous to lungs. Ancestral lung (breathing) was co-opted into a swim bladder (buoyancy) in many fish lineages. Tetrapods kept the lung for breathing.
⚠ Trap — Feathers Did NOT Evolve FOR Flight

FALSE. Feathers evolved in non-flying dinosaurs for insulation/display. Flight came later via co-option. Original function ≠ current function = exaptation.

Three Species Concepts — Side by Side
Biological Species Concept (BSC, Mayr): species = groups that interbreed and are reproductively isolated from others. Works for sexually reproducing species; fails for asexuals, fossils, allopatric populations.

Morphospecies Concept: species = populations distinguishable by morphology. Works on fossils and asexual taxa. Misses cryptic species; over-splits polymorphic species.

Phylogenetic Species Concept (PSC, Cracraft): species = smallest diagnosable clade with a unique synapomorphy. Works for any organism. Tends to over-split (recognizes many more species than BSC).

Ecological Species Concept (ESC, Van Valen): species = lineage occupying a distinct adaptive zone (ecological niche). Species boundaries are maintained by ecological selection, independent of reproductive isolation. Useful for ecologically distinct but interbreeding populations and asexual taxa.

14.1 — Dispersal vs. Vicariance (Biogeography)

What it is
Biogeography = study of spatial and temporal distribution of biodiversity. Initiated by Darwin and Wallace. Two processes explain why related species end up in different places: dispersal (organisms move) and vicariance (barrier forms). Phylogeography = linking phylogenetic relationships to geography to reconstruct historical events.
Marsupial Case Study (Lecture)
Oldest fossils: China 150 mya → dispersed to N. America ~120 mya → S. America → crossed Antarctica (when warm) → arrived in Australia ~55 mya. Australia drifted into isolation for 40+ million years with no placental mammals (until bats/rats arrived ~15 mya). Result: Australian marsupials radiated convergently into ecological roles paralleling placentals elsewhere — marsupial mole, marsupial wolf, etc.

14.2 — Standing Diversity Math

What it is
Standing diversity (D) = number of species present at a given time. λ = origination rate (species/my). μ = extinction rate (species/my). Turnover = total originations + extinctions.
The Formula (Global Scale)
  1. D&sub2 = D&sub1 + originations − extinctions
  2. At global scale: immigration and emigration cancel out — exclude them
  3. If λ > μ: diversity increases. If μ > λ: decreases. If λ ≈ μ: stable diversity + turnover
  4. Worked example (lecture): Start 24 sp. Stage B: +10, -6 = 28. Stage C: +8, -12 = 24.

⚠ Trap — Global vs. Local Scale

At GLOBAL scale: D&sub2 = D&sub1 + originations − extinctions ONLY. Immigration/emigration cancel globally. At LOCAL scale they matter. Robbins tests the global formula as fill-in-the-blank.

Mnemonic

“Diversity = Species Births minus Species Deaths” at global scale. λ = birth rate of species. μ = death rate of species.

14.5 — Island Biogeography (MacArthur & Wilson 1967)

What it is
Island species richness reflects a dynamic equilibrium between immigration (adds species) and extinction (removes species). At equilibrium immigration rate = extinction rate = stable species number. But species COMPOSITION keeps changing (turnover).
Key Predictions
  1. Larger islands → more species (more niches; lower extinction rate)
  2. Islands closer to mainland → more species (higher immigration rate)
  3. Large + near = most species. Small + far = fewest.
  4. Equilibrium number is STABLE; species IDENTITY still turns over
  5. Applies to any isolated habitat: forest fragments, mountaintops, nature reserves
⚠ Trap — Equilibrium = Same Number NOT Same Species

At equilibrium the NUMBER is stable but species COMPOSITION changes continuously through turnover. Equilibrium ≠ same species stay forever.

The thing to remember is:

Island biogeography is foundational for conservation — habitat fragments behave like islands. Larger + more connected reserves = more species preserved.

17.1 — Humans as Primates

What it is
Linnaeus (1758) placed humans in Order Primates based on anatomical similarities: forward-facing eyes, gripping hands with nails, large relative brain. Darwin (1871, Descent of Man) explained why: common ancestry with apes. Molecular phylogenetics unequivocally places humans nested within Primates. ⚠ Trap — "We Evolved FROM Chimps"

17.3 — Bipedalism

What it is
Habitual upright locomotion on two legs. Evolved ~6–7 mya. Bipedalism was the FIRST major hominin innovation; large brain expansion came LATER.
Fossil Evidence
  1. Foramen magnum position: humans = bottom center (upright head); apes = rear
  2. Pelvis shape: wide + bowl-shaped in humans; elongated in apes for quadrupedal locomotion
  3. Valgus femur angle: human femur angles inward to knee (feet under center of gravity); ape femur is vertical
  4. Laetoli footprints (3.5 mya, Tanzania): arched foot + forward-pointing big toe = bipedal walking identical to modern humans
  5. Lucy's knee joint: Au. afarensis knee anatomy confirms bipedal biomechanics
Why Did Bipedalism Evolve?
Thermoregulation: upright posture reduces direct sun exposure; tall body dissipates heat better. Freeing hands: frees forelimbs for carrying food, tools, and infants. Both likely contributed.
⚠ Trap — Bipedalism Trade-offs (Maladaptations)

Bipedalism came with real costs: ~80% of humans have lower back pain (spine retrofitted from horizontal to vertical without redesign); difficult childbirth (narrow bipedal pelvis + large infant head = obstetric dilemma). Evolutionary CONSTRAINTS from modifying an ancestral quadruped design.

The thing to remember is:

BIPEDALISM FIRST (~6–7 mya) → BIG BRAIN LATER. Australopithecus walked fully upright with ~450cc ape-sized brain. Don't say brain size drove bipedalism.

17.5 — Neanderthals, Modern Humans & Out of Africa

What it is
Out of Africa hypothesis: anatomically modern H. sapiens evolved in Africa (~195 kya) and expanded globally beginning ~70–50 kya, largely replacing other hominin populations but with interbreeding. Svante Pääbo's ancient DNA work (Nobel Prize 2022) provided molecular evidence.
Key Ancient DNA Findings
  1. Neanderthals diverged from H. sapiens lineage ~600–800 kya
  2. Interbreeding after H. sapiens left Africa → non-African humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA; sub-Saharan Africans carry little to none
  3. Denisovans (Siberia; known from a finger bone + DNA) also interbred → some Melanesians carry ~5% Denisovan DNA
  4. Neanderthals extinct ~40 kya — reasons debated: outcompeted, assimilation, disease, climate
  5. Oldest H. sapiens: Omo Kibish, Ethiopia (~195 kya)
⚠ Trap — Out of Africa vs. Multiregional

Multiregional: H. sapiens evolved simultaneously in multiple regions from H. erectus with continuous gene flow. Out of Africa: evolved once in Africa, spread and replaced others (with some interbreeding). Genetic evidence strongly supports Out of Africa. The geographic pattern of Neanderthal DNA (non-Africans only) PROVES it was post-migration interbreeding, not just shared ancestry.

The thing to remember is:

Non-African humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA. Geographic restriction to non-Africans = proof of post-migration interbreeding. All humans trace to African origins. African populations have greatest genetic diversity.

18.2 — Evolving Pathogens & Virulence

What it is
Virulence = degree of harm a pathogen causes its host. Pathogens evolve like any organism. Fitness = within-host replication × transmission to new hosts. This creates a fundamental virulence trade-off: maximizing replication may reduce transmission by killing the host too fast.
Virulence Trade-off Logic
  1. High virulence → more replication → more pathogen output per unit time
  2. But high virulence → kills/incapacitates host faster → fewer total transmission events
  3. Selection favors INTERMEDIATE virulence in most cases
  4. Exception: if transmission doesn't need active host (water-borne = cholera), high virulence can be maintained — bedridden/dead hosts still contaminate water supplies
Antibiotic Resistance Mechanisms
Bacteria evolve resistance through: (1) point mutations altering drug targets; (2) enzymes degrading antibiotics (beta-lactamases destroy penicillin); (3) efflux pumps expelling drugs from the cell; (4) horizontal gene transfer (HGT) — resistance plasmids passed between bacterial species, spreading resistance across phylogenetic lines rapidly.
Myxoma Virus Case Study
Introduced to Australia (1950) to kill invasive rabbits. Initially killed 99.8%. Within years: virus evolved LOWER virulence (rabbits survived longer = more mosquito feeding = more transmission). Rabbits evolved resistance. Classic coevolutionary arms race + virulence trade-off in real time.
⚠ Trap — Virulence Always Decreases?

WRONG. Virulence evolves toward whatever maximizes pathogen FITNESS. Water-borne pathogens (cholera) can maintain HIGH virulence. Evolution has no direction and no "niceness." Transmission mode is the key determinant.

13.4 — Allopolyploidy (Instant Speciation) & Haldane's Rule

What it is
Allopolyploidy = hybridization between two different species + whole-genome duplication. The offspring has a complete chromosome set from BOTH parents. Because it has a different ploidy level from either parent species, it is immediately reproductively isolated from both parents — speciation in ONE GENERATION (instant speciation). Most common in plants; ~50% of all flowering plants have polyploid ancestry.
How Allopolyploidy Creates Instant Speciation
  1. Species A (2n=14) × Species B (2n=14) → hybrid offspring (n=7 from each parent = 14 total, BUT chromosomes can't pair in meiosis — hybrid is sterile)
  2. Accidental genome duplication (e.g., failure of cell division) → allotetraploid offspring (2n=28: two complete sets from A + two from B)
  3. Chromosomes CAN now pair properly in meiosis (each set has a match)
  4. Allotetraploid is fertile but reproductively isolated from both parents — crossing back = ploidy mismatch → sterile offspring
  5. New species formed in a single generation with no geographic isolation required
Real Examples
Tragopogon (goatsbeard): two allopolyploid species formed in the Pacific Northwest within the last ~80 years from European species introduced to North America. Directly observed forming in real time. Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum): hexaploid (6n=42) from three ancestral grass species (AA + BB + DD) via two sequential allopolyploidy events over ~8,000 years.
Haldane's Rule (1922)
When hybrid offspring of one sex are inviable or sterile, it is more often the heterogametic sex (XY males in mammals/flies, ZW females in birds/butterflies). WHY: recessive incompatibility alleles on the X chromosome are exposed (hemizygous) in XY males (only one X) but masked in XX females (two X copies, second can compensate). XY males have no backup copy, so incompatibilities hit them first.
⚠ Trap — Allopolyploidy vs Autopolyploidy

Allopolyploidy = hybridization between TWO DIFFERENT SPECIES + genome duplication. Autopolyploidy = genome duplication within ONE species (extra copies of same genome). Allopolyploidy is far more common and evolutionarily important because the different chromosome sets pair properly in meiosis. ALLO = two different parents. AUTO = copies of yourself.

⚠ Trap — Allopolyploidy Requires Geographic Isolation?

NO. Allopolyploidy creates reproductive isolation IN A SINGLE GENERATION without any geographic separation. It is one of the few routes to sympatric speciation that is well-documented and uncontroversial. Plants are particularly prone to this because they tolerate polyploidy better than animals.

The thing to remember is:

Allopolyploidy = fastest possible speciation: 1 generation, no geography needed, immediately isolated from parents. ~50% of flowering plants have polyploid ancestry. Mule sterility is the FAILURE of this — no duplication step, so you get sterile hybrid not new species.

14.6 — Wallace Line, Conservation Genetics & 50/500 Rule

Wallace Line
A sharp biogeographic boundary running through the Malay Archipelago (between Bali and Lombok; between Borneo and Sulawesi). West of line: Asian fauna (primates, tigers, elephants). East of line: Australasian fauna (marsupials, cockatoos). The line marks the edge of the continental shelves of Asia and Australia. Even during ice age low sea levels, a deep water channel remained — preventing most organisms from crossing. Discovered by Alfred Russel Wallace in 1859. Classic example of vicariance creating biogeographic patterns.
Conservation Genetics: The 50/500 Rule
Minimum viable population size guideline: (1) Ne ≥ 50 in the short term to prevent rapid inbreeding depression (limits inbreeding rate to ~1% per generation); (2) Ne ≥ 500 in the long term to maintain sufficient genetic variation for adaptive evolution. Ne (effective population size) is always smaller than N (census population) due to unequal sex ratios, variance in reproductive success, and population fluctuations. Some scientists suggest 100/1000 as more realistic.
Inbreeding Depression
Inbreeding increases homozygosity. Mechanisms of harm: (1) deleterious recessive alleles normally masked in heterozygotes become expressed in homozygotes; (2) loss of heterozygote advantage (e.g., MHC immune genes where heterozygotes recognize more pathogens). Classic example: cheetahs have so little genetic diversity that skin grafts between unrelated individuals are accepted without immune rejection (normally rejected due to MHC diversity). Result: ~70% abnormal sperm, high disease susceptibility.
Parapatric Speciation
A third speciation mode (between allopatric and sympatric). Populations have PARTIAL geographic separation — they meet along a narrow contact zone where some gene flow occurs. Strong selection gradients on each side can overcome gene flow, driving divergence. If assortative mating evolves in the contact zone, full speciation can result. Less common than allopatric; harder to document than sympatric.
⚠ Trap — Ne vs. N

Effective population size (Ne) is ALWAYS smaller than census population (N). Ne reflects the number of breeding individuals that contribute equally to the next generation. Most real populations have Ne much smaller than N due to unequal reproductive success, sex ratio imbalances, and population bottlenecks. Conservation policy targets Ne, not N.

8.2 — Specific Human-Driven Evolution Examples

Trophy Hunting — Bighorn Sheep
Trophy hunting selectively removes individuals with the LARGEST traits (biggest horns = most desirable trophy). Over 30 years of trophy hunting in Canadian bighorn sheep: average horn size declined approximately 20%. Mechanism: large-horned males killed before they can maximize reproduction → genes for smaller horns increase in frequency. This is directional selection opposing natural selection (which favors larger horns for male–male competition). The evolutionary change may be difficult to reverse because the alleles for larger horns have been depleted.
Tusklessness in African Elephants
In Mozambique, decades of intense ivory poaching during the civil war (1977–1992) drove rapid evolution of tusklessness in elephants. Before the war: ~18% of females were naturally tuskless. After: ~33% tuskless in heavily poached populations. Tusklessness is heritable and linked to a dominant allele. Selection coefficient was extremely strong — tusked females were killed. Classic human-imposed directional selection on a heritable trait.
Antibiotic Resistance — Mechanisms
Four main mechanisms: (1) Target modification — mutations alter the antibiotic binding site (e.g., ribosome changes block tetracycline); (2) Enzymatic degradation — beta-lactamases destroy the beta-lactam ring of penicillin/ampicillin; (3) Efflux pumps — membrane proteins actively pump antibiotics OUT of the cell; (4) Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) — resistance plasmids passed between bacterial species via conjugation, transformation, or transduction — spreads resistance ACROSS species lines.
⚠ Trap — Trophy Hunting Selection Direction

Trophy hunting creates selection in the OPPOSITE direction from natural selection. Natural selection favors LARGE horns (male competition for mates). Trophy hunting removes large-horned individuals before they can fully reproduce, favoring SMALL horns. This is one of the clearest demonstrations that humans impose strong, unnatural selection pressures on wild populations.

17.6 — Denisovans, Anatomically vs. Behaviorally Modern, Brain Evolution

Denisovans
An archaic hominin group identified primarily through DNA from a finger bone in Denisova Cave, Siberia (2010). Known from very few fossils but extensive genomic data. Diverged from Neanderthals ~400 kya. Interbred with modern humans: Melanesians/Papua New Guineans carry ~3–6% Denisovan DNA; some mainland Asian and Indigenous Australian populations also show traces. Key finding: the EPAS1 gene (high-altitude adaptation) in Tibetans originated from a Denisovan gene variant. Modern humans acquired a Denisovan adaptation through interbreeding — direct fitness benefit from archaic introgression.
Anatomically Modern vs. Behaviorally Modern H. sapiens
Anatomically modern H. sapiens: oldest fossils ~195 kya (Omo Kibish, Ethiopia) / ~315 kya revised (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco). Have modern skeletal features (high domed skull, reduced brow ridges, chin).
Behaviorally modern H. sapiens: appear ~70–100 kya. Show symbolic thought, abstract art, personal ornaments (shell beads), long-distance trade networks, composite tools. There was a ~100,000 year gap between anatomical modernity and full behavioral modernity.
What Drove Brain Expansion? (Multiple Hypotheses)
Social brain hypothesis: tracking complex social relationships in large groups favored larger neocortex. Ecological hypothesis: environmental problem-solving (foraging, tool use) drove brain expansion. Cooking hypothesis (Wrangham): cooking food with fire dramatically increased caloric yield from same raw ingredients → provided energy to support larger, metabolically expensive brains. Cooking may have made brain expansion energetically feasible.
MHC and Mate Choice
Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) = immune gene region with extreme polymorphism. Studies show humans prefer mates with DIFFERENT MHC types (detected via body odor — "sweaty T-shirt" experiments). Benefit: offspring with diverse MHC can recognize more pathogens. This is one of the few documented cases of genetic mate choice in humans driven by immune gene diversity.

18.4 — Sickle Cell, Cancer as Somatic Evolution & More Trade-offs

Sickle Cell: Balanced Polymorphism
Sickle cell is caused by a single nucleotide change in beta-globin: Glutamic acid → Valine (Glu6Val). HbS/HbS homozygotes: severe sickle cell anemia (sickling of red blood cells, chronic illness, early death without treatment). HbA/HbS heterozygotes: ~25% protection against severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria (parasitized cells sickle and are cleared by spleen; intracellular environment hostile to parasite). In malaria-endemic Africa, HbS maintained at 10–20% frequency — classic heterozygote advantage (balancing selection via overdominance). In non-malarial regions: HbS is purely deleterious (no compensating benefit for heterozygotes).
Cancer as Somatic Evolution
Cancer is evolution happening INSIDE the body within a single lifetime. Key parallels: (1) Heritable variation: somatic mutations create variant cell lineages; (2) Natural selection: cells with mutations allowing faster proliferation, immune evasion, or metastasis are selected; (3) Adaptation: tumors evolve resistance to chemotherapy (same mechanism as antibiotic resistance). Why doesn't selection eliminate cancer-causing alleles? Because most cancers hit late in life — antagonistic pleiotropy: p53 prevents cancer early (fitness benefit) but its high activity may accelerate aging (cost). Also: high cell division rates in early development are good for growth but increase mutation risk later.
p53 — The Guardian of the Genome
p53 = tumor suppressor protein encoded by the TP53 gene. Triggers cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, or apoptosis (programmed cell death) when DNA damage is detected. Early-life benefit: prevents cancer during reproductive years. Late-life cost: excessive p53 activity may deplete stem cells → tissue atrophy, reduced regeneration = accelerated aging. Mouse experiments: enhanced p53 → reduced cancer BUT accelerated aging. Classic antagonistic pleiotropy.
Evolutionary Mismatch: The Back
The human spine evolved from a horizontal quadruped design (~300 million years of evolution) and was co-opted for vertical bipedalism only ~6–7 million years ago. The S-curve compensates for upright posture but creates mechanical vulnerabilities: lumbar disc herniation, vertebral compression fractures, spondylolisthesis, chronic lower back pain. ~80% of humans experience significant back pain. This is evolutionary constraint (Reason #4): selection can only tinker with existing structures, not redesign from scratch.
⚠ Trap — Sickle Cell Heterozygote Fitness

In malaria-endemic regions: fitness ranking is HbA/HbS > HbA/HbA > HbS/HbS. Heterozygotes have the HIGHEST fitness (heterozygote advantage). In non-malarial regions: HbA/HbA > HbA/HbS > HbS/HbS (sickle cell is just deleterious). The SAME genotype has completely different fitness in different environments. This is why the allele is found at high frequency in sub-Saharan Africa but not in non-malarial populations.

⚠ Trap — Cancer Is Evolution

Cancer IS evolution — it is natural selection acting on somatic cell lineages within a body. Cells with mutations enabling faster proliferation, immune evasion, or metastasis are "selected for" within the tumor microenvironment. Tumors evolve drug resistance by the same mechanism bacteria evolve antibiotic resistance: pre-existing variation + selection. Chemotherapy resistance = same logic as antibiotic resistance.

✅ A. The coccyx is a vestigial structure: it retains its homology to primate tails (same bones, same developmental origin, same genetic basis) but is greatly reduced in size and has changed function (from tail movement to posture muscle attachment). It is evidence of common ancestry because the most parsimonious explanation for sharing this structure is descent from a common tailed ancestor. Darwin used it explicitly in Descent of Man (1871). | Wrong E: The coccyx is homologous to primate tails (same embryological origin, same caudal vertebrae); calling it analogous would mean independent evolution, which contradicts the shared developmental evidence.
Q77 — Which evidence type is WEAKEST for phylogenetics?
Which type of similarity is LEAST reliable as evidence for inferring phylogenetic relationships?
✅ B. Analogous structures (homoplasy) are the LEAST reliable for phylogenetics because they arise from convergent evolution — independent evolution in unrelated lineages due to similar selective pressures. Using them as evidence for relationship gives false groupings. Synapomorphies (A), molecular data (C), and developmental homologies (D) all reflect shared ancestry and are reliable. Cladistics specifically excludes analogous traits and focuses only on synapomorphies. | Wrong E: Symplesiomorphies are shared ancestral traits that are uninformative for grouping taxa into specific derived clades; analogous structures from convergent evolution (B) are even more misleading for phylogenetics.
Q78 — Scenario: Gene flow and species status
Two populations of oak trees produce fertile hybrids in the narrow zone where their ranges overlap. However, morphologists classify them as separate species based on leaf shape differences. Which statement BEST represents the situation?
✅ C. Oaks are famous for hybridizing across apparent species boundaries — a classic problem for species concepts. BSC: fertile hybrids = some gene flow = possibly one species. Morphospecies: different enough morphologically = different species. The general lineage concept might consider them different if the bulk of each population is evolving independently despite rare hybridization. This illustrates WHY no single species concept works universally — hybridizing plants regularly break BSC assumptions. | Wrong E: Ring species involve a continuous geographic chain of interbreeding populations; the oak scenario describes two morphologically distinct populations with a hybrid zone, interpreted differently by BSC and morphospecies concepts.
Q79 — Turnover at equilibrium
Two islands have the same equilibrium species number (50 species each). Island A is far from the mainland; Island B is close. At equilibrium, which island has higher species turnover?
✅ B. Key MacArthur-Wilson insight: equilibrium species number is the same, but the RATES can differ. Island B (close) has a HIGH immigration rate. To reach the same equilibrium, it must also have a high extinction rate (more colonizers arriving = more competition = more local extinctions). Higher immigration + higher extinction = HIGHER TURNOVER at the same equilibrium number. Island A has LOW immigration and LOW extinction = same number but lower turnover. Species identity changes faster on B. | Wrong E: Endemism does not increase turnover rate; turnover is the rate of species replacement, and closer islands (B) have higher immigration and extinction rates, yielding higher turnover at the same equilibrium number.
Q80 — Final integrative question
A population of bacteria is treated with antibiotic X for one week. Resistance evolves. The bacteria are then treated with a COMBINATION of antibiotics X+Y simultaneously. Why is combination therapy much harder for bacteria to overcome?
✅ D. This is evolutionary logic applied to medicine. Single-drug resistance: the bacterial population already contains some cells with pre-existing resistance to drug X (frequent mutation). Combination therapy: a cell needs SIMULTANEOUS pre-existing resistance to BOTH X and Y. If resistance frequency to X is 10⁻⁶ and to Y is 10⁻⁶, the probability of one cell having both is ~10⁻¹² — vanishingly rare in a normal infection. This is why HIV triple therapy revolutionized AIDS treatment and why TB treatment uses 4+ drugs. | Wrong E: Mutations occur regardless of population size (they are random errors during replication); the key is that simultaneously carrying resistance to BOTH drugs is the product of two independent low probabilities, making it extremely unlikely.

⚠ Did antibiotics CAUSE resistance mutations?
NO. Mutations are random and pre-existing. Antibiotics KILL susceptibles and SELECT FOR pre-existing resistant variants. No directed mutation. Antibiotics reveal and amplify existing variation.
⚠ Did humans evolve FROM chimpanzees?
NO. Humans and chimps share a common ancestor ~7 mya. We are SISTER LINEAGES. Neither is the ancestor of the other. Both evolved since the split.
⚠ Bipedalism before or after large brains?
BIPEDALISM FIRST (~6–7 mya). Large brain expansion came later. Australopithecus walked fully upright with ~450cc ape-sized brain. Sequence matters for exams.
⚠ BSC fails in which 3 scenarios?
1) Fossils (can't test gene flow). 2) Asexual species (bacteria/archaea). 3) Hybridizing species (fertile hybrids exist). Know all three for Robbins' scenario questions.
⚠ Mule = hybrid inviability or sterility?
Hybrid STERILITY. Mules are healthy and vigorous but CANNOT REPRODUCE — 63 chromosomes can't pair in meiosis. Inviability = can't survive. Sterility = survives, can't reproduce.
⚠ Island equilibrium = same NUMBER or same SPECIES?
Same NUMBER but species IDENTITY changes (turnover). Some go extinct, new ones colonize. Equilibrium diversity is stable; which species are present is not.
⚠ Do pathogens always evolve LOWER virulence?
NO. Virulence evolves to maximize pathogen FITNESS. Water-borne pathogens (cholera) can maintain HIGH virulence — transmission doesn't need an active host. Evolution has no direction.
⚠ Is FOXP2 "the language gene"?
Oversimplification. FOXP2 = transcription factor for fine orofacial motor control. Functions in mice + birds too. Necessary but NOT sufficient for language. 2 human-specific amino acid changes after chimp split.
⚠ Global diversity formula — include immigration?
NO. Global: D2 = D1 + originations − extinctions ONLY. Immigration + emigration cancel globally. Only matter at local/regional scale. Robbins fill-in-the-blank target.
⚠ Dispersal vs vicariance — who moves?
DISPERSAL = organisms MOVE to new area. VICARIANCE = organisms STAY, BARRIER appears. Hawaiian crickets = dispersal. Panama Isthmus = vicariance. Active vs passive movement.
⚠ Neanderthal brain vs H. sapiens — who has bigger?
Neanderthals = LARGER or equal (~1500cc vs ~1350cc). Brain size ≠ survival advantage. Went extinct despite bigger brains. Organization + behavior matter more than raw volume.
⚠ Miller-Urey produced what exactly?
Amino acids (20+ kinds) from inorganic material + simulated lightning. NOT cells. NOT DNA. NOT life. Only proves building blocks can form abiotically. Don't overstate.
⚠ Feathers evolved FOR flight — true or false?
FALSE. Feathers evolved in non-flying theropods for insulation/display. Arms too short to fly early on. Flight evolved LATER when feathers were co-opted = EXAPTATION. Original function ≠ current function.
⚠ Antagonistic pleiotropy vs mutation accumulation?
Antagonistic pleiotropy = one allele with BOTH early benefits AND late costs (testosterone). Mutation accumulation = late mutations with NO early benefit accumulate because selection is weak. Does it HELP early? Yes = antagonistic pleiotropy.
⚠ Synapomorphy vs ancestral character — which defines a clade?
SYNAPOMORPHY (shared DERIVED character) only. Ancestral characters shared by many groups tell you NOTHING about relationships within those groups. Only derived characters evolved in the immediate ancestor of a specific clade are informative.
⚠ Allopolyploidy needs geographic isolation to form new species?
FALSE. Allopolyploidy creates immediate reproductive isolation (different ploidy level) in a SINGLE GENERATION with no geographic separation needed. One of the only true routes to instant sympatric speciation. ~50% of flowering plants have polyploid ancestry.
⚠ What causes hybrid male sterility before female sterility? (Haldane's Rule)
Heterogametic sex (XY males) has recessive incompatibility alleles on the single X EXPOSED (no second X to mask them). Homogametic (XX females) have a backup. In birds, females are ZW (heterogametic) so FEMALES are sterile first. Rule = heterogametic sex goes first.
⚠ Sickle cell in malaria region — which genotype has HIGHEST fitness?
HbA/HbS HETEROZYGOTE has HIGHEST fitness in malaria-endemic regions. HbA/HbA = susceptible to malaria. HbS/HbS = severe anemia. HbA/HbS = protected from malaria WITHOUT anemia. Classic heterozygote advantage (overdominance).
⚠ Where is Denisovan DNA found (vs. Neanderthal DNA)?
Denisovan DNA: primarily Melanesians/Pacific Islanders (~3–6%) + some Asians. Neanderthal DNA: ALL non-African modern humans (~1–4%). Denisovan = Pacific/Asian region. Neanderthal = global non-African. Africans have neither (never encountered either species).
⚠ Great Oxidation Event — who caused it and when?
Cyanobacteria evolving oxygenic photosynthesis (~2.6 bya) → O2 accumulated → Great Oxidation Event ~2.4 bya. Consequence: mass extinction of anaerobes; ozone layer formed; aerobic respiration became possible. NOT from eukaryotes (they came AFTER GOE).
⚠ Ne vs. N — which is always smaller?
Ne (effective population size) is ALWAYS smaller than N (census population). Ne reflects actual breeding individuals contributing equally. Unequal sex ratios, variance in reproductive success, and bottlenecks reduce Ne below N. Conservation targets Ne ≥ 50 (short-term) and Ne ≥ 500 (long-term).
⚠ Cancer = evolution? What does that mean?
YES. Cancer IS somatic evolution: heritable variation (somatic mutations) + natural selection (cells with proliferation advantages replicate more) = adaptation of tumor. Drug resistance in cancer = same mechanism as antibiotic resistance: pre-existing resistant variants selected for when drug kills susceptibles.
⚠ Anatomically modern vs. behaviorally modern H. sapiens — which came first and by how long?
ANATOMICAL modernity (~195 kya) came ~100,000 years BEFORE BEHAVIORAL modernity (~70–100 kya). Modern skeleton first; art, symbols, long-distance trade, ornaments came later. Don't equate looking modern with acting modern.
⚠ Wallace Line: what's on each side?
WEST: Asian fauna (primates, tigers, elephants, Asian deer). EAST: Australasian fauna (marsupials, cockatoos, birds of paradise). A deep ocean channel persisted even during ice ages — discovered by Alfred Russel Wallace 1859. Classic vicariance in biogeography.
⚠ Trophy hunting: which direction does it drive evolution vs. natural selection?
OPPOSITE directions. Natural selection favors LARGE horns/tusks (male competition, mate attraction). Trophy hunting removes LARGE-horned/tusked individuals → selects for SMALL traits. Bighorn sheep: 20% horn size decline over 30 years. Elephants: tusklessness increasing in poached populations.

Radiometric dating — mechanism
Answer
WHAT: Measure parent:daughter isotope ratio in a rock. Apply known half-life to calculate elapsed time. HOW: Radioactive parent decays at constant rate into daughter isotope — temperature, pressure, chemistry cannot change this rate. WHY reliable: Decay constant = true clock.
🧠 Remember it
A ticking hourglass of radioactive sand — unstable parent isotopes grain-by-grain decay into stable daughter isotopes at a fixed rate, and the ratio between the two chambers tells you how long the glass has been running.
The fixed decay constant is the hourglass's calibrated flow rate; measuring parent-to-daughter ratio in a rock is reading the sand levels to back-calculate elapsed time since the mineral crystallized.

Half-life — definition
Answer
WHAT: Time for 50% of parent isotope to decay into daughter. KEY: FIXED — unaffected by temperature, pressure, chemistry, or burial depth. Each isotope has a unique half-life. WHY useful: After N half-lives, (½)ᴺ of original parent remains — gives precise age.
🧠 Remember it
A pile of popcorn kernels where exactly half pop during each identical interval — 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 — regardless of how many kernels you started with.
Half-life is the fixed time for 50% of parent atoms to decay, and like the popcorn it is independent of starting quantity, temperature, or chemistry, giving radiometric dating its reliability.

CH4
Phylogenetic tree — 5 components defined
Answer
TIPS: Living (or fossil) taxa being compared. BRANCHES: Lineages through time. NODES: Speciation events (common ancestors). ROOT: Most recent common ancestor of all taxa shown. CLADE: Node + ALL descendants. WHY it matters: Trees are hypotheses of evolutionary history, not observations — tested with more data.
🧠 Remember it
A family tree with named parts — root (common ancestor), nodes (speciation events), branches (lineages through time), tips (taxa, extant or extinct), and clades (any node plus all its descendants).
Each element maps to a biological entity: the root is LUCA-level ancestor, nodes are splitting events, branches are evolving populations, tips are the species sampled, and a clade is the monophyletic unit of comparison.
CH4
Sister taxa — definition
Answer
WHAT: Two lineages that diverged from the SAME immediate ancestral node. HOW to identify: Share a node not shared with any other taxon in the analysis. KEY: Sister taxa are each other's CLOSEST relatives — most recently diverged. EXAMPLE: Humans and chimps are sister taxa within hominins.
🧠 Remember it
Two siblings who share only their parents with each other — taxa that share a most recent common ancestor exclusive to the two of them, like chimps and bonobos on the hominid tree.
Sister taxa are each other's closest relative because no other lineage branches off between them and their shared node, just as siblings share parents with no one else.
CH4
Synapomorphy — definition + why it defines clades
Answer
WHAT: A SHARED DERIVED character state — evolved in the common ancestor of a clade and inherited by ALL descendants. WHY it defines clades (not ancestral characters): Ancestral characters (plesiomorphies) are shared by groups with different evolutionary histories → mislead. Only synapomorphies uniquely mark a lineage. EXAMPLE: Hair = synapomorphy of mammals; vertebrae = synapomorphy of vertebrates.
🧠 Remember it
A shared family heirloom invented by grandma — a derived trait (feathers, amniotic egg, mammary glands) that arose in a common ancestor and is inherited by all descendants, marking them as one clade.
Because synapomorphies are novelties originating at a single node, every organism carrying one must descend from that node, making them the logical evidence for monophyletic group membership.
CH4
Monophyletic vs. Paraphyletic vs. Polyphyletic
Answer
MONOPHYLETIC: Common ancestor + ALL descendants = VALID clade. PARAPHYLETIC: Common ancestor + SOME descendants (some excluded) = INVALID. Example: 'Reptiles' without birds. POLYPHYLETIC: Does NOT include most recent common ancestor of all members = INVALID. Example: 'warm-blooded animals' (birds + mammals separately evolved endothermy).
🧠 Remember it
A whole pizza (mono = ancestor + all descendants), a pizza with a slice missing (para = ancestor + some descendants, e.g., 'reptiles' minus birds), and toppings picked off two different pizzas (poly = no common ancestor, e.g., 'warm-blooded animals').
Only monophyletic groups reflect true evolutionary history; paraphyletic groups arbitrarily exclude a descendant clade, and polyphyletic groups lump traits that evolved independently, so cladistics only accepts monophyly.
CH4
Are 'Reptiles' monophyletic? Why not?
Answer
NO — PARAPHYLETIC. 'Reptiles' traditionally = lizards, snakes, turtles, crocs, but EXCLUDES birds. WHY wrong: Crocodilians are MORE closely related to birds than to lizards. Birds ARE avian theropod dinosaurs. FIX: Reptilia must include birds to be monophyletic. Lesson: Common names often reflect appearance, not evolutionary history.
🧠 Remember it
A pizza with birds sliced off the top — traditional 'Reptilia' includes turtles, lizards, crocs but excludes birds, even though birds nest inside Reptilia (sister to crocodilians).
Because birds descend from within the reptile clade, any 'reptile' group that excludes them is paraphyletic; the monophyletic fix is Sauropsida, which includes birds.
CH4
Principle of parsimony
Answer
WHAT: Choose the phylogenetic tree requiring the FEWEST evolutionary changes (steps) as the preferred hypothesis. WHY: Extra steps = extra homoplasy assumed — we should minimize unparsimonious assumptions. HOW applied: Count total character state changes on each possible tree; tree with minimum steps is most parsimonious. LIMITATION: Rapid evolution or convergence can mislead parsimony (long-branch attraction).
🧠 Remember it
Occam's razor applied to trees — given competing phylogenies, prefer the one requiring the fewest independent evolutionary changes (character-state transitions), like choosing the shortest route on a road map.
Parsimony minimizes ad-hoc assumptions of convergence or reversal; the simplest explanation with the fewest evolutionary steps is treated as the best working hypothesis until contradicted.
CH4
Homoplasy — definition + 2 types
Answer
WHAT: Similarity NOT due to shared ancestry. TYPE 1 — Convergent evolution: Same trait evolves independently via DIFFERENT developmental/genetic mechanisms (e.g., camera eye in vertebrates vs. octopus — same structure, different embryonic origin). TYPE 2 — Evolutionary reversal: Derived character reverts to ancestral state (e.g., limblessness in snakes — tetrapod ancestor lost limbs). WHY problematic: Can mislead phylogenetic analysis.
🧠 Remember it
Two unrelated actors wearing the same costume — trait similarity not inherited from a common ancestor, arising by (1) convergence (independent evolution from different starting points) or (2) reversal (loss back to ancestral state).
Homoplasy is the noise that parsimony tries to minimize: convergence invents the same look twice (wings in bats vs. birds), reversal unwinds a trait (leglessness in snakes).
CH4
Homology vs. homoplasy — examples
Answer
HOMOLOGY: Similarity due to SHARED ANCESTRY. Human arm bones = whale flipper bones = bat wing bones — all modified from same tetrapod forelimb (different function, same bones). HOMOPLASY: Dolphin streamlined body vs. shark streamlined body — similar shape via DIFFERENT evolutionary paths (mammals vs. fish). TEST: Homologs have same embryonic origin; analogs do not.
🧠 Remember it
Inherited family silver (homology) vs. two strangers who independently bought the same fork (homoplasy) — human arm, bat wing, whale flipper are homologous pentadactyl limbs; bird wing vs. insect wing are homoplastic flight surfaces.
Homology reflects shared ancestry (same bones under different skins), while homoplasy reflects similar selective pressures producing lookalike solutions in different lineages — only homology builds trees.
CH4
Convergent vs. parallel evolution
Answer
CONVERGENT: Different genetic/developmental mechanisms in distantly related organisms produce similar phenotype. Example: camera eyes in vertebrates vs. octopus (different genes, different embryology). PARALLEL: SAME genetic pathway in closely related organisms. Example: loss of armor plates in multiple freshwater stickleback populations — same Pitx1 gene each time. KEY distinction: Mechanism, not just outcome.
🧠 Remember it
Two cars ending up with the same paint job — convergent: started different colors (sharks and dolphins, different body plans → same fusiform shape); parallel: started the same color (marsupial and placental sabertooth cats, similar starting morphology → same daggers).
Both produce homoplasy, but parallel evolution operates on lineages with very similar genetic/developmental starting points, while convergence reaches similarity from markedly different ancestors.
CH4
Exaptation — definition + feather example
Answer
WHAT: Trait that evolved for function A later co-opted for function B. FEATHERS: Evolved in non-flying theropod dinosaurs (Velociraptor had feather quill nodes; arms too short for flight). Original functions: insulation, species recognition, display, egg incubation. Flight was a LATER exaptation. OTHER example: Fish swim bladder (ancestral lung) → buoyancy organ in bony fishes.
🧠 Remember it
Converting an old pickup truck into a flatbed — feathers originally evolved for insulation/display in theropod dinosaurs, then were repurposed as aerodynamic surfaces for flight in birds.
Exaptation (Gould & Vrba) keeps the existing part but recruits it for a new function; the feather example shows that the trait's current utility need not equal its original adaptive origin.
CH4
Feathers — did they evolve FOR flight?
Answer
NO — classic exaptation. Fossil evidence (Sinosauropteryx, Velociraptor, Anchiornis) shows feathers in non-flying theropods. Velociraptor's arms were too short to generate lift. Feathers predate flight by tens of millions of years. Original functions: Insulation (found on small body dinosaurs that lose heat fast), display, brooding eggs. Archaeopteryx (150 mya) = earliest known flying bird.
🧠 Remember it
A down jacket later duct-taped into a paraglider — feathered non-avian dinosaurs like Sinosauropteryx had insulating filaments and display plumage millions of years before any feathered animal could fly.
Flight is an exaptive reuse of feathers that first served thermoregulation and signaling; the fossil record shows feathers on flightless ground-dwelling theropods, falsifying a flight-first origin.
CH4
Cladistics — what, who, how
Answer
WHAT: Phylogenetic classification method using ONLY shared derived characters (synapomorphies) to build trees. WHO: Developed by Willi Hennig (1966). HOW: (1) Identify character states; (2) Determine ancestral vs. derived states (using outgroup comparison); (3) Group by synapomorphies; (4) Apply parsimony. WHY preferred: Produces strictly monophyletic groupings.
🧠 Remember it
Willi Hennig's 1950s tree-building method — group organisms by shared derived characters (synapomorphies), not overall similarity, using parsimony to find the tree requiring the fewest character-state changes.
Hennig's insight was that only synapomorphies carry phylogenetic signal; his algorithm (outgroup rooting + parsimony) replaced intuitive taxonomy with a testable, repeatable phylogenetic method.
CH4
Outgroup comparison — what, why
Answer
WHAT: Compare in-group taxa to an outgroup (a taxon outside the group of interest) to determine which character state is ancestral vs. derived. HOW: Character state shared with outgroup = ANCESTRAL (plesiomorphy). Character state unique to in-group = DERIVED (apomorphy). WHY essential: Cannot identify synapomorphies without knowing which state is ancestral.
🧠 Remember it
A control group for the tree — a taxon known to branch off before the ingroup (e.g., a shark when studying bony fish) whose character states polarize which traits are ancestral vs. derived inside the ingroup.
Without an outgroup you cannot tell a synapomorphy from a symplesiomorphy; the outgroup supplies the ancestral reference state needed to root the tree and polarize character changes.
CH4
Molecular phylogenetics — what sequences used
Answer
WHAT: Build phylogenies using DNA/RNA/protein sequences instead of morphology. MOLECULES used: rRNA genes (highly conserved — good for deep phylogeny, e.g., 3 domains); mitochondrial DNA (fast-evolving — good for recent divergences, population-level); nuclear protein-coding genes; whole genomes. WHY powerful: Overcomes morphological convergence; provides thousands of independent characters.
🧠 Remember it
Reading heritable text — DNA/RNA sequences (rRNA genes for deep splits, mitochondrial COI for species barcoding, nuclear protein-coding genes and whole genomes for finer resolution) used as aligned character matrices.
Different loci evolve at different rates, so slow-evolving rRNA resolves domain-level splits while fast-evolving mtDNA resolves recent speciation; concatenated or coalescent analyses combine many loci for robust trees.
CH4
Phylogeography — vicariance & dispersal questions it answers
Answer
WHAT: Study of the geographic distributions of genealogical lineages within and among species. HOW: Integrates phylogenetics + biogeography. QUESTIONS asked: Did populations separate by vicariance (barrier formed) or dispersal (organisms moved)? Do phylogenetic patterns match geographic patterns? EXAMPLE: Hawaiian island chain phylogeny mirrors island age (youngest island = most recently colonized populations).
🧠 Remember it
A genealogy plotted on a map — tracing gene lineages across landscapes to see how populations spread, split, and came back together.
Phylogeography links intraspecific gene trees to geographic distribution, revealing recent history (refugia, expansions, barriers) that deeper phylogenies miss.
CH4
Bootstrap support — what it measures
Answer
WHAT: Statistical measure of confidence in a node (clade) in a phylogenetic tree. HOW: Resample data randomly with replacement hundreds of times; rebuild tree each time; count what % of resampled trees contain the same node. INTERPRETATION: Bootstrap value ≥70% = moderately supported; ≥95% = strongly supported. WHY: Tells you how reproducible a node is — low bootstrap = node sensitive to data subset.
🧠 Remember it
Repeatedly re-rolling the data dice — randomly resampling columns of the sequence alignment 1000 times with replacement and recomputing the tree, then reporting the percentage of replicates recovering each branch.
A bootstrap value (e.g., 95%) quantifies how robust a clade is to sampling variation in the data; high values mean the signal is consistent across characters, low values mean the branch is fragile.

CH13 • Speciation Ch 13 • The Origin of Species

CH13
Biological Species Concept (BSC) — definition + 3 failures
Answer
WHAT (Mayr 1942): Groups of interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups. FAILS for: (1) Fossils — can't test gene flow in extinct taxa; (2) Asexual species — bacteria, archaea, many plants reproduce without sex; (3) Hybridizing species — lions × tigers produce ligers; many plant species freely hybridize. STRENGTH: Gene flow = evolutionary unit — biologically meaningful.
🧠 Remember it
Mayr's 'interbreeding community' rule — species = groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations reproductively isolated from others; fails on (1) asexual organisms (bacteria), (2) fossils (can't test mating), and (3) ring species / hybridizing species (horses-donkeys, wolves-coyotes).
BSC is powerful for sexual, extant animals but inapplicable to asexuals, untestable on fossils, and breaks down when reproductive isolation is incomplete, motivating alternative species concepts.
CH13
Morphospecies Concept — definition + failure
Answer
WHAT: Species defined by measurable morphological differences from all other species. STRENGTHS: Works for fossils; fast field identification. FAILS for: (1) Cryptic species — look identical but genetically distinct (e.g., mosquitoes in Anopheles gambiae complex); (2) Phenotypically plastic species — same genotype produces very different morphologies in different environments. USE: Most museum taxonomy + fossil record.
🧠 Remember it
Sorting organisms by their silhouette — species defined by distinct morphology; fails on cryptic species (morphologically identical but reproductively isolated, like many frogs, mosquitoes, and Amazonian dolphins) and sexually dimorphic species (males/females look different).
It's the practical default for fossils and museum collections but mistakes look-alikes for one species and splits dimorphic species into two, because appearance is an imperfect proxy for lineage identity.
CH13
Phylogenetic Species Concept (PSC) — definition
Answer
WHAT (Cracraft): Smallest monophyletic group sharing a unique combination of character states (synapomorphies). STRENGTHS: Works for asexual and sexual organisms; works for fossils; theoretically clean. FAILS for: (1) Requires established phylogeny (not always available); (2) Over-splits — defines many more 'species' than BSC → management/conservation complications. IMPLICATION: PSC species are often subspecies under BSC.
🧠 Remember it
The smallest identifiable branch on a gene tree — a species is the smallest monophyletic group of organisms diagnosable by a shared derived character (molecular or morphological).
PSC operationalizes species as tip-level clades on a phylogeny, which works for asexuals and fossils, though it tends to split lineages into more species than BSC because any fixed difference can diagnose a new 'species.'
CH13
General Lineage Species Concept
Answer
WHAT (de Queiroz): Species = independently evolving metapopulation lineages. Unifying concept — all other species concepts detect different properties of the same underlying lineage separation. KEY INSIGHT: Speciation is a process (gradual), not an event — different criteria (gene flow, morphology, diagnosis) reach the same conclusion at different points in the process. USE: Academic synthesis; explains why species concepts disagree.
🧠 Remember it
A river delta where distributaries have clearly separated — multiple criteria (interbreeding, ecology, diagnosability) are just different ways of noticing the same diverging channel.
De Queiroz unifies species concepts by defining species as separately evolving metapopulation lineages; different concepts (BSC, PSC, ecological) are just operational criteria for detecting lineage separation at different stages.
CH13
3 steps of speciation (+ reinforcement)
Answer
STEP 1: Barrier to gene flow forms (geographic, behavioral, ecological, temporal). STEP 2: Populations diverge genetically via mutation, drift, and/or natural selection. STEP 3: Reproductive isolation evolves as byproduct of divergence (Bateson-Dobzhansky-Muller incompatibilities). STEP 4 (if secondary contact): Reinforcement — natural selection strengthens prezygotic barriers if hybrids have low fitness.
🧠 Remember it
A divorce in three acts plus a restraining order — (1) isolation of populations, (2) divergence under drift/selection, (3) development of reproductive isolation, with reinforcement as the post-contact step that strengthens barriers if hybrids are unfit.
Speciation requires populations to stop exchanging genes, accumulate differences, and evolve barriers; reinforcement adds selection against hybridization when incipient species re-meet, completing the split.
CH13
Prezygotic barriers — pre-mating (3 types)
Answer
1. Habitat isolation: Same region but different microhabitats — populations don't encounter each other. Example: Rhagoletis on hawthorn vs. apple. 2. Temporal isolation: Different breeding/flowering times. Example: hummingbird plants staggering flowering phenology. 3. Behavioral isolation: Different mate-recognition signals, courtship displays, mating calls. Example: firefly flash patterns species-specific.
🧠 Remember it
Three reasons the date never happens — (1) ecological/habitat isolation (they live in different neighborhoods), (2) temporal isolation (they're active in different seasons/times), (3) behavioral isolation (different courtship signals, like firefly flash codes).
All three prevent mating from ever being attempted, so no gametes meet; examples are rainbow trout stream races, 13- vs. 17-year cicadas, and Drosophila courtship songs.
CH13
Prezygotic barriers — post-mating pre-fertilization (2 types)
Answer
4. Mechanical isolation: Physical incompatibility of reproductive structures — flower shape + pollinator mismatch. 5. Gametic incompatibility: Sperm/pollen physically fails to reach or penetrate egg — species-specific surface proteins prevent fertilization even after mating. Example: sea urchin bindin protein (Strongylocentrotus) — diverges rapidly between species.
🧠 Remember it
The date happens but no baby — (1) mechanical isolation (lock-and-key genital mismatch, e.g., damselfly claspers), (2) gametic isolation (sperm can't fertilize egg, e.g., species-specific bindin proteins in sea urchins).
Mating occurs but a zygote is never formed; these barriers act between copulation and fertilization through physical fit or molecular recognition failure at the gamete surface.
CH13
Postzygotic barriers — 2 types
Answer
6. Hybrid inviability: Hybrid embryo forms but developmental incompatibilities cause death before reproductive age (Bateson-Dobzhansky-Muller incompatibilities). 7. Hybrid sterility: Hybrids survive and are healthy BUT cannot reproduce. Example: MULE (horse 2n=64 × donkey 2n=62 = mule 2n=63). Odd chromosome number → meiosis fails → sterile. Horse + donkey gene pools cannot merge.
🧠 Remember it
A hybrid is born but the line stops there — (1) hybrid inviability (offspring die as embryos/juveniles), (2) hybrid sterility (offspring live but can't reproduce, like the mule from horse x donkey).
The zygote forms but gene flow is still blocked because the hybrid either fails to develop or cannot pass its genes on, making the reproductive barrier functionally complete.
CH13
Bateson-Dobzhansky-Muller (BDM) incompatibilities
Answer
WHAT: Two alleles that evolved independently in two isolated populations are INCOMPATIBLE when combined in a hybrid — cause inviability or sterility. HOW: Allele A₁B₂ works fine; A₂B₁ works fine in isolation. Hybrid A₁B₁ or A₂B₂ combination = developmental failure. WHY happens: Each allele is fine in its own genetic background but epistatic interactions go wrong in the hybrid's novel combination.
🧠 Remember it
Two branches of a software project with forked commits — allele A evolves in one lineage, allele B in the other; each works in its own genome, but when combined in a hybrid they crash because they were never tested together.
BDM solves Darwin's dilemma of how isolation evolves without passing through unfit intermediates: each lineage's novel alleles are fine in their native background, and incompatibilities only appear in hybrids where the untested combination meets.
CH13
Reinforcement — definition, mechanism, result
Answer
WHAT: Natural selection STRENGTHENS prezygotic barriers when populations meet in secondary contact and produce low-fitness hybrids. MECHANISM: Individuals that avoid mating with the other species waste no gametes on failed offspring → higher fitness → alleles for stronger prezygotic barriers spread. RESULT: Prezygotic isolation increases over time. EVIDENCE: More pronounced mating signal differences between sympatric than allopatric populations (reproductive character displacement).
🧠 Remember it
A restraining order after a bad breakup — when diverged populations re-contact and produce unfit hybrids, natural selection strengthens prezygotic isolation (mate preferences, signal differences) to avoid wasted matings.
Selection against hybrids favors individuals that mate only within their own population, producing stronger premating barriers in sympatry than allopatry — a pattern called reproductive character displacement (Pfennig's spadefoot toads).
CH13
Allopatric speciation — steps, examples
Answer
WHAT: Speciation with geographic isolation. STEPS: (1) Geographic barrier forms OR colonization of new area; (2) Gene flow stops; (3) Divergence via drift + selection; (4) Reproductive isolation evolves. EXAMPLES: Darwin's finches (dispersal to Galápagos); Snapping shrimp separated by Panama Isthmus 3.5 mya; Hawaiian Laupala crickets (island-hopping, matches island age sequence).
🧠 Remember it
Geography as a wall — a physical barrier (river, mountain, ocean) splits a population, the two halves diverge independently by drift and selection, and reproductive isolation evolves as a byproduct; Isthmus of Panama snapping shrimp, Grand Canyon antelope squirrels.
Allopatry (different homelands) is the default, easiest speciation mode because zero gene flow lets any divergence accumulate; the Panamanian shrimp pairs split ~3 Mya when the isthmus closed are the textbook case.
CH13
Sympatric speciation — definition, example
Answer
WHAT: New species evolves WITHIN the same geographic area as ancestor WITHOUT geographic isolation. EXAMPLE — Rhagoletis pomonella: Apple maggot fly shifted from native hawthorn to introduced apple trees ~140 years ago. Host plant = (1) mating site (assortative mating by host), (2) different developmental timing (temporal isolation), (3) different selection pressures. KEY: Ecological divergence alone can produce reproductive isolation.
🧠 Remember it
A divorce under the same roof — new species arise within a single interbreeding population without geographic isolation, typically via host shifts or polyploidy; Rhagoletis apple maggot flies (apple vs. hawthorn host races) and cichlids in Lake Apoyo.
Sympatry requires disruptive selection plus assortative mating to overcome homogenizing gene flow; Rhagoletis shows this because host fidelity automatically sorts mates, and polyploidy instantly creates reproductive isolation within the parent range.
CH13
Vicariance vs. dispersal — what, difference
Answer
VICARIANCE: Organisms STAY PUT — a barrier FORMS and splits their range (e.g., Panama Isthmus rising 3.5 mya splits Atlantic/Pacific snapping shrimp populations). DISPERSAL (founder event): A SUBSET of organisms MOVES to a new area (e.g., a hawk flies to a new island and founds a population). KEY distinction: In vicariance, both daughter populations large; in dispersal, founder population small → strong genetic drift.
🧠 Remember it
The wall moves vs. the person walks — vicariance: a geographic barrier rises and splits a formerly continuous range (Gondwana break-up, Isthmus of Panama); dispersal: individuals cross a pre-existing barrier to found a new population (Darwin's finches reaching Galapagos).
Both generate allopatry, but vicariance is driven by the environment partitioning the species while dispersal is driven by the organism crossing into empty territory, predicting different biogeographic patterns.
CH13
Allopolyploidy — instant speciation, how
Answer
WHAT: Hybridization between two species + genome duplication = new allopolyploid species instantly reproductively isolated from both parents. HOW it isolates: Allopolyploid has double the chromosomes → when it tries to cross back with either parent → odd ploidy → meiosis fails → sterile hybrids. FREQUENCY: ~70–80% of flowering plant species are polyploids. EXAMPLE: Bread wheat: hexaploid (6n=42) from 3 ancestral grasses.
🧠 Remember it
Merging two different decks of cards and then photocopying the whole stack — interspecies hybridization followed by genome doubling produces a fertile AABB hybrid that is reproductively isolated from either AA or BB parent in one generation.
The doubled genome pairs normally at meiosis (each chromosome has a homolog), so the hybrid is fertile with its own kind but sterile with parents, creating a new species instantly without gradual divergence.
CH13
Allopolyploidy — real-time examples
Answer
Tragopogon: Two allopolyploid species (T. mirus, T. miscellus) formed in Pacific Northwest within ~80 years after European grasses were introduced — observed speciation in real time. Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum): Emmer wheat (4n) × Aegilops (2n) → 6n hexaploid ~8,000 years ago. WHY plants: Animals rarely tolerate polyploidy because sex determination systems break down.
🧠 Remember it
Species born in human memory — Tragopogon miscellus and T. mirus (salsify) arose in the Pacific Northwest in the 1940s from introduced European parents, and Spartina anglica (cordgrass) arose in Britain ~1890 from S. alterniflora x S. maritima.
These plants are documented cases of new species forming by allopolyploidy within the last ~150 years, providing real-time empirical proof that speciation can happen in a single generation.
CH13
Haldane's Rule
Answer
WHAT: When one sex of hybrid offspring is inviable or sterile, it is almost always the HETEROGAMETIC sex (XY males in mammals; ZW females in birds/butterflies). WHY: Recessive BDM incompatibility alleles on the X (or Z) chromosome are EXPOSED in hemizygous XY males (no second X to mask them). XX females have a backup allele. PREDICTION: BDM incompatibility genes accumulate on sex chromosomes disproportionately.
🧠 Remember it
The heterogametic sex takes the hit — in hybrid crosses, if one sex is absent, rare, sterile, or inviable, it is the heterogametic sex (XY in mammals/Drosophila, ZW in birds/butterflies), so mule males are worse off than mule females.
Haldane (1922) observed this nearly universal pattern in hybrid zones; it arises because recessive X-linked incompatibilities are fully exposed in the hemizygous heterogametic sex but masked in the homogametic sex.
CH13
Parapatric speciation
Answer
WHAT: Populations have PARTIAL geographic separation; gene flow occurs in a narrow contact zone. HOW speciation occurs: Selection gradients stronger than gene flow → divergence despite some hybridization. EXAMPLE: Anthoxanthum odoratum grass populations at mine boundary — populations separated by <1 meter but heavy metal tolerance diverges; different flowering times evolve → temporal isolation.
🧠 Remember it
Parapatric speciation happens along a zipper — two populations occupy adjacent habitats with a narrow contact zone, gene flow continues at the seam, but divergent selection on each side still pulls them apart into two species, like neighborhoods on either side of a busy street growing distinct accents.
With partial (not complete) geographic separation, strong habitat-specific selection overrides limited gene flow; grass populations on mine tailings versus adjacent clean soil diverge in metal tolerance and flowering time, producing reproductive isolation along an environmental gradient without full allopatry.
CH13
Peripatric speciation (founder effect speciation)
Answer
WHAT: Subset of allopatric speciation — a tiny founder population colonizes a new, isolated habitat. KEY: Small founder size → strong genetic drift; new alleles can fix rapidly regardless of fitness in original environment. EXAMPLE: Hawaiian islands — each colonization event = new founder population. Hawaiian honeycreepers: 1 founder species → ~50+ species. WHY rapid: Drift + new selective environment accelerate divergence.
🧠 Remember it
A small breakaway colony on the outskirts — a tiny peripheral population (founder event) becomes isolated, undergoes strong drift and novel selection, and diverges rapidly from the large parent population; Hawaiian Drosophila and Galapagos finches exemplify this.
Mayr's peripatric model emphasizes that small founder populations sample only a fraction of parental genetic variation, letting drift and selection fix new genetic backgrounds (genetic revolutions) far faster than in large continuous ranges.
CH13
Hawaiian Laupala crickets — what they demonstrate
Answer
37 endemic species on Hawaiian archipelago. PHYLOGENY mirrors island age: Oldest populations on oldest island (Kauai ~5 mya), youngest on Big Island (<1 mya). DISPERSAL model confirmed: Colonized sequentially from older to younger islands (not vicariance — islands formed independently). MECHANISM: Each island colonization = new founder population → peripatric speciation. RATE: ~4× faster than insular Drosophila.
🧠 Remember it
The fastest speciation rate on record — ~38 endemic Laupala species across the Hawaiian islands diverging in under 0.43 Myr per speciation event, driven entirely by male song pulse rate and female preference co-evolving.
Laupala show that sexual selection on a single trait (pulse rate) can drive extraordinarily rapid speciation in allopatry, as each new island colonization yields a new species distinguished only by mating signal.
CH13
Ecological speciation — what, why important
Answer
WHAT: Speciation driven directly by natural selection due to adaptation to different ecological environments — no geographic isolation required. HOW: Different ecological niches → different selective regimes → different traits → assortative mating by habitat/resource → reproductive isolation as byproduct. RHAGOLETIS example: Hawthorn vs. apple host plant = different timing, morphology, behavior → mating isolation within same range. WHY important: Shows ecology drives species formation.
🧠 Remember it
Divergent habitats doing the splitting — reproductive isolation evolves as a byproduct of adaptation to different ecological niches, as in Rhagoletis apple vs. hawthorn races, threespine stickleback benthic vs. limnetic pairs, and Timema walking-sticks on different host plants.
It shows natural selection (not just drift or geography) can directly generate species by linking ecological divergence to mate choice; this demonstrates Darwin's original 'origin of species by natural selection' at the genomic level.

CH14
Biogeography — definition
Answer
WHAT: Study of spatial and temporal distribution of biodiversity — WHERE organisms live and WHY. INTEGRATES: Biology, geology, geography, paleontology, evolution. FOUNDED by: Darwin and Wallace (independently, 1850s). KEY TOOLS: Vicariance analysis, dispersal modeling, phylogeography. FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION: Are distribution patterns explained by organisms moving (dispersal) or barriers forming around stationary organisms (vicariance)?
🧠 Remember it
A detective mapping crime scenes across a city — where species live tells you their history, not just their biology.
Biogeography is the study of species distributions in space and time; the spatial pattern is the evidence, and evolutionary/geological history is the cause.
CH14
Continental drift — Pangaea timeline
Answer
WHAT: Earth's continents move (plate tectonics). PANGAEA: Supercontinent assembled ~300 mya, began breaking up ~200 mya. KEY SPLITS: N. America / Eurasia separated ~80 mya; S. America isolated ~65 mya; India-Asia collision ~55 mya (formed Himalayas); Australia separated from Antarctica ~35 mya. WHY matters: Explains disjunct distributions — marsupials in Australia + S. America (both Gondwanan lineages).
🧠 Remember it
A giant puzzle that started whole ~335 Mya, split into two halves (Laurasia north, Gondwana south) around 200 Mya, and finished breaking into today's seven pieces by ~65 Mya.
The puzzle metaphor captures the sequential fragmentation of Pangaea and explains why southern-hemisphere lineages (marsupials, ratites) share ancestry despite ocean gaps.
CH14
Vicariance biogeography — principle
Answer
WHAT: Organisms in a continuous population are SEPARATED by a geological or climatic barrier forming BETWEEN them → two daughter populations. PREDICTION: Phylogeny of separated populations should MATCH the sequence of barrier formation. EXAMPLE: Snapping shrimp (Alpheus): 15+ species pairs separated by Panama Isthmus (formed 3.5 mya) — shrimp species diverged ~3.5 mya. Isthmus age = speciation age.
🧠 Remember it
A river cuts through a farmer's field, splitting one herd of cattle into two separate herds without any animal moving.
In vicariance, a geographic barrier arises and divides a single ancestral range; the organisms stay put while the landscape splits them.
CH14
Dispersal biogeography — principle
Answer
WHAT: Organisms MOVE from their origin to a new area — usually rare 'sweepstakes' events across barriers. EVIDENCE for: Phylogeny shows dispersal direction (e.g., New World monkeys — closest relative in Africa, must have crossed Atlantic ~35 mya on floating vegetation mats). HOW distinguished from vicariance: In dispersal, only 1 lineage crosses; in vicariance, related populations on both sides of barrier.
🧠 Remember it
A coconut floating across the ocean to colonize a new island while the continents stay fixed.
In dispersal, barriers are stable and organisms actively cross them to found new populations, the opposite of vicariance.
CH14
Island biogeography — MacArthur & Wilson equilibrium theory
Answer
THEORY (1967): Species richness on islands is an EQUILIBRIUM between immigration rate and extinction rate. IMMIGRATION: Decreases as species richness increases (fewer new species available to colonize). EXTINCTION: Increases as species richness increases (more competition, smaller populations). EQUILIBRIUM: Where immigration rate = extinction rate. PREDICTION: Larger islands and closer-to-mainland islands have MORE species.
🧠 Remember it
A busy parking lot at equilibrium — cars drive in (immigration) at the same rate cars leave (extinction), so the total number of parked cars stays constant even though individual cars turn over.
MacArthur & Wilson's 1967 model balances two opposing curves — immigration falls as species accumulate, extinction rises as crowding increases — and their crossover sets the equilibrium species richness S*.
CH14
Island biogeography — distance + area effects
Answer
DISTANCE effect: Islands closer to mainland have HIGHER immigration rate → higher equilibrium species richness. Each km of ocean reduces probability of successful colonization. AREA effect: Larger islands support LARGER populations → lower extinction rate; also more habitat diversity → more niches. SPECIES-AREA RELATIONSHIP: S = cA^z (where z ≈ 0.20–0.35 for most taxa). Doubling island area increases species ~15%.
🧠 Remember it
A food truck parked near a stadium (near + large) draws more and keeps more customers than one far out in a tiny parking lot (far + small).
Near islands get more immigrants (distance effect) and large islands have lower extinction (area effect), so near-large islands hold the most species.
CH14
Adaptive radiation — what, conditions, examples
Answer
WHAT: Rapid diversification of one lineage into many ecologically different species — exploiting new resources or habitats. CONDITIONS: (1) Access to new ecological opportunity (after extinction, colonizing new island, key innovation); (2) Low competition from other lineages. EXAMPLES: Darwin's finches (Galápagos — 1 ancestor → 13+ species); Hawaiian honeycreepers (1 ancestor → 50+ species); mammals (post K-Pg — rapid diversification in 10 mya).
🧠 Remember it
A Swiss Army knife unfolding — one ancestor rapidly diversifies into many specialized tools when new ecological opportunity opens.
Radiations happen when a lineage encounters empty niches (new habitat, key innovation, mass extinction); Darwin's finches, Hawaiian silverswords, and cichlids are the textbook cases.
CH14
Wallace Line — what, significance
Answer
WHAT: Biogeographic boundary between Asian and Australasian fauna running between Bali/Lombok and Borneo/Sulawesi (discovered by Alfred Russel Wallace, 1859). WHY persists: Deep ocean trench between islands — persisted even when sea levels dropped 120m during ice ages → never a land bridge. WEST: Asian fauna (primates, deer, tigers, Asian birds). EAST: Australian fauna (marsupials, cockatoos, birds of paradise). SIGNIFICANCE: Demonstrates how water barriers maintain faunal boundaries.
🧠 Remember it
An invisible fence between Bali and Lombok — tigers and monkeys to the west, cockatoos and marsupials to the east, even though the islands are miles apart.
The deep-water strait kept Asian and Australasian faunas separate despite near proximity, showing that geological history (ocean depth during ice ages) trumps present-day distance.
CH14
Species-area relationship (SAR)
Answer
EQUATION: S = cA^z, where S = species richness, A = area, c = taxa-specific constant, z = slope (~0.20–0.35). PRACTICAL USE: Predict species loss from habitat destruction — if 90% of habitat is lost (A reduced to 10%), species richness drops to ~50% (using z=0.30: 0.1^0.3 = 0.5). APPLICATION: Conservation — minimum reserve size to maintain species richness; predicts extinction debt from fragmentation.
🧠 Remember it
Bigger pizzas feed more people — double the area and you get roughly a predictable bump in species, following S = cA^z.
Species richness scales as a power function of area because larger patches hold more habitats and larger populations; z typically ranges 0.2–0.35 for islands.
CH14
Species-area relationship — habitat fragmentation consequences
Answer
WHAT: Habitat fragmentation creates 'islands' of suitable habitat in a 'sea' of unsuitable matrix. CONSEQUENCES: (1) Smaller patches → fewer species (SAR); (2) Reduced immigration between patches → higher extinction; (3) Edge effects increase (altered microclimate at habitat edges); (4) Inbreeding depression in small isolated populations. SOLUTION: Wildlife corridors connect patches → restore immigration → slow extinction.
🧠 Remember it
Breaking one large pizza into ten slivers — total crust area shrinks, and the number of species each sliver can feed plummets.
Fragmentation reduces effective area, and by the SAR this causes extinction debt — many species will be lost even after habitat loss stops.
CH14
Metapopulation dynamics
Answer
WHAT: A population of populations — subpopulations in distinct patches connected by dispersal. KEY DYNAMICS: Local extinction and recolonization are normal. As long as recolonization rate > extinction rate, metapopulation persists even if individual patches go extinct. RESCUE EFFECT: Immigration from other patches can prevent local extinction (genetic + demographic). WHO: Levins (1969). EXAMPLE: Bay checkerspot butterfly (Euphydryas editha bayensis).
🧠 Remember it
A patchwork of habitat fragments is a regional 'population of populations' — some patches wink out like lightbulbs burning, others flicker back on as recolonizers wander in, and the whole grid stays lit only because migration connects them.
Levins' model treats patches as occupied or empty with rates of local extinction and colonization; the metapopulation persists when colonization exceeds extinction, so dispersal corridors are critical for regional survival even when individual patches fail.
CH14
Conservation genetics — 50/500 rule
Answer
WHAT: Rule of thumb for minimum population sizes. 50 rule: Ne ≥ 50 to avoid immediate inbreeding depression (lose ~1% heterozygosity per generation). 500 rule: Ne ≥ 500 to maintain evolutionary potential long-term (mutation input ≈ drift loss). NOTE: Ne (effective) = usually 10–25% of census population N. LIMITATION: Some argue 5,000 needed for long-term viability in changing environments.
🧠 Remember it
A retirement fund rule of thumb — 50 is enough to pay this month's bills (avoid inbreeding), 500 is enough to stay solvent long-term (retain evolutionary potential).
Ne = 50 prevents short-term inbreeding depression; Ne = 500 preserves enough additive genetic variance to respond to selection over evolutionary timescales.
CH14
Inbreeding depression — mechanism
Answer
WHAT: Reduced fitness in offspring of closely related parents. MECHANISM: Inbreeding increases homozygosity → previously masked recessive deleterious alleles become homozygous → expressed as defects. ALSO: Overdominance (heterozygote advantage) lost when alleles become homozygous. EXAMPLES: Florida panther (skull defects, cryptorchidism from inbreeding). FIX: Gene flow from other populations — even one migrant per generation reduces inbreeding depression.
🧠 Remember it
A family secret becoming everyone's problem — when close relatives mate, recessive disease alleles that were hidden in heterozygotes get exposed as homozygotes.
Inbreeding increases homozygosity, unmasking deleterious recessives and reducing heterozygote advantage, so fitness drops in offspring of close kin.
CH14
Area phylogeny — what it is
Answer
WHAT: A cladogram where tips are geographic AREAS (not taxa). Built by replacing taxon names with their geographic locations in a phylogenetic tree. PURPOSE: Detect shared historical biogeographic patterns across multiple lineages — if fish, insects, and plants in region A all show same vicariance pattern relative to region B, it implies a common historical barrier between A and B. USE: Distinguish vicariance (repeated pattern) from dispersal (idiosyncratic pattern).
🧠 Remember it
A family tree where the tips are places, not species — each branch represents the splitting of geographic regions inferred from the branching of their endemic lineages.
By mapping phylogenies onto geography, an area cladogram reveals the sequence of vicariance events that shaped a biota.
CH14
Phylogeography — definition
Answer
WHAT (Avise 1987): Study of principles and processes governing geographic distributions of genealogical lineages within and among closely related species. HOW: Build gene tree from DNA sequences of individuals across their range; map on geography. QUESTIONS: Do different parts of range have different lineages? Did populations expand post-ice age? Where did glacial refugia exist? EXAMPLE: North American trees have S. Appalachian refugia lineages — survived ice ages there.
🧠 Remember it
A genealogy plotted on a map — tracing gene lineages across landscapes to see how populations spread, split, and came back together.
Phylogeography links intraspecific gene trees to geographic distribution, revealing recent history (refugia, expansions, barriers) that deeper phylogenies miss.
CH14
Gondwana vs. Laurasia — what each contained
Answer
GONDWANA (southern Pangaea): South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, India, Madagascar. After breakup: explains why marsupials in S. America + Australia (both Gondwanan); why ratites (ostrich, emu, rhea) on separate southern continents. LAURASIA (northern Pangaea): North America, Europe, Asia. After breakup: explains why N. American + Eurasian mammals closely related (land bridges persisted longer). SPLIT: ~200 mya.
🧠 Remember it
A southern team jersey (Gondwana = South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, India, Madagascar) vs. a northern team jersey (Laurasia = North America, Europe, Asia).
Pangaea split along an east-west seam into these two supercontinents; today's southern biotas (marsupials, ratites, southern beeches) share Gondwanan ancestry, northern biotas share Laurasian ancestry.

CH8 • Humans & Selection Ch 8 • Natural Selection: Empirical Studies

CH8
Humans as selective force — 4 key examples
Answer
1. Trophy hunting (bighorn sheep, African elephants) — selects AGAINST large traits. 2. Fishing (Atlantic cod) — selects for smaller, younger maturation. 3. Antibiotic/pesticide use — selects for resistance. 4. Habitat change (peppered moth industrial melanism) — changes cryptic coloration selection. WHY matters: Humans are the dominant selective force on Earth — evolutionary consequences have practical implications for conservation and medicine.
🧠 Remember it
A plant breeder who never planted a seed — trophy hunting (bighorn horns), poaching (elephant tusks), fishing (cod size), and antibiotics all act as unintended artificial selection.
Whenever humans kill non-randomly based on a heritable trait, we impose strong directional selection — the same mechanism as breeding, just with death instead of breeding choice.
CH8
Trophy hunting evolution — bighorn sheep example
Answer
WHAT: Trophy hunters selectively kill LARGE-horned rams (most desirable trophies). EVOLUTIONARY RESULT: This is artificial selection IN REVERSE — removes genes for large horns from population. DATA: Ram horn size in Ram Mountain, Alberta DECLINED ~20% in 30 years (Coltman et al. 2003). Heritable horn size selected AGAINST by hunting. KEY: Demonstrates evolution is occurring RIGHT NOW under human pressure.
🧠 Remember it
A reverse beauty pageant where the winners get shot — Ram Mountain hunters removed rams with the biggest horns for 30 years, and average horn size shrank ~20%.
Selective removal of large-horned individuals before they reproduced favored small-horned survivors; horn size is heritable, so the population evolved smaller horns.
CH8
Tusklessness in African elephants
Answer
WHAT: In heavily poached populations, proportion of tuskless female elephants has increased dramatically. MECHANISM: Poachers target elephants WITH tusks → tuskless individuals survive and reproduce → tuskless allele increases in frequency. DATA: Gorongosa NP (Mozambique) — proportion of tuskless females rose from ~18% pre-war to ~51% post-civil war (heavy poaching). GENETIC: Tusklessness linked to X chromosome — heterozygous females tuskless; hemizygous males lethal.
🧠 Remember it
Gorongosa elephants that lost their dental jewelry during the civil war — poachers killed tusked elephants for ivory, so tuskless females (rare before, up to 50% after) were the survivors who reproduced.
Ivory poaching was directional selection against the tusk allele; because tusklessness is X-linked dominant and lethal in males, female tusklessness rose rapidly in one generation.
CH8
Antibiotic resistance — how it evolves
Answer
MECHANISM: (1) Random mutations create resistance alleles in bacterial population (pre-exist before antibiotic exposure). (2) Antibiotic kills susceptible cells — resistant cells survive and reproduce. (3) Resistance alleles increase in frequency via selection. (4) Resistant cells may transfer R-plasmids to other cells (horizontal gene transfer). KEY: Antibiotics do NOT cause mutations — they SELECT for pre-existing resistance.
🧠 Remember it
Bacteria running a natural-selection gauntlet — antibiotics don't CAUSE resistance mutations, they just kill off everyone who didn't already happen to have them. Survivors reproduce, population evolves.
The gauntlet framing corrects the common misconception that the drug 'teaches' bacteria; selection acts on pre-existing variation just like Darwin's finches.
CH8
4 mechanisms of antibiotic resistance
Answer
1. Efflux pumps: Protein pumps in cell membrane actively expel antibiotic out of cell. 2. Enzymatic degradation: Enzyme breaks down antibiotic (e.g., β-lactamase destroys penicillin). 3. Target modification: Change the target protein so antibiotic no longer binds (e.g., modified ribosome). 4. Reduced permeability: Alter cell membrane porins to block antibiotic entry.
🧠 Remember it
Four ways a burglar defeats a lock — pump it out (efflux), chew it up (enzymatic degradation, e.g., beta-lactamase), change the lock (target modification), or never let it in (reduced permeability).
These four strategies — efflux, degradation, target alteration, and permeability — cover nearly all molecular resistance phenotypes bacteria deploy against antibiotics.
CH8
Atlantic cod — fishing-driven evolution
Answer
WHAT: Industrial fishing removes largest, oldest cod. EVOLUTIONARY CONSEQUENCE: Selection for SMALLER body size and YOUNGER age at maturity over 30+ years. DATA: Average age at maturation in Atlantic cod declined from ~6 years (1970s) to ~4 years (2000s). Body size declined ~30%. WHY dangerous: Smaller, younger fish = less reproductive output per individual. Even if fishing stopped, evolutionary change persists. EVIDENCE it's evolution: Changes maintained across generations + heritable.
🧠 Remember it
A size-selective vacuum cleaner that sucks up every big fish — after decades of net fishing, cod mature earlier at smaller body sizes.
Fishing gear kills large, late-maturing fish before they spawn, favoring genotypes that mature early and small; this heritable shift is why cod stocks don't recover even after moratoriums.
CH8
Industrial melanism — peppered moth
Answer
WHAT: Biston betularia (peppered moth) exists in light-speckled and dark melanic forms. PRE-INDUSTRIAL: Light form cryptic on lichen-covered trees; dark form rare (conspicuous to birds). INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION: Pollution killed lichens; soot blackened tree bark → dark form now cryptic. Dark form rose to 90%+ in industrial areas by 1900. AFTER CLEAN AIR ACTS: Dark form declined as pollution reduced, lichens returned. GENETICS: Single gene (cortex) controls morph.
🧠 Remember it
Lichen-covered bark turned into coal-black soot — the typica (pale) moth that once hid on clean trees became conspicuous, while the carbonaria (dark) morph now vanished against sooty bark.
Soot from the Industrial Revolution killed lichens and darkened trees, flipping which morph bird predators could see; allele frequency tracked pollution levels up and back down.
CH8
Habitat fragmentation + genetic consequences
Answer
WHAT: Human land use fragments continuous habitat into isolated patches. GENETIC EFFECTS: (1) Reduced effective population size → more genetic drift → loss of heterozygosity; (2) Reduced gene flow between patches → inbreeding depression; (3) Loss of evolutionary potential (fewer alleles). REAL EXAMPLE: Florida panther: severe inbreeding depression (heart defects, cryptorchidism, kinked tail). FIXED by importing Texas pumas — rescued genetic diversity.
🧠 Remember it
A shattered mirror — each shard holds a smaller, more isolated reflection, and over time the pieces drift apart and lose their shine (diversity).
Fragmentation reduces Ne per patch, increasing drift and inbreeding while reducing gene flow; the result is lost heterozygosity and divergence among patches.
CH8
Selective breeding vs. natural selection
Answer
SELECTIVE BREEDING: Humans choose which individuals reproduce based on desired traits → same mechanism as natural selection but humans control who reproduces. RATE: Much faster than natural selection (can select one trait intensely each generation). CONSEQUENCES: Extreme phenotypes with fitness costs (bulldogs: breathing problems; dairy cows: mastitis). KEY: Artificial selection demonstrates evolution is real and fast when selection is strong — same mechanism Darwin used as evidence for natural selection.
🧠 Remember it
A dog show judge picking winners by eye vs. a marathon where only finishers reproduce — both weed out variation, but the judge has a goal and nature just has survival.
Artificial selection has a human-chosen target and is usually faster; natural selection is goalless and filters whatever affects survival/reproduction in the current environment. Mechanism is identical.
CH8
Why fishing-driven evolution is real evolution, not plasticity
Answer
QUESTION: Maybe fish just grow slower because of overfishing (less food = smaller fish = phenotypic plasticity)? EVIDENCE it's GENETIC: (1) Common garden experiments — raised fish from fished vs. unfished populations under IDENTICAL conditions; fished populations still maturate younger and smaller. (2) Changes maintained in captive populations across generations. (3) Heritability studies confirm traits are heritable. LESSON: Must distinguish phenotypic plasticity from genetic evolution.
🧠 Remember it
Common-garden experiments with cod — raise harvested and unharvested lineages in identical tanks and the harvested fish STILL mature early and small.
Because the trait persists in a standardized environment, the change must be heritable (genetic), not a flexible response to conditions — that is the textbook definition of evolution.

CH17 • Human Evolution Ch 17 • Human Evolution: A New Kind of Ape

CH17
Why Linnaeus classified humans as primates
Answer
WHAT: Carolus Linnaeus (1758) placed Homo sapiens in Order Primates in Systema Naturae. CRITERIA used (morphology): Forward-facing eyes, fingernails (not claws), five digits, complex brain, social behavior. SIGNIFICANCE: First formal scientific classification acknowledging human kinship with apes. NOW confirmed by: Molecular data — humans share ~98.8% DNA identity with chimpanzees, ~98.4% with bonobos.
🧠 Remember it
A cataloger who grouped items by anatomy without reading the labels — forward-facing eyes, grasping hands with nails, large brain-to-body ratio, and clavicle put Homo in with the apes long before evolution was accepted.
Linnaeus used shared morphological features (synapomorphies in hindsight) to place humans among primates in 1735, anticipating Darwin by a century based on pure anatomy.
CH17
Molecular evidence of human-chimp kinship
Answer
DNA similarity: 98.8% sequence identity (coding regions). Shared ERVs: Identical endogenous retrovirus insertions at same genomic locations (ERVs = retroviruses that integrated into germline — must share ancestor that was infected). Chromosome 2 fusion: Human chr 2 = fusion of two ancestral chromosomes (still separate in chimps). Protein similarity: Cytochrome c: 100% identical; hemoglobin: 99% identical. CONCLUSION: Shared ancestry is overwhelming.
🧠 Remember it
Two textbooks that share 98–99% of their sentences, including the typos — shared SINEs, pseudogenes, and chromosome 2 fusion (two ape chromosomes fused into one in humans) are copy errors no independent author would produce.
Shared derived molecular 'errors' cannot be explained by common design; they require common ancestry, placing humans and chimps as sister taxa ~6–7 Mya.
CH17
Did humans evolve FROM chimpanzees? Why not?
Answer
NO — common MISCONCEPTION. Humans and chimps share a COMMON ANCESTOR that was NEITHER modern human NOR modern chimp. Chimps have been evolving just as long as humans have. Saying 'humans evolved from chimps' is like saying you descended from your cousin rather than from your shared grandparent. ANALOGY: Humans and chimps are sister lineages — both descended from an unknown ancestral ape ~6–7 mya.
🧠 Remember it
You and your cousin share grandparents, not each other — humans and chimps both descend from a common ancestor that was neither human nor modern chimp.
Both lineages have been evolving independently for ~6–7 My; modern chimps are our sister group, not our ancestors, and they have their own derived traits we lack.
CH17
Sahelanthropus tchadensis — key features
Answer
WHEN: ~7 mya (Chad, Central Africa). WHY important: Possibly EARLIEST known hominin. KEY features: Small canine teeth (reduced like hominins); foramen magnum positioned anteriorly (suggests upright posture); small brain (~360 cc). CONTROVERSY: Some scientists argue it was quadrupedal and not a hominin based on femur morphology. SIGNIFICANCE: If correctly classified = pushes human-chimp split toward 7 mya.
🧠 Remember it
The earliest candidate on the hominin resume (~7 Mya, Chad) — chimp-sized brain (~350 cc) but a foramen magnum centered under the skull hinting at bipedalism.
Near the human-chimp split, Sahelanthropus shows the first possible bipedal signal (foramen position) with otherwise ape-like anatomy — a transitional mosaic.
CH17
Ardipithecus ramidus — key features
Answer
WHEN: ~4.4 mya (Ethiopia). KEY features: Walked upright (bipedal pelvis + femur) BUT retained grasping big toe (could climb trees). Brain ~300–350 cc. Small, ape-like canines. SIGNIFICANCE: Before Lucy — shows early hominins were bipedal BEFORE large brains evolved. Bipedalism preceded encephalization. Overturns 'knuckle-walking ancestor' model.
🧠 Remember it
'Ardi' — a 4.4 Mya woodland walker with a grasping big toe for climbing AND a bipedal pelvis, like wearing hiking boots on one foot and climbing shoes on the other.
Ardi shows bipedalism evolved in a forested (not savanna) setting and coexisted with arboreal adaptations, overturning the classic savanna hypothesis.
CH17
Australopithecus — key facts
Answer
WHEN: ~4.2–1.9 mya (Africa). BRAIN: 420–550 cc (larger than apes, smaller than Homo). BIPEDAL: Yes — Laetoli footprints (3.6 mya, Tanzania) show bipedal gait. ARMS: Long arms + curved fingers — still climbed trees. MOST FAMOUS: 'Lucy' (A. afarensis, 3.2 mya, Ethiopia) — 40% complete skeleton. TEETH: Large molars (herbivorous diet). KEY: BIPEDAL BEFORE LARGE BRAIN — overturns earlier expectation.
🧠 Remember it
'Lucy' (A. afarensis, 3.2 Mya) — a fully bipedal ape from ~3–4 feet tall with a chimp-sized brain (~400 cc) and the Laetoli footprints proving upright walking.
Australopithecines had committed to bipedalism long before brain expansion, directly contradicting the myth that big brains came first.
CH17
Homo erectus — key facts
Answer
WHEN: ~1.9 mya–100,000 ya. BRAIN: 850–1,100 cc. DISTRIBUTION: Africa, Asia (Peking Man, Java Man), possibly Europe — first hominin to leave Africa. TOOLS: Acheulean hand axes. FIRE: Evidence of fire control (Zhoukoudian, China ~400,000 ya). BODY: Fully modern human proportions (long legs adapted for long-distance running). SIGNIFICANCE: Long geographic range; modern locomotion; increasing cognitive capacity.
🧠 Remember it
The first global backpacker (~1.9 Mya–110 kya) — modern-proportioned body, brain ~900 cc, Acheulean hand axes, controlled fire, first hominin out of Africa.
H. erectus is the first hominin with human-like body proportions and the first to leave Africa; long persistence and broad range mark it as a major success story.
CH17
Homo neanderthalensis — key facts
Answer
WHEN: ~400,000–40,000 ya. RANGE: Europe + western Asia. BRAIN: ~1,500 cc — EQUAL TO or larger than modern humans. ADAPTATIONS: Cold climate (compact body, large nasal passages). TOOLS: Mousterian stone tools. CULTURE: Buried dead, used ochre, made jewelry, cared for injured. INTERBREEDING: Had sex with H. sapiens — all non-African modern humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA. EXTINCTION: ~40 kya.
🧠 Remember it
Ice-age cousins (~400–40 kya, Europe/W. Asia) — stockier, cold-adapted, BIGGER brain (~1600 cc) than us, Mousterian tools, buried their dead, interbred with sapiens.
Neanderthals were not 'dumb brutes' — bigger-brained and culturally sophisticated, they contributed ~1–4% of non-African modern human DNA before going extinct.
CH17
Homo sapiens — origin + timeline
Answer
WHEN: ~300,000 ya (anatomically modern). WHERE: Multiple locations in Africa (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco ~315 kya; Florisbad, S. Africa ~259 kya). BEHAVIORALLY MODERN: ~100,000–50,000 ya (symbolic art, complex tools, language). OUT OF AFRICA: ~60,000–70,000 ya major migration wave out of Africa → colonized entire world. BRAIN: ~1,350 cc. KEY: Anatomically modern ≠ behaviorally modern — ~200,000 year gap.
🧠 Remember it
A family that left home late — anatomically modern humans arose in Africa ~300 kya (Jebel Irhoud), behaviorally modern ~70–50 kya, expanded globally after ~60 kya.
The pan-African origin with gradual behavioral modernity (beads, art, projectile weapons) replaced the old 'Eve' replacement model with a more complex structured-population picture.
CH17
Bipedalism — when did it evolve?
Answer
WHEN: Before large brains — ~7 mya (Sahelanthropus) suggests upright posture; Ardipithecus (~4.4 mya) was bipedal with small brain (~300 cc); Laetoli footprints (3.6 mya) = modern bipedal gait. KEY POINT: Bipedalism preceded encephalization by millions of years. Old hypothesis ('brain first, then stand up') was WRONG.
🧠 Remember it
Long before the brain got big — Sahelanthropus (7 Mya), Ardipithecus (4.4 Mya), and Lucy (3.2 Mya) were all bipedal with chimp-sized brains.
The fossil record places bipedalism near the base of the hominin lineage, millions of years before brain enlargement in Homo — order matters for the exam.
CH17
4 anatomical markers of bipedalism
Answer
1. Foramen magnum position: Shifted anterior (bottom of skull → head balanced atop spine, not hanging in front). 2. Pelvis shape: Broad, bowl-shaped ileum (supports organs; provides gluteal muscle attachment for stability). 3. Femur angle: Femur angled inward (knock-kneed) → keeps center of gravity over feet while walking. 4. Foot arch + big toe: Non-opposable big toe; longitudinal arch acts as spring for efficient locomotion.
🧠 Remember it
A bipedal checklist — foramen magnum underneath the skull, S-shaped spine, short/wide pelvis (basin-shaped), valgus knee (angled inward), non-grasping big toe.
Each feature redirects weight down a vertical column: skull balances on top, spine absorbs shock, pelvis supports viscera, knee centers mass, foot forms a propulsive lever.
CH17
Why did bipedalism evolve? 2 main hypotheses
Answer
1. Thermoregulation hypothesis: Upright posture exposes less body surface to direct overhead sun at noon; raised height catches cooling breezes on open savanna. 2. Energetic efficiency hypothesis: Bipedal walking is MORE energetically efficient than chimpanzee knuckle-walking for the same distance — frees hands for carrying food. ADDITIONAL: Freed hands for tool use, throwing, carrying infants. EVIDENCE: Treadmill studies — human walking significantly more efficient than chimp walking.
🧠 Remember it
Hands-free shopping (carrying hypothesis — free hands for food/infants) vs. staying cool (thermoregulation — less solar area, catch the breeze on savanna).
Both hypotheses identify selective advantages of upright posture; neither excludes the other, and the forested Ardi discovery complicated the pure savanna thermoregulation story.
CH17
Bipedalism costs — maladaptations
Answer
WHY costs exist: Bipedal pelvis evolved under conflicting selection: wide enough to birth large-brained babies vs. narrow enough for efficient locomotion. COSTS: (1) Back pain — lumbar lordosis + intervertebral discs poorly designed for vertical load; (2) Difficult childbirth — neonatal head barely fits birth canal → obstetric dilemma; (3) Knee + hip problems — joints bearing full body weight; (4) Varicose veins — blood pumped uphill against gravity.
🧠 Remember it
The price of standing upright — back pain, knee problems, hernias, difficult childbirth (obstetric dilemma), and varicose veins.
Selection favored bipedal benefits despite structural compromises; these ailments are the evolutionary tax on a body plan retrofitted from a quadruped ancestor.
CH17
Hominin tool traditions in chronological order
Answer
1. Oldowan (~2.6 mya): Simple choppers and flakes — Homo habilis. 2. Acheulean (~1.7 mya): Bifacial hand axes — Homo erectus. Required planning: hold 3D shape in mind while knapping. 3. Mousterian (~300–40 kya): Prepared core technique — Neanderthals + archaic H. sapiens. More standardized flakes. 4. Upper Paleolithic (~45–10 kya): Blade tools, needles, art, projectiles — behaviorally modern H. sapiens. TREND: Increasing sophistication + planning depth.
🧠 Remember it
A tech upgrade timeline — Oldowan (flakes, 2.6 Mya, Homo habilis) → Acheulean (hand axes, 1.7 Mya, H. erectus) → Mousterian (prepared cores, 300 kya, Neanderthals) → Upper Paleolithic (blades/art, 50 kya, H. sapiens).
Each industry reflects both cognitive and cultural advances; the progression maps onto expanding brain size and increasing behavioral complexity.
CH17
FOXP2 gene — what it is, why it matters
Answer
WHAT: Transcription factor gene on chromosome 7, regulates ~100+ downstream genes. HUMAN VERSION: 2 amino acid differences vs. chimp; human version under positive selection. FUNCTION: Required for orofacial motor control and language — mutations cause severe speech/language disorders. MYTH: NOT 'the language gene' — it doesn't encode language itself; it encodes fine motor control circuits needed for speech. Found in songbirds, crocodiles too. SIGNIFICANCE: Hints at genetic basis of human speech evolution.
🧠 Remember it
A transcription-factor conductor — it doesn't play the language symphony itself, but coordinates hundreds of downstream genes needed for fine motor control of speech.
FOXP2 mutations (KE family) cause severe speech apraxia; humans have two derived amino acid changes vs. chimps, but FOXP2 is a regulator, not 'the' language gene.
CH17
Out of Africa hypothesis — evidence
Answer
WHAT: All modern humans derive from an African ancestral population that migrated out of Africa ~60–70 kya. EVIDENCE: (1) Genetic diversity HIGHEST in Africa (more time for mutations to accumulate); (2) All major human haplogroups root in Africa; (3) Fossil anatomically modern H. sapiens earliest in Africa; (4) Population structure: African populations basal to all others in phylogeny. COMPETING: Multiregional hypothesis (largely rejected by genetics).
🧠 Remember it
A genetic GPS pointing back to Africa — highest genetic diversity in Africa, all non-African lineages nested within African diversity, serial founder effects with distance from Africa.
mtDNA, Y-chromosome, and genome-wide data all converge on an African origin with expansion ~60 kya; diversity declines with distance because each migration was a bottleneck.
CH17
Neanderthal DNA in modern humans
Answer
HOW MUCH: ~1–4% of the genome in ALL non-African modern humans. PATTERN: Sub-Saharan Africans: essentially 0% Neanderthal DNA. East Asians: slightly more than Europeans. WHERE in genome: Neanderthal DNA absent from X chromosome + gene desert regions (negative selection — BDM incompatibilities). Present in immune genes, skin/hair genes. WHEN interbreeding: Primarily in Middle East ~60 kya as H. sapiens exited Africa and encountered Neanderthals.
🧠 Remember it
A few souvenirs from a fling — non-Africans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA from interbreeding ~50–60 kya in the Middle East.
Introgression was limited but real; some introgressed alleles (immune HLA, skin/hair) were adaptive, others were selected against, and sub-Saharan Africans have near-zero Neanderthal ancestry.
CH17
Denisovans — who, where, legacy
Answer
WHAT: Archaic hominin known primarily from DNA extracted from Denisova Cave, Siberia. WHO THEY ARE: Sister group to Neanderthals; diverged ~400 kya. INTERBREEDING: Denisovan DNA in Melanesians/Pacific Islanders (~3–6%), some mainland Asians. Sub-Saharan Africans and Europeans: essentially 0%. ADAPTIVE GIFT: EPAS1 gene (high-altitude oxygen adaptation in Tibetans) came from Denisovans via introgression.
🧠 Remember it
Ghost cousins known mostly from a pinky bone and a tooth from Denisova Cave (Siberia) — genome reveals a distinct archaic human that interbred with sapiens in Asia/Oceania.
Melanesians/Papuans carry up to 4–6% Denisovan DNA, including the EPAS1 altitude allele passed to Tibetans — a classic case of adaptive introgression from a ghost lineage.
CH17
Adaptive introgression — definition + examples
Answer
WHAT: Gene flow that transfers BENEFICIAL alleles from one species/population to another — alleles spread because they increase fitness. EXAMPLE 1: EPAS1 from Denisovans → Tibetans (oxygen regulation at high altitude). EXAMPLE 2: Neanderthal immune genes (HLA alleles) in non-African humans — beneficial because Neanderthals were already adapted to Eurasian pathogens. WHY important: Interbreeding was not just 'noise' — it transferred locally adapted alleles.
🧠 Remember it
Borrowing a neighbor's already-tested genetic toolkit — EPAS1 from Denisovans to Tibetans for high-altitude oxygen, HLA variants from Neanderthals to non-Africans for immunity.
Introgression = gene flow between lineages via hybridization; when the introduced allele is favored by selection, it can sweep to high frequency in the recipient population.
CH17
Anatomically vs. behaviorally modern H. sapiens
Answer
ANATOMICALLY MODERN: Modern skeleton — rounded skull, small brow ridges, chin, gracile frame. ~300 kya. BEHAVIORALLY MODERN: Symbolic art, beads, long-distance trade networks, complex tools, hafted projectiles, musical instruments. ~100–50 kya (Upper Paleolithic 'revolution'). GAP: ~150,000–200,000 years between anatomical and behavioral modernity. WHY gap? Unknown — possibly language, larger population size enabling cumulative culture, or genetic change.
🧠 Remember it
A computer that has modern hardware at 300 kya but modern software at ~70–50 kya — the brain shape existed before the full suite of symbolic behavior appeared.
Anatomically modern = skeletal traits of today's humans; behaviorally modern = symbolic thought, art, projectile tech, long-distance trade; they do NOT appear simultaneously.
CH17
Cooking hypothesis (Wrangham)
Answer
WHAT: Cooking food was the KEY innovation enabling the reduction of jaw muscles and expansion of brain in Homo. HOW: Cooking breaks down fibers, denatures proteins, gelatinizes starch → MUCH easier to digest → more caloric yield per gram. Less time chewing (chimps chew ~6 h/day; humans ~1 h/day). RESULT: Smaller gut needed (expensive tissue); energy freed for brain. WHEN: Controlled fire evidence ~400 kya; possibly earlier uncontrolled fire use by H. erectus.
🧠 Remember it
Wrangham argues cooking was the spark that lit modern humans — fire turned tough calories into bioavailable energy, shrunk our guts, fed our ballooning brains, and freed social time once we stopped chewing raw food eight hours a day.
Cooking denatures proteins and gelatinizes starches, raising caloric yield; Wrangham ties this dietary shift at ~1.8 Ma to Homo erectus's smaller teeth, smaller gut, and expanded brain, framing fire control as the enabling technology of our genus.
CH17
MHC and human mate choice
Answer
WHAT: Major Histocompatibility Complex — immune system genes on chromosome 6; highly polymorphic. SWEATY T-SHIRT EXPERIMENT (Wedekind 1995): Women rated men's T-shirts by smell — preferred MHC-DISSIMILAR men. WHY: Offspring of MHC-dissimilar parents have more diverse immune repertoire (heterozygote advantage for immunity). SIGNIFICANCE: Humans detect MHC-related mate quality via body odor — evidence for mate quality assessment based on genetic compatibility.
🧠 Remember it
T-shirt smell tests (Wedekind) — women rated MHC-dissimilar men's body odor as more attractive, an unconscious way to avoid inbreeding and boost offspring immune diversity.
MHC heterozygosity improves pathogen resistance, and olfactory preferences for dissimilar MHC provide a mechanism to avoid kin and produce immunocompetent offspring.

CH18 • Evolutionary Medicine Ch 18 • Evolutionary Medicine

CH18
Evolutionary medicine — definition + why it matters
Answer
WHAT: Application of evolutionary principles to understand disease, health, aging, and the human body's design. KEY INSIGHT: The body wasn't designed perfectly — it was shaped by evolution to maximize reproductive success in ancestral environments, often at the cost of health in modern environments. FOUNDERS: Nesse & Williams (1994). POWER: Explains WHY disease exists rather than just HOW — leads to novel therapeutic strategies.
🧠 Remember it
Asking 'why' a body is vulnerable, not just 'how' it breaks — evolutionary medicine applies Darwinian thinking to disease, asking why natural selection left us susceptible.
Proximate (mechanism) explanations are insufficient; ultimate (evolutionary) explanations reveal trade-offs, mismatches, and constraints that inform treatment and prevention.
CH18
Nesse's 6 reasons we get sick
Answer
1. Pathogens evolve faster than we do. 2. Evolutionary mismatch (modern body in unnatural environment). 3. Trade-offs (adaptations for one trait create vulnerabilities elsewhere). 4. Evolutionary constraints (body can't be redesigned from scratch). 5. Antagonistic pleiotropy (genes beneficial early are harmful late). 6. Apparent disease IS an adaptation (fever, vomiting, pain = defenses, not failures).
🧠 Remember it
A six-point defect list — (1) pathogens evolve faster, (2) mismatch, (3) trade-offs, (4) constraints, (5) antagonistic pleiotropy, (6) defenses mistaken for disease.
Nesse and Williams' framework organizes all evolutionary explanations for vulnerability; any given disease usually reflects one or more of these six.
CH18
Reason 1: Pathogens evolve faster
Answer
WHY: Bacteria generation time = ~20 min vs. human ~25 years. Bacteria can evolve 1 million generations in 40 years. Mutation rate per generation also higher. RESULT: Coevolutionary arms race — bacteria can always evolve resistance faster than we can deploy new drugs. EXAMPLE: Antibiotic resistance arises within years of introducing new antibiotic. IMPLICATION: We can never 'win' permanently — must keep developing new antibiotics OR manage resistance strategically.
🧠 Remember it
An arms race where one side reloads in minutes and the other reloads in decades — bacteria double in 20 minutes while humans take 20 years per generation.
Short generation times and huge population sizes let pathogens out-evolve host defenses, explaining why we're chronically behind in the Red Queen race.
CH18
Reason 2: Evolutionary mismatch
Answer
WHAT: Human body evolved in ancestral (Pleistocene) environment; modern environment is RADICALLY different. EXAMPLES: (1) Salt preference evolved when salt was scarce → hypertension in salt-rich modern diet. (2) Fat preference evolved when calories were scarce → obesity epidemic. (3) Sedentary lifestyle → cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes. (4) Vitamin D deficiency in northern latitudes (evolved in Africa). KEY: Body is fit for ancestral environment, not modern one.
🧠 Remember it
Paleolithic software running on a Twinkie — bodies tuned for scarcity now face abundance, leading to obesity, diabetes, myopia, and back pain.
Rapid cultural/environmental change outpaces genetic adaptation; traits adaptive in ancestral environments are maladaptive in modern ones.
CH18
Reason 3: Trade-offs
Answer
WHAT: Natural selection cannot simultaneously maximize ALL traits — improving one character often worsens another. EXAMPLES: (1) Upright bipedalism → back pain + difficult childbirth; (2) Large brain + narrow pelvis → obstetric dilemma (human birth extremely difficult vs. other primates); (3) Sickle cell: HbS/HbA heterozygote resistant to malaria but HbS/HbS has severe anemia. WHY important: There may be NO perfect solution — only trade-off optima.
🧠 Remember it
A sports car that's fast but fragile — high testosterone boosts male reproduction but raises prostate cancer and immunosuppression risk.
Selection optimizes lifetime reproductive success, not health; genes that boost one fitness component often impose costs elsewhere.
CH18
Reason 4: Evolutionary constraints
Answer
WHAT: Evolution cannot redesign the body from scratch — it modifies existing structures. New adaptations must build on existing architecture. EXAMPLES: (1) Human back pain — lumbar spine repurposed from horizontal to vertical orientation; (2) Blind spot in vertebrate eye — retinal nerve fibers exit in front of photoreceptors (cephalopod eyes have no blind spot — evolved independently, correct 'wiring'); (3) Recurrent laryngeal nerve takes ridiculous detour around aorta.
🧠 Remember it
A vertebrate eye wired backwards — photoreceptors behind the blood vessels create a blind spot, but selection can't start over, only tinker.
Path dependency means evolution can only modify what exists; historically contingent solutions (e.g., recurrent laryngeal nerve, appendix) persist even when suboptimal.
CH18
Reason 5: Antagonistic pleiotropy (aging)
Answer
WHAT: Single gene has BENEFICIAL effects early in life (high reproductive value) and HARMFUL effects late in life (low reproductive value). MECHANISM: Selection removes alleles harmful early, but late-acting harmful effects 'invisible' to selection (most individuals already reproduced). EXAMPLE 1: p53 — suppresses cancer growth early BUT high p53 activity accelerates tissue aging. EXAMPLE 2: Testosterone — promotes reproduction early; may promote prostate cancer + cardiovascular risk late. RESULT: Senescence (aging) is inevitable.
🧠 Remember it
A loan with a youth bonus and late-life payment — genes that boost early reproduction at the cost of late-life health are favored because selection is weak after reproduction.
Williams' theory of aging: pleiotropic alleles with early benefits and late costs accumulate because late-life fitness effects are discounted; result is senescence.
CH18
Reason 6: Apparent disease = adaptation
Answer
KEY INSIGHT: Some 'symptoms' are ADAPTATIONS — defenses that increase fitness, NOT failures of the body. FEVER: Elevated temperature inhibits pathogen growth + speeds immune cell activity. Taking fever reducers may prolong infection. VOMITING: Expels toxins/pathogens rapidly. DIARRHEA: Flushes gut pathogens. MORNING SICKNESS: Avoids teratogenic foods during organogenesis. PAIN: Signals tissue damage — necessary for behavior modification. LESSON: Don't suppress symptoms without understanding their function.
🧠 Remember it
A smoke alarm, not a fire — fever, cough, nausea, and anxiety are defenses, not diseases; suppressing them sometimes makes outcomes worse.
Many 'symptoms' are evolved host responses (Nesse's smoke detector principle); blocking them removes protective function unless costs of false alarms exceed benefits.
CH18
Antagonistic pleiotropy vs. mutation accumulation (aging theories)
Answer
ANTAGONISTIC PLEIOTROPY (Williams 1957): Genes with beneficial early effects and harmful late effects — selected positively early, harmful late effects 'ride along.' Predicts some late-acting deleterious alleles should be under POSITIVE selection early. MUTATION ACCUMULATION (Medawar 1952): Deleterious mutations that act late in life escape purifying selection (few individuals survive to old age in nature) → accumulate in population. BOTH operate simultaneously. PREDICTION: Aging is not programmed — it's the cost of early fitness.
🧠 Remember it
Antagonistic pleiotropy is a loan you actively take out in your youth and pay back with interest in old age; mutation accumulation is a drawer of late-acting junk mail that piles up because no one filters it past reproductive age.
Williams (1957) requires one allele with OPPOSING fitness effects across life stages, while Medawar (1952) just needs the selection shadow over post-reproductive years — both explain aging but through different genetic mechanics.
CH18
p53 — antagonistic pleiotropy example
Answer
WHAT: Tumor suppressor protein; triggers apoptosis or cell cycle arrest when DNA damage detected. EARLY BENEFIT: High p53 activity = low cancer risk (kills pre-cancerous cells). LATE COST: High p53 also triggers cell senescence — aging of tissues (stem cells retire permanently). Mice engineered for extra p53 had LOWER cancer but aged FASTER. TRADE-OFF: Cancer suppression vs. tissue aging. Classic antagonistic pleiotropy at the molecular level.
🧠 Remember it
An overzealous security guard — p53 suppresses tumors when young (good) but accelerates stem cell depletion and aging when old (bad).
p53 illustrates AP: the same allele that prevents cancer early in life contributes to tissue aging later, selected because early cancer prevention boosts fitness more than late-life vigor.
CH18
Virulence — definition + trade-off
Answer
WHAT: The harm a pathogen causes to its host. TRADE-OFF: Virulence vs. transmission. Extremely virulent pathogen kills host too fast → no time to transmit. Extremely benign pathogen → doesn't exploit host enough → outcompeted by more virulent strains. EVOLUTIONARY STABLE VIRULENCE: At intermediate virulence level where transmission rate is maximized. KEY: Virulence is NOT just an accident — it's the evolved optimum of this trade-off.
🧠 Remember it
A parasite walking a tightrope — exploit the host too little and you don't transmit, too much and you kill the host before transmitting.
Virulence = host damage caused by a pathogen; transmission-virulence trade-off predicts intermediate virulence, modulated by transmission mode.
CH18
Why cholera maintains high virulence
Answer
BIOLOGY: Vibrio cholerae causes massive diarrhea (10–20L/day) → kills rapidly. WHY STILL VIRULENT? Transmission DOES NOT require mobile host (unlike flu — you need to walk around to spread it). Cholera spreads via contaminated water — host can be incapacitated or dead and bacteria still spread into water supply. PREDICTION: Improve water sanitation → select for less virulent strains (mobile host needed). CONFIRMED: Cholera strains less virulent in clean-water environments.
🧠 Remember it
A sick carrier bed that spreads the disease — waterborne transmission means an immobile, dying patient still contaminates the water supply for others.
When transmission doesn't require host mobility, selection permits higher virulence; clean water infrastructure breaks transmission and selects for less virulent strains (Ewald).
CH18
Antibiotic resistance — evolutionary medicine angle
Answer
CLINICAL INSIGHT: Every antibiotic use = a selection event. Incomplete courses leave intermediate-resistance survivors (more dangerous than full survivors or full kills). STRATEGIES from evo medicine: (1) Antibiotic stewardship — don't use unless necessary; (2) Combination therapy — multiple drugs simultaneously (probability of simultaneous resistance to all = very low); (3) Phage therapy; (4) 'Collateral sensitivity' — resistance to drug A makes bacteria MORE susceptible to drug B.
🧠 Remember it
A treadmill of our own making — every prescription is a selection event, and stockpiling antibiotics in livestock feed is running the treadmill at full speed.
Resistance is not a mystery but a predictable outcome of selection; evolutionary medicine prescribes stewardship, cycling, combination therapy, and reserving last-line drugs.
CH18
Cancer as somatic evolution
Answer
WHAT: Cancer is evolution happening in somatic (body) cells. MECHANISM: Mutations accumulate in somatic cells → mutant cells with growth advantage outcompete normal cells → natural selection within body → clonal expansion → metastasis. DARWINIAN PROPERTIES: Heritable variation (somatic mutations), differential reproduction (faster-dividing cells leave more offspring), natural selection. IMPLICATION: Using cytotoxic chemo = strong selection for resistant cells. Need evolutionary-aware treatment strategies.
🧠 Remember it
Darwin inside your body — tumor cells mutate, compete, and are selected for growth and evasion; metastasis is a range expansion by the fittest clones.
Cancer satisfies all three conditions for evolution (variation, heritability, differential survival) at the cellular level; treatment resistance is evolution in real time.
CH18
Sickle cell — fitness ranking in malaria-endemic Africa
Answer
HbS/HbS (homozygous sickle): LOWEST fitness — severe sickle cell disease, often fatal before reproduction. HbA/HbA (normal homozygote): INTERMEDIATE — susceptible to Plasmodium falciparum malaria. HbA/HbS (heterozygote): HIGHEST fitness — protected from malaria (parasites can't thrive in sickled cells at low O₂) + no severe anemia. TYPE: Heterozygote advantage → balancing selection → both alleles maintained at equilibrium.
🧠 Remember it
A classic heterozygote advantage — AS (carrier) > AA (normal, dies of malaria) > SS (sickle, dies of anemia).
Heterozygotes resist malaria without severe anemia, so HbS is maintained at intermediate frequencies by balancing selection in malarial regions.
CH18
Sickle cell — fitness ranking in NON-malarial regions
Answer
HbA/HbA: HIGHEST fitness — no malaria to worry about, normal blood function. HbA/HbS: INTERMEDIATE — mild sickling episodes under extreme conditions, but no malaria protection benefit. HbS/HbS: LOWEST — severe sickle cell disease without any compensating benefit. RESULT: HbS allele frequency DECLINES over generations in non-malarial populations — natural selection removes it. Currently declining in African-American populations relative to West African origin populations.
🧠 Remember it
Remove malaria and the ranking flips — AA > AS > SS, because there's no malaria benefit to offset anemia costs.
Without the malarial pressure maintaining the heterozygote, the S allele is purely deleterious and slowly declines by selection in non-endemic populations.
CH18
Hygiene hypothesis
Answer
WHAT: Reduced exposure to parasites, bacteria, and pathogens in early childhood increases risk of allergies and autoimmune disease. MECHANISM: Immune system evolved to combat helminths, bacteria, protozoa. In their absence, immune system 'misdirects' inflammatory response toward harmless antigens (pollen, peanuts, own tissues). EVIDENCE: Inverse correlation between parasitic worm prevalence and autoimmune disease prevalence globally. MISMATCH: Over-clean modern environments lack immune-calibrating signals.
🧠 Remember it
An immune system raised in a bubble — without childhood exposure to microbes, the body mistakes dust mites and peanuts for threats.
The modern deficit of immune 'training' by parasites and commensals is associated with rising allergies, asthma, and autoimmunity — a mismatch disease.
CH18
Myxoma virus — evolutionary case study
Answer
WHAT: Deliberately introduced to Australia (1950) to control European rabbits. Initially: 99.8% mortality. WHAT HAPPENED: Within years, both virus AND rabbits evolved. Virus: less virulent strains spread better (rabbit lived longer → more mosquito bites → more transmission). Rabbits: evolved partial resistance. CURRENT: ~50% mortality (vs. 99.8% originally). LESSON: Virulence evolves to transmission-optimizing intermediate. Cannot deploy biocontrol without expecting evolutionary response.
🧠 Remember it
Australian rabbits and myxoma running a virulence race — the virus started at 99% lethal, dropped toward intermediate virulence within years, and the rabbits evolved resistance.
Classic coevolution: selection for transmission favored less lethal strains (dead rabbits don't transmit via mosquitoes) while host resistance evolved simultaneously.
CH18
Mismatch disease examples
Answer
DEFINITION: Diseases caused by a mismatch between the environment humans evolved in (Pleistocene Africa) and the modern environment. EXAMPLES: (1) Type 2 diabetes — fat/sugar storage genes optimal in feast-famine cycle; (2) Cardiovascular disease — salt preference + sedentary lifestyle; (3) Myopia — eyes adapted for distance, not reading 30 cm away; (4) Vitamin D deficiency in high latitudes; (5) Anxiety disorders — stress response evolved for acute threats, not chronic modern stress. TREATMENT: Understand ancestral environment.
🧠 Remember it
A museum of modern afflictions — obesity, type 2 diabetes, myopia, flat feet, wisdom tooth impaction, lower back pain — each reflects a novel environment.
Each condition arises where ancestral adaptations (fat storage, close-focus visual plasticity, unshod feet, large jaws) collide with modern diets, screens, shoes, and soft foods.
CH18
Evolutionary mismatch — back pain
Answer
WHY SO COMMON: Human lumbar spine derived from horizontal ancestral mammal spine. Evolution co-opted a horizontal support structure for vertical load-bearing. DESIGN PROBLEMS: Intervertebral discs bulge under compression (herniation); lumbar lordosis curve puts uneven pressure on vertebrae; gluteal muscles and hip flexors poorly balanced for prolonged sitting. EPIDEMIOLOGY: ~80% of people experience significant back pain — #1 cause of disability globally. LESSON: Evolutionary constraint + mismatch = imperfect design.
🧠 Remember it
A quadrupedal chassis running upright software — the spine evolved as a horizontal beam and was retrofitted into a vertical column, stressing lumbar discs.
Bipedalism is only ~6 My old, not enough for full skeletal redesign; combined with sedentary modern posture, this produces near-universal lower back pain.
CH18
Peto's paradox — cancer resistance in large animals
Answer
WHAT: Large, long-lived animals (whales, elephants) should get more cancer — more cells = more mutations = more cancer. But they DON'T. PARADOX: Cancer rates similar or lower in large animals than small ones. SOLUTION: Large/long-lived lineages evolved BETTER cancer suppression mechanisms. Elephants: 20 copies of p53 gene (humans have 2). Whales: more efficient DNA repair, alternative tumor suppression pathways. SIGNIFICANCE: Evolution solved cancer suppression repeatedly — understand HOW for cancer therapy.
🧠 Remember it
A Costco freezer with fewer fires than a kitchen — elephants and whales have vastly more cells yet don't have vastly more cancer (elephants carry ~20 copies of TP53).
Big, long-lived animals should have more cancer, but they evolved enhanced tumor suppression; Peto's paradox reveals that cancer resistance evolves with body size.
CH18
Conserved gene networks in evolutionary medicine
Answer
INSIGHT: Many disease-causing genes are evolutionarily ancient and conserved — same pathway exists in model organisms. PRACTICAL: Model organisms (C. elegans, Drosophila, mouse) reveal disease mechanism because gene function conserved. EXAMPLES: BRCA1 breast cancer gene homologs in yeast; Huntington's gene (huntingtin) in flies; p53 family in Hydra (1 billion years old). LESSON: Cancer genes are often ancient growth/cell cycle regulators re-purposed; their ancient function matters for therapy design.
🧠 Remember it
The same toolkit, reused across species — insulin signaling, Hox genes, p53 pathways are ancient, so fly and mouse studies illuminate human disease.
Deep homology means model organisms reveal conserved disease mechanisms; evolutionary medicine leverages comparative biology to find drug targets.

TRAP • Trap Cards ⚠ Common Exam Traps

TRAP
TRAP: Using C-14 to date dinosaur fossils
Answer
WRONG. C-14 has a half-life of only 5,730 years — after ~50,000 years virtually ALL C-14 has decayed. Dinosaurs = ~66+ million years old. Must use U-Pb or K-Ar for such ancient material. Students confuse 'radioactive dating' with C-14 specifically.
🧠 Remember it
A stopwatch that only counts the last 50,000 seconds being used to time the Ice Age — C-14's half-life is 5,730 years, useless for 65-million-year-old dinosaurs.
C-14 is exhausted after ~10 half-lives (~50 ky); dinosaurs require long-half-life isotopes like U-Pb or K-Ar in associated volcanic rocks.
TRAP
TRAP: 'Reptiles' are a valid taxonomic group
Answer
NO — 'Reptiles' (lizards + snakes + turtles + crocs, excluding birds) = PARAPHYLETIC. Excludes birds, which ARE avian theropod dinosaurs. Crocodilians are more closely related to birds than to lizards. Correct monophyletic group = Reptilia INCLUDING birds.
🧠 Remember it
A pizza with a slice missing — 'Reptilia' excludes birds even though birds are nested inside Archosauria, making the traditional group paraphyletic.
Valid clades are monophyletic (ancestor + all descendants); Sauropsida (includes birds) is monophyletic, 'reptiles' in the old sense is not.
TRAP
TRAP: K-Pg was the largest mass extinction
Answer
NO. The PERMIAN extinction (~252 mya) was the largest = ~96% of species lost ('The Great Dying'). K-Pg (~66 mya) killed ~76% of species — catastrophic but smaller. Permian = #1, K-Pg = #3. Students confuse fame (K-Pg killed dinosaurs) with severity.
🧠 Remember it
The dinosaur extinction gets the movie poster, but the Permian-Triassic (~252 Mya) was the real apocalypse — ~96% marine species lost.
K-Pg killed ~75% of species (and non-avian dinosaurs), but the P-Tr 'Great Dying' was far more severe; K-Pg is famous, not largest.
TRAP
TRAP: Antibiotics cause bacteria to mutate
Answer
NO — antibiotics SELECT for pre-existing resistance mutations; they do NOT cause mutations. Mutations occur randomly due to replication errors — they pre-exist antibiotic exposure. Antibiotics simply kill susceptible cells, leaving resistant ones to reproduce. Directed mutation = Lamarckism (wrong).
🧠 Remember it
A gauntlet, not a classroom — antibiotics don't teach bacteria to mutate; mutations already exist, and the drug just kills the non-resistant ones.
Resistance mutations arise spontaneously (Luria-Delbrück fluctuation test proved this in 1943); antibiotics select for pre-existing variation, they don't induce it.
TRAP
TRAP: Humans evolved from chimpanzees
Answer
NO — humans and chimps share a COMMON ANCESTOR ~6–7 mya that was neither modern human nor modern chimp. Both lineages have been evolving since the split. Saying 'humans evolved from chimps' = saying you descended from your cousin. Chimps have evolved just as long as we have.
🧠 Remember it
Cousins with shared grandparents — humans and chimps both descend from a common ancestor ~6–7 Mya, not from each other.
Modern chimps are a sister lineage that has been evolving just as long as we have; calling them our ancestors confuses sister taxa with ancestral taxa.
TRAP
TRAP: Bipedalism evolved AFTER large brains
Answer
WRONG. BIPEDALISM FIRST (~7 mya Sahelanthropus, ~4.4 mya Ardipithecus). Large brains came MUCH LATER (~2 mya with Homo). Australopithecus: fully bipedal with only 420–550 cc brain (barely larger than chimp). The old 'brain first' hypothesis was disproven by fossil evidence.
🧠 Remember it
Lucy walked upright with a chimp-sized brain — bipedalism at ~4 Mya, brain expansion only after ~2 Mya in Homo.
Fossils (Sahelanthropus, Ardipithecus, Australopithecus) all show bipedal anatomy with small brains; brain size explodes millions of years later.
TRAP
TRAP: BSC works for all organisms
Answer
BSC (Mayr) FAILS for: (1) Fossils — cannot test gene flow in extinct taxa; (2) Asexual species — bacteria/archaea/many plants/fungi don't interbreed sexually; (3) Hybridizing species — many plant species produce fertile hybrids across species boundaries. BSC applies only to sexual species with living representatives.
🧠 Remember it
A relationship-status rule that only works on people who date — the Biological Species Concept needs sexual reproduction, so asexuals, fossils, and ring species break it.
BSC defines species by reproductive isolation, which requires sexual reproduction and living individuals; morphological, phylogenetic, or ecological concepts are needed for the rest.
TRAP
TRAP: Mule is an example of hybrid inviability
Answer
NO — mule is HYBRID STERILITY. Mule is a healthy, long-lived, strong animal (survives into 30s). It is STERILE because 63 chromosomes cannot pair in meiosis. Hybrid inviability = hybrid dies before reproductive age. Mule = survives but cannot reproduce = sterility, not inviability.
🧠 Remember it
A mule is alive and kicking — it's hybrid STERILITY (postzygotic), not inviability (which would mean the zygote dies).
Hybrid inviability = offspring doesn't survive; hybrid sterility = offspring survives but can't reproduce. Mules are healthy but sterile due to chromosomal mismatch between horse (2n=64) and donkey (2n=62).
TRAP
TRAP: Island biogeography equilibrium never changes
Answer
WRONG. The equilibrium SPECIES RICHNESS changes dynamically — it is an equilibrium of RATES not a fixed value. As island size or distance from mainland changes, equilibrium shifts. Turnover continues at equilibrium — species going extinct are replaced by immigrants. Total number stable, but COMPOSITION changes constantly.
🧠 Remember it
A bathtub with the same water level but a constant turnover — equilibrium species NUMBER stays roughly constant while species IDENTITY keeps changing.
Equilibrium is dynamic: immigration and extinction continue, just at equal rates; predictions include turnover, not frozen composition.
TRAP
TRAP: Virulence always evolves lower over time

TRAP
TRAP: Global species diversity = immigration + extinction
Answer
NO. Global diversity ≠ island diversity. Island biogeography: diversity = balance of IMMIGRATION from mainland minus local EXTINCTION. But GLOBAL diversity = speciation rate minus global extinction rate. No 'mainland' exists globally — new species can only come from speciation. Immigration applies at local/regional scale.
🧠 Remember it
Islands receive immigrants from a mainland pool, but the GLOBAL pool has no mainland — global diversity is speciation minus extinction, not immigration.
MacArthur-Wilson equilibrium is ISLAND-scale. At a global scale, immigration isn't a source (there's nowhere to immigrate from); diversity is governed by origination minus extinction.
TRAP
TRAP: In vicariance, organisms MOVE; in dispersal, barriers FORM
Answer
REVERSED. VICARIANCE: organisms STAY PUT, a BARRIER forms between them (e.g., Isthmus of Panama separates Atlantic/Pacific populations). DISPERSAL: organisms MOVE across an existing barrier to colonize new territory. Easy memory: Vicariance = 'Victimized by geography' (barrier forms around them); Dispersal = 'organisms Do the Dispersing.'
🧠 Remember it
Vicariance = 'earthquake splits your neighborhood' (you stay, the ground cracks beneath you); dispersal = 'kids move out to a new city' (you move, the map doesn't change).
The reversal is the trap — in vicariance organisms stay put while the landscape shifts (Isthmus of Panama, continental rifting), and in dispersal organisms cross an already-existing barrier (founders reaching an island).

Answer

TRAP
TRAP: Antagonistic pleiotropy = one gene with two pleiotropic effects
Answer
REQUIRES SPECIFIC TIMING. Antagonistic pleiotropy specifically means a gene is BENEFICIAL early in life (during peak reproduction) and HARMFUL late in life (post-reproduction). This is why selection doesn't remove it — early benefit outweighs late cost. Simply having two pleiotropic effects (both bad, or both good, or both at same age) is NOT antagonistic pleiotropy.
🧠 Remember it
Not just two effects — it's two OPPOSING fitness effects at different life stages (early benefit, late cost).
Pleiotropy alone = one gene, multiple traits. Antagonistic pleiotropy requires the effects to be in OPPOSITE fitness directions across age, which is what drives its role in senescence.
TRAP
TRAP: Synapomorphies = any shared character
Answer
NO — synapomorphies = SHARED DERIVED characters. There are 4 types: (1) Synapomorphy: shared derived (defines clades); (2) Symplesiomorphy: shared ANCESTRAL (doesn't define clades — e.g., 'has bones' = ancestral for all vertebrates); (3) Autapomorphy: unique derived character of one taxon only; (4) Homoplasy: similar character in unrelated groups (NOT synapomorphy). Only synapomorphies are phylogenetically informative.
🧠 Remember it
A shared DERIVED character (new in the common ancestor of the group), not just any shared trait — the latter are synapomorphies OR symplesiomorphies.
Synapomorphies are shared DERIVED characters that define clades; shared ancestral characters (symplesiomorphies) are uninformative for grouping.

TRAP: Allopolyploidy requires geographic isolation
Answer
NO — allopolyploidy produces INSTANT reproductive isolation within one generation WITHOUT any geographic isolation. The new allopolyploid is immediately reproductively isolated from both parent species (different ploidy → meiosis failure when crossed back). This is WHY it's classified as a form of sympatric speciation. No geographic isolation step needed.
🧠 Remember it
Polyploid speciation creates its own reproductive isolation via chromosome doubling — no mountain range needed.
Allopolyploidy = hybridization + genome doubling between two species in sympatry; it produces instant reproductive isolation (sympatric speciation), not allopatric speciation.

TRAP: Sickle cell heterozygote fitness in malaria regions
Answer
HbA/HbS (sickle cell CARRIER) has the HIGHEST fitness in malaria-endemic regions — NOT HbA/HbA. This is HETEROZYGOTE ADVANTAGE (overdominance). HbA/HbA: susceptible to malaria. 🧠 Remember it
Heterozygote advantage at the sickle locus — the carrier (HbA/HbS) wins the three-way race in malaria zones, not the normal homozygote, because they get partial malaria resistance without severe anemia.
In malaria-endemic Africa the fitness ranking is AS > AA > SS — the carrier is protected from Plasmodium falciparum while avoiding disease, which is exactly why the sickle allele persists via balancing selection.
TRAP
TRAP: Cancer is not evolution
Answer
Cancer IS evolution — Darwinian evolution in somatic cells. Cancer cells have: heritable variation (somatic mutations), differential reproduction (some cells divide faster), natural selection (fastest-growing clones dominate). This is WHY cancer is so hard to cure — applying one drug = strong selection for resistant cells. Evolutionary-aware treatment strategies (e.g., 'adaptive therapy') use this insight.
🧠 Remember it
A tumor is a Galápagos in your tissue — variation (mutation), heritability (cell division), and differential survival (clonal selection) all satisfy Darwin's criteria.
Somatic cells in a tumor meet all requirements for evolution by natural selection; drug resistance in cancer is literal evolution in real time.

TRAP: Denisovan DNA = same distribution as Neanderthal DNA
Answer
DIFFERENT distributions. NEANDERTHAL DNA: found in ALL non-African modern humans (~1–4%) — Eurasians, Asians, Pacific Islanders, Native Americans. DENISOVAN DNA: mainly MELANESIANS and Pacific Islanders (~3–6%) + some mainland Asians. Notably ABSENT in sub-Saharan Africans AND in most Europeans. Different geographic interbreeding zones — Denisovans were in eastern Asia/Southeast Asia when H. sapiens arrived.
🧠 Remember it
Two separate inheritance tracks — Neanderthal DNA is the 1–4% souvenir all non-Africans carry from Middle East contact, while Denisovan DNA is a 3–6% signature concentrated in Melanesians and Oceanians from a separate Asian introgression event.
The trap assumes archaic admixture was one event, but Neanderthal and Denisovan crosses happened at different times in different regions, producing geographically distinct distributions in modern human genomes.
