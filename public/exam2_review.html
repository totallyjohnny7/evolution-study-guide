Ch 10 Complex Adaptations & Evo Devo

How do complex traits evolve? Molecular mechanisms of morphological change.

10.1 — Evolution of Complex Traits (Vertebrate Eye)

What it is
Complex traits evolve GRADUALLY via many small mutations, each providing a slight fitness advantage. No single mutation produces a fully-formed complex organ. Darwin himself addressed the eye: every intermediate step from a light-sensitive patch to a camera eye is an improvement over having no light detection. Each step is favored by selection.
Key Evo Devo Concepts
  1. Regulatory networks: Mutations in genes that CONTROL other genes (transcription factors, enhancers) produce large morphological changes from small genetic changes
  2. Protein promiscuity: Proteins can adopt NEW functions while retaining old ones — allows novel traits to evolve from existing proteins
  3. Heterochrony: Changes in TIMING or RATE of gene expression (earlier/later, faster/slower) produce new phenotypes without changing the genes themselves
  4. Hox genes: Master regulatory genes that define body segment IDENTITY along the anterior-posterior axis. Highly conserved across animals (Drosophila and mice use the same Hox genes). Changes in Hox expression = major body plan changes
  5. Pax6: Master regulator of eye development. Found in animals as diverse as flies and humans — shows conserved genetic toolkit for eye development despite very different final eye structures
⚠ Trap — Complex Traits Are NOT Perfect

Evolution produces traits that are “good enough” but not optimal. The human eye has a BLIND SPOT (where the optic nerve attaches, no photoreceptors) — an engineering flaw that the octopus eye avoids because octopus eyes evolved independently. Complex traits are constrained by historical contingency (what existed before). Selection cannot design from scratch.

The thing to remember is:

Evo Devo = evolution of REGULATORY machinery is more important than evolution of coding sequences. Same genes (e.g., Pax6, Hox) control development across vastly different organisms = deep conservation. Change WHERE and WHEN genes are expressed, not necessarily the genes themselves.

Q1
The fact that Pax6 gene activates eye development in both Drosophila (flies) and mice, despite these species having very different eye structures, BEST demonstrates:
✅ B. Pax6 is a MASTER REGULATORY GENE conserved across bilaterian animals. The same gene triggers eye development in flies (compound eye) and mice (camera eye) — very different end structures produced by the same upstream trigger. This shows the conserved developmental toolkit: the 'switch' (Pax6) is ancient and shared, but what the switch turns on (downstream targets) varies between lineages.
Q2
Heterochrony in evolutionary developmental biology refers to:
✅ B. Heterochrony = hetero (different) + chronos (time). Changes in WHEN developmental events occur. Example: neoteny (retaining juvenile features into adulthood). Paedomorphosis in axolotls: retain larval (juvenile) features permanently due to heterochrony in thyroid hormone signaling. Same genes, different timing = radically different phenotype.

10.2 — Homology vs. Analogy in Complex Traits

What it is
Homologous traits = similar structures INHERITED from a common ancestor (same developmental origin). May differ in function. Analogous traits (convergence) = similar in function but evolved INDEPENDENTLY in separate lineages (different developmental origins). Identifying the correct relationship is critical for phylogenetics.

Q3
The wings of bats and insects are BOTH used for flight. Regarding their evolutionary relationship, which is CORRECT?
✅ B. Bat wings and insect wings are ANALOGOUS (convergent). They evolved independently. Bat wings are modified tetrapod forelimbs (bone + membrane). Insect wings are outgrowths of the thoracic wall (exoskeleton-derived). Completely different developmental origins. Same function (flight) produced by natural selection in separate lineages. If the same gene pathway (e.g., wingless/Wnt) is involved, it would be parallel, but the structures themselves are non-homologous.

Ch 11 Sexual Reproduction & Sexual Selection

Why sex? The paradox of the twofold cost and its solutions.

11.1 — The Paradox of Sex & Its Benefits

What it is
Sexual reproduction has a twofold cost: in an asexual population, EVERY individual reproduces. In a sexual population, only ~half (females) produce offspring. An asexual mutant in a sexual population would double in frequency each generation. Despite this massive disadvantage, sex persists in almost all multicellular organisms. WHY?
Benefit of SexMechanismKey Example
Combines beneficial mutationsRecombination can put two beneficial mutations in the same genome (without recombination, competing beneficial mutations must wait — Clonal Interference)E. coli experiments: sexual populations adapt faster when multiple beneficial mutations are available simultaneously
Muller's RatchetAsexual populations accumulate deleterious mutations irreversibly (ratchet clicks only forward). Recombination can reassemble low-mutation genomes.Bacterial pathogens using sexual recombination are more evolvable than obligate asexuals
Reduces sibling competitionAsexual offspring are genetically identical — compete for same niche. Sexual offspring are genetically diverse — occupy different nichesGenetically diverse litters compete less intensely than clones
Red Queen HypothesisParasites evolve to exploit COMMON host genotypes. Sex generates rare genotypes that parasites can't easily exploitNew Zealand snails (Potamopyrgus): sexual populations persist where parasites are common; asexual populations take over where parasites are rare
Red Queen Hypothesis — Why Sex Persists
⚠ Trap — Twofold Cost of Sex

In a SEXUAL population, males don't directly produce offspring. A population that is 50% male, 50% female has only HALF as many individuals producing offspring as an all-female asexual population of the same size. An asexual mutant in a sexual population doubles in frequency every generation. This is the twofold cost (also called the “cost of males”). The benefits of sex must OUTWEIGH this 2× disadvantage to be maintained.

The thing to remember is:

Muller's Ratchet: asexual lineages irreversibly accumulate mutations (ratchet clicks only one direction — more mutations). Red Queen: sex is an evolutionary arms race against parasites — constant genotype shuffling prevents parasites from “locking on.” Both are about generating variation faster than asexuals can.

Q4
In a New Zealand lake, snails in parasite-rich habitats are mostly sexual, while snails in parasite-free habitats are mostly asexual. This pattern BEST supports:
✅ B. This is the key study supporting the Red Queen hypothesis. Where parasites are COMMON: common genotypes are exploited → sexual reproduction generates rare genotypes → sex is advantageous → sexual individuals persist. Where parasites are RARE: no frequency-dependent advantage to rare genotypes → asexual reproduction (no twofold cost) wins. The geographic pattern exactly matches what Red Queen predicts.
Q5
Muller's Ratchet describes the disadvantage of asexual reproduction because:
✅ D. Muller's Ratchet has two related consequences: (1) it's impossible to reassemble a low-mutation genome without recombination (the “ratchet” only clicks toward more mutations), AND (2) beneficial mutations that occur in different individuals in an asexual lineage cannot be combined — they must compete (clonal interference). Sex solves both by recombination, which can remove deleterious mutations AND combine beneficial ones.

11.2 — Sexual Selection & Anisogamy

What it is
Sexual selection = selection arising from differential MATING success. Anisogamy = production of differently-sized gametes (large eggs vs. small sperm). Females invest MORE per gamete (large, few eggs) → more selective. Males invest LESS per gamete (many small sperm) → compete for access to females. This creates the Bateman gradient and sexual dimorphism.
FemalesMales
GametesFew, large, energetically costly eggsMany, small, cheap sperm
Limiting resourceQuality mates with good genes / resourcesAccess to females (mate number)
StrategyCHOOSY — discriminate; assess mate qualityCOMPETE — for access to as many females as possible
Sexual selection typeIntersexual: prefers traits in males (mate choice)Intrasexual: competes with other males (male-male competition)
ResultMales evolve elaborate displays/traitsSexual dimorphism — males often larger, showier
⚠ Trap — Sexual Selection vs. Natural Selection

Sexual selection can favor traits that REDUCE survival (e.g., peacock's tail — attracts predators) if they sufficiently increase MATING success. Natural selection maximizes SURVIVAL; sexual selection maximizes MATINGS. These can conflict. The peacock's tail is maintained because the mating advantage outweighs the predation cost.

⚠ Trap — Anisogamy Is the Root Cause

All of sexual selection — female choosiness, male competition, sexual dimorphism — traces back to ANISOGAMY (unequal gamete sizes). It is NOT an arbitrary social pattern. It's a fundamental asymmetry: females invest more per gamete, so their time/energy is the limiting resource. Males “compete” for access to that limiting resource.

Q6
A species of fish has females that produce millions of tiny eggs and males that produce far fewer, larger sperm with additional nutrients. According to anisogamy theory, which prediction is MOST likely correct?
✅ A. This is a REVERSAL of standard anisogamy predictions — when males invest MORE per gamete (fewer, larger, nutrient-rich sperm), the standard Bateman gradient is flipped. Males become the limiting resource → females compete for access to males, and males become choosy. This is called sex-role reversal. The example tests whether you understand that choosiness follows from INVESTMENT per gamete, not from sex per se.
Q7
Sexual dimorphism (males having elaborate traits like peacock tails that reduce survival) persists because:
✅ C. Sexual dimorphism reflects the balance between SEXUAL selection (favors elaborate traits → increases matings) and NATURAL selection (disfavors elaborate traits → reduces survival). The peacock's tail persists because the increase in mating success is GREATER than the cost in reduced survival. Zahavi's handicap principle: the tail's costliness is actually the point — only genuinely high-quality males can afford to survive with such a burden.

Ch 12 Life History Evolution

Trade-offs between growth, reproduction, and survival. When to be born. How many babies. How long to live.

12.1 — Life History Trade-offs

What it is
Life history = the schedule of an organism's survival, growth, and reproduction across its lifespan. Energy/time are finite → allocation to one function REDUCES available for others. Key trade-offs: current reproduction vs. future survival; offspring number vs. offspring size; age at first reproduction vs. lifespan.
Life History Trade-off: Reproduction vs. Survival
Key Predictors of Life History Strategy
  1. Extrinsic mortality (predation risk): HIGH predation → early reproduction (may not survive long enough to reproduce later) → shorter lifespan, more offspring per bout. LOW predation → delay reproduction, invest in growth and survival
  2. Guppy experiment (Reznick): Guppies from high-predation streams evolved earlier maturation, smaller body size at maturity, more offspring per litter compared to low-predation streams. ~30 generations for detectable evolution.
  3. r/K selection continuum: r-selected = fast life, many small offspring (unstable environments). K-selected = slow life, few large offspring (stable, near carrying capacity).
⚠ Trap — Guppy Experiment Duration

Reznick's guppy experiment showed detectable evolutionary change in life history traits in approximately ~30 generations (not 18, not 100). Guppies were transplanted from high-predation to low-predation streams and evolved LATER maturation, LARGER body at maturity, FEWER offspring — in just ~30 generations. This demonstrates rapid evolution of life history traits under changed selective pressure.

⚠ Trap — Current vs. Future Reproduction

High current reproduction REDUCES future survival. Classic example: opossum experiment (Austad) — opossums on predator-free island evolved SLOWER aging (longer lifespan, delayed senescence) compared to mainland opossums with high predation. Less predation = more future to invest in = delayed reproduction possible = slower aging.

Q8
Guppies transplanted from high-predation streams to low-predation streams evolved over ~30 generations. Which life history changes occurred?
✅ C. In low-predation environments: guppies can SURVIVE to reproduce multiple times → invest more in growth and survival → evolve LATER maturation, LARGER body at maturity, FEWER but larger offspring per litter. This is the K-selected direction. In high-predation: evolve EARLY maturation (reproduce before being eaten), smaller body, more offspring. Reznick's famous experiment directly demonstrated this in ~30 generations.
Q9
Which factor MOST strongly predicts whether a species will have a 'fast' vs. 'slow' life history?
✅ C. Extrinsic mortality (predation risk, disease) is the strongest predictor. HIGH extrinsic mortality → individuals often die before reproducing multiple times → selection favors EARLY reproduction and many offspring per bout (fast life). LOW extrinsic mortality → individuals survive long enough to reproduce many times → selection can favor LATER reproduction, fewer offspring per bout, more investment in each offspring (slow life).

12.2 — Aging: Mutation Accumulation & Antagonistic Pleiotropy

Why do we age?
Aging (senescence) persists despite natural selection, which usually removes deleterious traits. Two evolutionary explanations:

Core ideaDeleterious mutations with late-acting effects accumulate because selection cannot “see” them (most organisms die of predation/accident before old age)Genes that BENEFIT early reproduction may HARM survival later — selection maintains them for early benefits even at the cost of late-life damage

Q10
An experiment removes predators from a population of mice for many generations. Evolutionary theory predicts the mice would evolve:
✅ C. This is the KEY life history test. Without predators, mice LIVE LONG ENOUGH to suffer late-life fitness costs. Now selection can “see” late-acting deleterious mutations and antagonistically pleiotropic genes. Over generations, these are removed/modified → slower aging. Austad's opossum study: island opossums (low predation) evolved slower aging than mainland opossums (high predation) in just 4,000 years.

Ch 15 Coevolution

Species evolving in response to each other — arms races, mimicry, and mutualism.

15.1 — Coevolutionary Arms Races & Geographic Mosaic

What it is
Coevolution = reciprocal evolutionary change between interacting species. Each species acts as a selective agent on the other. An arms race is directional escalation: one species evolves better offense, the other evolves better defense, driving each to ever more extreme traits.
Newt-Garter Snake Arms Race
Taricha newts (Pacific Coast) produce tetrodotoxin (TTX) — one of the most potent neurotoxins known. Garter snakes (Thamnophis sirtalis) have evolved voltage-gated sodium channel mutations that confer TTX resistance. Cost: slower locomotion in resistant snakes. Geography: some populations of snakes are highly resistant (live with toxic newts), others have little resistance (no toxic newts). Classic geographic mosaic.
Geographic Mosaic Theory (Thompson)
Coevolution varies across landscapes: Hotspots = locations where both species are present and reciprocal selection is intense (e.g., highly toxic newts + highly resistant snakes). Coldspots = locations where the interaction is weak or one partner is absent. Gene flow between hotspots and coldspots creates a geographic mosaic of traits.
⚠ Trap — Arms Race vs. Red Queen

Arms race = DIRECTIONAL ESCALATION (both species evolve ever more extreme traits over time). Red Queen = FREQUENCY-DEPENDENT CYCLING (common genotypes are exploited; rare genotypes gain advantage; no directional escalation — traits cycle). Both are coevolutionary dynamics but in different directions. Newt-snake = arms race (escalation). Host-parasite common genotype cycling = Red Queen.

Q11
In the garter snake-newt coevolutionary arms race, snakes with high TTX resistance have slower locomotion speed. This illustrates:
✅ A. The TTX-resistance mutation in the snake's sodium channel confers a BENEFIT (survives eating toxic newts) and a COST (slower movement from slightly impaired muscle function). This is a fitness trade-off or antagonistic pleiotropic effect of the same genetic change. It's not A-P in the aging sense but it is a single mutation with two fitness consequences. Also illustrates why the arms race doesn't escalate to infinity — costs constrain how extreme traits can become.
Q12
A population of garter snakes lives far from any toxic newts. According to the Geographic Mosaic Theory, what do you expect for their TTX resistance?
✅ B. Geographic Mosaic Theory predicts COLDSPOTS where the coevolutionary interaction is absent. Without selection pressure (no toxic newts), AND with a cost to resistance (slower locomotion), resistance alleles should be at LOW frequency. This is what's observed: newt-free areas → low-resistance snakes. Hotspots (coexisting toxic newts) → high-resistance snakes. Geographic variation in trait expression = the mosaic.

15.2 — Mimicry & Endosymbiosis

Mimicry Types
Batesian mimicry: A palatable (harmless) species mimics an unpalatable (toxic/dangerous) species. The mimic “free rides” on the model's reputation. Works ONLY when mimics are RARE (if mimics become common, predators learn the signal is unreliable).

Müllerian mimicry: Two or more UNPALATABLE species all converge on the same warning pattern. Both species benefit because predators learn the warning signal faster when it's common. Cooperation in defense. Heliconius butterflies = classic example.
Endosymbiosis
Mitochondria and chloroplasts originated as FREE-LIVING BACTERIA engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells (~1.5-2 bya for mitochondria). Evidence: (1) circular DNA like bacteria, (2) double membranes, (3) 70S ribosomes (bacterial type — inhibited by bacterial antibiotics), (4) binary fission (divide like bacteria), (5) phylogeny places them within bacteria. Lynn Margulis proposed the endosymbiotic theory (initially rejected, now well-established).
⚠ Trap — Batesian vs. Müllerian Mimicry

Batesian: mimic is PALATABLE (harmless), model is UNPALATABLE. Mimic is “lying.” Works best when mimics are RARE (honest warning signal maintained). Müllerian: BOTH species are unpalatable. BOTH benefit. Works better when COMMON (shared signal learned faster). On exams: “harmless species mimics harmful” = Batesian. “Two harmful species look alike” = Müllerian.

Q13
Heliconius butterfly species in the Neotropics are all toxic and share the same striking warning color patterns within each region. This is an example of:
✅ B. Müllerian mimicry: all species involved are UNPALATABLE (toxic). By sharing warning patterns, predator learning about one species benefits ALL species with that pattern. The more common the pattern, the faster predators learn to avoid it. Both species benefit — mutualistic mimicry. Batesian mimicry requires one palatable mimic and one unpalatable model (not applicable when all are toxic).
Q14
Which evidence MOST directly supports the endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria from ancient bacteria?
✅ B. The definitive evidence package for endosymbiotic origin: (1) circular DNA = bacterial-style genome, (2) binary fission = bacterial-style division, (3) 70S ribosomes = bacterial-type ribosomes sensitive to bacterial antibiotics (chloramphenicol, erythromycin) but NOT eukaryotic antibiotics (cycloheximide). Option A is WRONG — mitochondria have DOUBLE membranes (inner from original bacterium, outer from engulfment vesicle).

Ch 16 Evolution of Social Behavior

Cooperation vs. selfishness. Kin selection. The math of altruism.

16.1 — Evolutionarily Stable Strategy (ESS)

What it is
An ESS (Maynard Smith & Price, 1973) is a strategy that, once adopted by most members of a population, CANNOT be invaded by any alternative rare mutant strategy. ESS is a GAME THEORETIC concept: payoffs depend on what others are doing (frequency-dependent). The most stable strategy is not necessarily the one that maximizes individual fitness in isolation.
Hawk-Dove Game
Two strategies: Hawk (escalate fight until injury) and Dove (display, retreat if opponent escalates). If resource V > injury cost C: pure Hawk ESS (aggression pays). If V < C: MIXED ESS (H frequency = V/C; no pure strategy is stable). At ESS frequency, both strategies have equal fitness. Side-blotched lizards: Rock-Paper-Scissors ESS with 3 male throat-color strategies (orange dominates blue, blue dominates yellow, yellow dominates orange).
⚠ Trap — ESS ≠ Optimal for Every Individual

An ESS is the strategy that RESISTS INVASION by mutants — not necessarily the strategy with highest absolute fitness for every individual. The mixed Hawk-Dove ESS has both strategies with EQUAL fitness at equilibrium (otherwise one would increase). If you understand that ESS fitness is frequency-dependent (payoff depends on what others play), you understand why no single strategy dominates in mixed ESS scenarios.

Q15
In the Hawk-Dove game, when the value of the resource (V) is LESS than the cost of injury (C), the ESS is:
✅ C. When V < C (risk of injury outweighs resource value): pure Hawk isn't stable (if everyone is a Hawk, injuries are frequent and costly). Pure Dove isn't stable (a Hawk mutant always wins against all Doves). The ESS is a MIXED strategy: proportion of Hawks = V/C. At this frequency, Hawk and Dove have EQUAL fitness → no selection for either to increase → stable equilibrium.

16.2 — Kin Selection, Altruism & Hamilton's Rule

What it is
Altruism (biological) = behavior that REDUCES the actor's direct fitness while INCREASING the recipient's direct fitness. Paradoxical under individual selection. Explained by kin selection: altruism can be favored if the INDIRECT fitness gains (through enhanced reproduction of relatives sharing your genes) exceed the direct fitness cost.
Hamilton's Rule: rB > C
Key Relatedness Values
  1. Full sibling (diploid): r = 0.5
  2. Parent to offspring: r = 0.5
  3. Half-sibling: r = 0.25
  4. First cousin: r = 0.125
  5. In HAPLODIPLOIDY (Hymenoptera — bees, wasps, ants): full sisters r = 0.75 (!), brothers r = 0.25, mother r = 0.5
Inclusive Fitness = Direct + Indirect Fitness
Total fitness = direct fitness (own reproduction) + indirect fitness (reproduction of relatives, weighted by relatedness). Altruism “works” genetically because your relatives share your alleles — helping them reproduce propagates copies of your alleles.
⚠ Trap — Worker-to-Queen's-Son Relatedness (r = 0.25)

In haplodiploidy: worker bees are related to their FULL SISTERS at r = 0.75. But workers are related to the QUEEN'S SONS at r = 0.25 (not 0.75). Sons are haploid, from unfertilized eggs. Workers share the queen's genome but NOT the father's — they're only related to brothers through the queen (r = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25). This is why worker bees prefer raising SISTERS over BROTHERS if they can control sex ratio.

⚠ Trap — Only B is Discounted by r, NOT C

In Hamilton's Rule rB > C: you discount the BENEFIT by relatedness (how much of the benefit goes to shared alleles). The COST is NOT discounted — you always pay 100% of your own cost. If you incorrectly discounted C by r too, you'd overestimate when altruism should evolve.

Mnemonic — Hamilton's Rule

Haldane quip: “I would lay down my life for 2 brothers or 8 cousins” = 2 × r(0.5) = 1.0 = same as saving yourself. 8 × r(0.125) = 1.0. Break even = worth it; more = favored by kin selection.

Q16
An altruistic act costs an individual 3 offspring (C=3) and benefits a full sibling (r=0.5) by 10 offspring (B=10). Is this act favored by kin selection?
✅ B. Apply Hamilton's Rule: rB > C? rB = 0.5 × 10 = 5. C = 3. 5 > 3 = YES, this act IS favored. The altruist loses 3 units of direct fitness but gains 5 units of indirect fitness (through their sibling's enhanced reproduction, weighted by r). Net inclusive fitness change = +2. The altruist's alleles come out ahead.
Q17
In a haplodiploid (Hymenoptera) bee colony, a worker bee is related to her full SISTERS at r=0.75. What is her relatedness to her mother (the queen)?
✅ B. In haplodiploidy: worker's relatedness to QUEEN (mother) = 0.5 (same as normal diploid parent-offspring). The extraordinary relatedness is to SISTERS (r = 0.75) because: sisters share ALL of the father's genome (father is haploid → all sperm identical) plus half of mother's genome. This asymmetry (sisters r=0.75 vs. own offspring r=0.5) is what theoretically makes raising sisters MORE genetically profitable than having your own children.
Q18
Group selection is generally considered WEAKER than individual selection in most scenarios because:
✅ B. The fundamental problem with group selection: even if groups of cooperators outcompete groups of selfish individuals, WITHIN each cooperating group, selfish mutants have higher individual fitness than cooperators. Selfish individuals spread WITHIN the group → eventually all cooperators converted → group fails. The within-group selection against altruism is typically stronger than between-group selection favoring it. Kin selection “solves” this by making the group also be relatives.

Ch 10 • Evo Ch 10 • Evo Devo

CH10
Evo Devo — what is it?
Answer
Evolutionary Developmental Biology — the study of how evolutionary changes in developmental processes produce morphological diversity. Merges molecular genetics with evolutionary biology.
CH10
Why are regulatory mutations more important than coding mutations?
Answer
Changing a gene's EXPRESSION (when/where/how much it's made) can produce large morphological changes without altering protein structure. The same gene toolkit (Hox, Pax6) produces radically different body plans by changing REGULATION.
CH10
Hox genes — what do they do?
Answer
Master regulatory genes that define body segment IDENTITY along the anterior-posterior axis. Highly conserved across bilaterian animals (insects and mice use the same Hox genes). Changes in Hox expression → major body plan evolution (e.g., leg number, segment identity).
CH10
Pax6 — what does it demonstrate?
Answer
Master regulator of eye development, conserved across animals with radically different eyes (insects, vertebrates). Demonstrates the CONSERVED DEVELOPMENTAL TOOLKIT: same upstream switch, different downstream targets produce different eye types. Ancient deep conservation of genetic modules.
CH10
Protein promiscuity — definition
Answer
A protein that originally evolved for one function can adopt NEW functions while retaining the old one. Allows new traits to evolve from pre-existing proteins. Example: crystallins (eye lens proteins) recruited from metabolic enzymes.
CH10
Heterochrony — definition + example
Answer
Change in TIMING or RATE of gene expression during development. Same genes, different timing = different phenotype. Example: neoteny (retention of juvenile features in adults) — axolotl salamanders remain aquatic and larval-looking due to heterochrony in thyroid signaling.
CH10
Why is the human eye 'imperfect'?
Answer
Blind spot where the optic nerve exits (no photoreceptors). Retinal vessels run on the FRONT of the retina (inverted). The octopus eye solved both problems independently — different evolutionary history, no blind spot. Illustrates historical constraint in trait evolution.
CH10
Homology definition
Answer
Structural similarity due to SHARED ANCESTRY (inherited from common ancestor). May differ in function. Evidence of common descent. Example: human arm + bat wing + whale flipper — same bones (humerus, radius, ulna), different functions.
CH10
Analogy (convergence) definition
Answer
Structural similarity due to INDEPENDENT EVOLUTION for same function. Different developmental origins. NOT evidence of common ancestry. Example: bat wing vs. insect wing — both fly, completely different structure and developmental origin.

Ch 11 • Sexu Ch 11 • Sexual Reproduction

CH11
Twofold cost of sex
Answer
In a sexual population, ~50% are males who don't produce offspring directly. An asexual mutant doubles in frequency every generation. Sexual reproduction must confer benefits > 2× disadvantage to persist.
CH11
Red Queen hypothesis for sex
Answer
Parasites/pathogens evolve to exploit COMMON host genotypes. Sexual reproduction constantly generates RARE genotype combinations that parasites haven't caught up with. Rare genotypes have higher fitness → sex maintains rare genotypes → advantage to sex in parasite-rich environments.
CH11
Muller's Ratchet — what is it?
Answer
Asexual lineages irreversibly accumulate deleterious mutations. The genotype with FEWEST mutations can only be LOST, never restored, without recombination. Like a ratchet clicking only forward (toward more mutations). Sex allows recombination to reconstruct low-mutation genomes.
CH11
Combining beneficial mutations — why sex helps
Answer
In asexual populations, two beneficial mutations that occur in DIFFERENT individuals compete (Clonal Interference). In sexual populations, recombination can PUT BOTH in the same offspring genome. Sex speeds adaptation when multiple beneficial mutations are segregating simultaneously.
CH11
Anisogamy — definition and consequences
Answer
Unequal gamete sizes: large, costly eggs (females) vs. small, cheap sperm (males). Creates asymmetry: females invest more per gamete → female reproductive rate is the LIMITING RESOURCE → males compete for females, females choose males. Drives sexual dimorphism.
CH11
Sexual selection — two types
Answer
Intersexual (mate choice): females choose males with preferred traits → males evolve elaborate displays. Intrasexual (male-male competition): males compete directly for access to females → males evolve larger size, weapons.
CH11
Why can sexual selection produce 'costly' male traits?
Answer
Mating success advantage (sexual selection) can outweigh survival cost (natural selection). Peacock tail: reduces survival but increases mating success enough to be maintained. Zahavi's Handicap Principle: costly signals are HONEST — only high-quality males can afford the cost.
CH11
Sperm competition — what is it?
Answer
Competition between sperm from different males to fertilize the same egg. Occurs when females mate with multiple males. Drives evolution of: larger testes (more sperm), faster sperm, sperm plugs (blocking competitors). Intensifies where female polyandry is common.
CH11
Sexual conflict — what is it?
Answer
Males and females have DIFFERENT optimal reproductive strategies. What's optimal for one sex reduces the fitness of the other. Example: male Drosophila seminal proteins increase female egg-laying rate (male benefit) but reduce female lifespan (female cost). Arms race between sexes.

Ch 12 • Life Ch 12 • Life History

CH12
Life history trade-off — fundamental cause
Answer
Energy and time are FINITE. Allocation to one function (growth, current reproduction, survival) REDUCES availability for others. Trade-offs are universal — no organism can maximize everything simultaneously.
CH12
High extrinsic mortality → what life history?
Answer
Favors FAST life history: early reproduction (may not survive to reproduce later), more offspring per bout, less investment per offspring. Selected because organisms may die before getting another chance to reproduce.
CH12
Low extrinsic mortality → what life history?
Answer
Favors SLOW life history: later reproduction, fewer offspring per bout, more investment per offspring (K-selected). When survival is likely, investing in growth and survival enables better future reproduction.
CH12
Guppy experiment (Reznick) — key result
Answer
Guppies transplanted from high- to low-predation streams evolved LATER maturation, LARGER body size at maturity, FEWER offspring per litter in ~30 generations. Demonstrated rapid evolutionary change in life history traits under altered selective pressure.
CH12
Mutation accumulation theory of aging
Answer
Deleterious mutations with LATE-ACTING effects accumulate because selection cannot see them — most organisms die of predation/accident before old age. Late-acting harmful alleles are nearly neutral from selection's perspective; they pile up over evolutionary time.
CH12
Antagonistic pleiotropy theory of aging
Answer
A gene that BENEFITS early reproduction can HARM survival later. Selection maintains it for the early benefit even though it causes late-life harm. Example: testosterone (early mating success → late prostate cancer). The gene has two effects in opposite directions at different life stages.
CH12
r-selection vs. K-selection
Answer
r-selected: many small offspring, early reproduction, short lifespan, unstable environments (e.g., insects, weeds, oysters). K-selected: few large offspring, late reproduction, long lifespan, stable environments near carrying capacity (e.g., elephants, humans). Most organisms fall somewhere in between.
CH12
Kiwi egg — significance
Answer
Kiwi (bird) lays an egg ~15-20% of the female's body mass — largest egg-to-body ratio of any bird. Extreme K-selected investment in single offspring quality. Illustrates how evolution can push life history traits to extremes when ecological conditions favor it.
CH12
Opossum experiment (Austad) — result
Answer
Island opossums (low predation → long lives possible) evolved SLOWER aging (later onset of senescence, longer lifespan) compared to mainland opossums (high predation → die young regardless). In just ~4,000 years. Selection against late-acting deleterious alleles was possible when individuals survived long enough to express them.

Ch 15 • Coev Ch 15 • Coevolution

CH15
Coevolution — definition
Answer
Reciprocal evolutionary change between two or more species that are ecologically interacting. Each species acts as a selective agent on the other. Can be antagonistic (arms race) or mutualistic (cooperative escalation).
CH15
Coevolutionary arms race — definition
Answer
Directional escalation: one species evolves better offense → other evolves better defense → repeat. Both species continuously evolve more extreme traits. Example: newt-garter snake TTX arms race. Escalation is limited by COSTS of extreme traits.
CH15
Newt-garter snake system
Answer
Taricha newts produce tetrodotoxin (TTX). Thamnophis sirtalis snakes evolved TTX-resistant voltage-gated Na⁺ channel mutations. Resistance has a COST (slower locomotion). Different populations show different levels of toxicity/resistance — geographic mosaic theory.
CH15
Geographic Mosaic Theory (Thompson)
Answer
Coevolution varies across landscapes. Hotspots = both species present, reciprocal selection intense. Coldspots = weak/absent interaction. Gene flow between hotspots and coldspots creates geographic variation in coevolved traits. No global escalation — patchwork of local coevolutionary states.
CH15
Batesian mimicry
Answer
Palatable (harmless) mimic resembles unpalatable (toxic) model. Mimic 'free rides' on model's predator avoidance. Works best when mimics are RARE (if too common, predators learn the signal is unreliable and no longer avoid it).
CH15
Müllerian mimicry
Answer
Two or more UNPALATABLE species all resemble each other. BOTH benefit from sharing the warning pattern — predators learn ONE pattern and avoid ALL species sharing it. Works better when COMMON (more encounters = faster learning). Heliconius butterflies = textbook example.
CH15
Endosymbiotic theory — evidence
Answer
Mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from engulfed bacteria. Evidence: (1) circular DNA like bacteria, (2) double membranes, (3) 70S ribosomes (bacterial type, inhibited by bacterial antibiotics), (4) binary fission, (5) phylogenetics places them inside bacterial clades. Proposed by Lynn Margulis.
CH15
Red Queen in host-parasite coevolution
Answer
Parasites evolve to exploit COMMON host genotypes (frequency-dependent selection). Common host genotypes become disadvantaged → rare genotypes increase → were common → parasites track them → oscillating frequency cycles. Different from arms race: cycling, not escalation.
CH15
Arms race vs. Red Queen dynamics
Answer
Arms race = DIRECTIONAL ESCALATION (traits become ever more extreme). Red Queen = FREQUENCY-DEPENDENT CYCLING (no net escalation; rare types favored, then become common, then disfavored). Newt-snake = arms race. Host-parasite genotype tracking = Red Queen.

Ch 16 • Soci Ch 16 • Social Behavior

CH16
ESS — definition
Answer
Evolutionarily Stable Strategy: a strategy that, when adopted by most of the population, CANNOT be invaded by a rare mutant strategy. Game-theoretic concept (Maynard Smith & Price 1973). Fitness payoffs are FREQUENCY-DEPENDENT.
CH16
Hawk-Dove game — when is ESS mixed?
Answer
When V (resource value) < C (injury cost): pure Hawk not stable (too many injuries when all play Hawk). Pure Dove not stable (Hawks invade). Mixed ESS at frequency of Hawks = V/C. At this equilibrium, both strategies have equal fitness.
CH16
Side-blotched lizards — why famous?
Answer
Three male throat-color morphs (orange, blue, yellow) form a Rock-Paper-Scissors ESS. Orange (aggressive, many mates) beats Blue (territorial). Blue beats Yellow (sneaker strategy). Yellow beats Orange. Frequency-dependent cycling maintains all three strategies.
CH16
Altruism — biological definition
Answer
Behavior that REDUCES the actor's direct fitness while INCREASING the recipient's direct fitness. Does NOT require conscious intent. Defined purely by fitness consequences.
CH16
Hamilton's Rule
Answer
rB > C. r = relatedness (fraction of alleles shared by descent). B = benefit to recipient (extra offspring). C = cost to actor (lost offspring). If rB > C: altruistic act is favored by kin selection. ONLY B is multiplied by r (not C).
CH16
Why only B is multiplied by r (not C)
Answer
The actor always pays 100% of the cost — it's to THEMSELVES (r = 1 to yourself). Only the BENEFIT needs to be discounted by r to account for how much of the benefit reaches shared alleles. Discounting C by r would undercount actual cost.
CH16
Inclusive fitness — definition
Answer
Total fitness = Direct fitness (own reproduction) + Indirect fitness (reproduction of relatives, weighted by relatedness r). Kin selection maximizes inclusive fitness, not just personal direct reproduction.
CH16
Haplodiploidy and eusociality
Answer
In haplodiploid Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, ants): females develop from fertilized eggs (diploid); males from unfertilized eggs (haploid). Full sisters share ALL of father's genes + half of mother's genes = r = 0.75. More related to sisters than own offspring (r = 0.5). Facilitates evolution of female worker castes, but is NOT sufficient alone (many haplodiploid species are NOT eusocial).
CH16
Worker-to-queen's-son relatedness
Answer
Workers are related to their full SISTERS at r = 0.75 (haplodiploidy). But related to the QUEEN'S SONS (their brothers) at r = 0.25 only. Workers share queen's genes but not father's genes through brothers (brothers are haploid from unfertilized eggs). This asymmetry explains why workers prefer raising sisters.
CH16
Group selection — why it's usually weak
Answer
Within-group selection consistently favors SELFISH individuals — they have higher fitness than cooperators within the same group. Even if cooperative groups do better than selfish groups, selfish mutants spread WITHIN cooperative groups. Within-group individual selection usually overcomes between-group selection.
CH16
Meerkat sentinel behavior — kin selection
Answer
Sentinels stand guard and alarm-call while family members forage (costly: lost foraging time, predation risk). The group consists largely of close relatives. By Hamilton's Rule, indirect fitness gained through protecting kin exceeds the direct cost. Classic kin-selected altruism.

⚠ Exam 2 Tra ⚠ Exam 2 Traps

TRAP
TRAP: Twofold cost of sex
Answer
In sexual populations, ~50% are males who don't directly produce offspring. An asexual mutant would DOUBLE in frequency each generation. Sex must confer benefits EXCEEDING 2× disadvantage. The twofold cost is paid every generation.
TRAP
TRAP: Red Queen vs. Muller's Ratchet
Answer
Red Queen = sex helps by generating rare genotypes that escape parasites (frequency-dependent, parasite-driven). Muller's Ratchet = sex helps by reversing mutation accumulation in asexual lineages (mutation-driven). DIFFERENT mechanisms, both benefits of sex.
TRAP
TRAP: Guppy experiment — number of generations
Answer
~30 GENERATIONS for detectable life history evolution in Reznick's guppy study. Not 18, not 100. Guppies transplanted from high-predation to low-predation streams evolved later maturation, larger body size, fewer offspring in approximately 30 generations.
TRAP
TRAP: Only B is multiplied by r in Hamilton's Rule
Answer
rB > C. ONLY B (benefit to recipient) is multiplied by relatedness r. C (cost to actor) is NOT multiplied by r — you always pay 100% of your own cost. Discounting C by r too would overpredict when altruism should evolve.
TRAP
TRAP: Worker-to-queen's-son relatedness
Answer
Workers are related to full SISTERS at r = 0.75 (haplodiploidy). But related to the queen's SONS (brothers) at r = 0.25 (NOT 0.75). Brothers are haploid from unfertilized eggs — workers only share the queen's genes with brothers, not the father's genes.
TRAP
TRAP: Batesian vs. Müllerian mimicry
Answer
Batesian = palatable MIMIC of unpalatable model. Works when RARE. Müllerian = BOTH species are unpalatable, share warning signal. Works when COMMON. 'Harmless imitates harmful' = Batesian. 'Two harmful species look alike' = Müllerian.
TRAP
TRAP: Mitochondria have single membrane?
Answer
NO. Mitochondria have DOUBLE membranes: inner membrane (from original bacterium) and outer membrane (from the engulfing vesicle). Double membranes = evidence of endosymbiosis. Also 70S ribosomes (bacterial, not 80S eukaryotic), circular DNA, binary fission.
TRAP
TRAP: ESS = highest fitness strategy for individual?
Answer
NO. ESS = strategy that cannot be INVADED by rare mutant. At mixed ESS equilibrium, both strategies have EQUAL fitness. ESS is a stability criterion (resistant to invasion), not an optimality criterion (highest absolute fitness).
TRAP
TRAP: Haplodiploidy is sufficient for eusociality?
Answer
NO. Haplodiploidy increases relatedness among sisters (r=0.75) and facilitates kin-selected worker behavior, but it is neither NECESSARY (termites and naked mole rats are eusocial but diploid) nor SUFFICIENT (many haplodiploid species are NOT eusocial). Other factors (ecological constraints, nest defense) are also necessary.
TRAP
TRAP: Natural selection maintains sexual dimorphism because males are 'better'?
Answer
NO. Sexual dimorphism (elaborate male traits) persists because MATING SUCCESS ADVANTAGE exceeds SURVIVAL COST for that trait. It's not about 'better' — it's about the balance between sexual selection (mating success) and natural selection (survival). The handicap can be a NET BENEFIT only because of the mating advantage.
TRAP
TRAP: Group selection is equivalent in power to individual selection?
Answer
NO. Group selection is generally WEAKER because selfish individuals consistently have higher fitness than cooperators WITHIN groups, even if cooperative groups outperform selfish groups. Within-group individual selection usually overrides between-group selection.
TRAP
TRAP: Antagonistic pleiotropy = a gene with two functions?
Answer
AP is more specific: a gene (or allele) that BENEFITS fitness EARLY in life and HARMS fitness LATER in life. The early benefit causes selection to MAINTAIN the allele despite late-life costs. Example: testosterone, p53. Not just any gene with two effects — specifically early benefit + late cost.
