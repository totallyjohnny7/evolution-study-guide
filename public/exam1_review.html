Unit I Evolutionary Mechanisms

The five forces that change allele frequencies — the engine of evolution.

Natural Selection

What it is
Natural selection = differential survival and reproduction of individuals based on heritable traits. The NON-RANDOM mechanism of evolution. Requires 3 conditions: (1) phenotypic variation, (2) variation is heritable (genetic), (3) variation affects fitness (differential survival/reproduction).
Why it matters
Natural selection is the ONLY mechanism that consistently produces adaptation. All other forces (drift, mutation, gene flow) are either random or directionless. Selection specifically favors traits that improve reproductive success in the current environment.
Key formulas
  1. Breeder's Equation: R = h² × S (Response = heritability × selection differential)
  2. Selection Differential (S) = mean of breeders − mean of total population
  3. Heritability (h²) = VA / VP = additive genetic variance / total phenotypic variance
  4. If h² = 0 OR S = 0 → R = 0 → NO evolutionary change (selection without heritability = no evolution)
Breeder's Equation: Response to Selection
⚠ Trap — Selection Without Heritability ≠ Evolution

Natural selection CAN act on a trait without causing EVOLUTION if the variation is not heritable. Example: all tall plants grew in good soil (environment), not because of genes. Selection culls them, but allele frequencies don't change. R = h² × S: if h² = 0, then R = 0 regardless of how strong selection is.

⚠ Trap — Selection is Non-Random, Not Deterministic

Selection is non-random (favors higher fitness genotypes) but outcomes are not guaranteed. In small populations, drift can overpower selection even for beneficial alleles. “Evolution is not completely random” is the key phrase — selection is directional but the environment + alleles determine direction.

The thing to remember is:

Selection acts on PHENOTYPES, but evolution occurs in ALLELE FREQUENCIES. The bridge is heritability (h²). No heritability = selection cannot move allele frequencies = no evolution.

Mnemonic

“V-F-H-P: Variation, Fitness differences, Heritable, Predictable change” = the 4 conditions that guarantee natural selection produces evolution.

Q1
A population of mice is culled: larger mice are selectively removed. However, ALL of the size variation in this population is due to differences in food availability (not genetics). What happens to mean body size in the next generation?
✅ B. This is the Selection Without Heritability trap. R = h² × S. If all variation is environmental (h² = 0), then R = 0 regardless of selection differential. Selection is occurring (larger mice die) but allele frequencies don't change because the size differences aren't genetic. No evolution.
Q2
The selection differential (S) in an artificial selection experiment is LARGEST when:
✅ C. S = mean of breeders − mean of total population. S is maximized when the BREEDERS are most different (extreme) from the whole population. Heritability (B) determines how much of S translates to R, but S itself is purely about how different breeders are from the population.
Q3
Why does a rare beneficial recessive allele increase in frequency slowly even though natural selection favors it?
✅ B. When a recessive allele is rare, nearly all copies occur in Aa heterozygotes — where the dominant allele MASKS the recessive phenotype. Selection can only act on the expressed phenotype. Since Aa looks like AA, selection can't distinguish them and can't favor the recessive allele efficiently. Only when the allele becomes common enough that aa homozygotes appear in significant numbers does selection become fully effective.

Genetic Drift

What it is
Genetic drift = random fluctuation of allele frequencies due to chance sampling. Strongest in small populations. Can cause evolution even when selection is absent. Results in: (1) reduced genetic variation, (2) non-adaptive allele frequency changes, (3) fixation or loss of alleles by chance.
Why it matters
In small populations, drift OVERWHELMS natural selection — a beneficial allele can be lost by chance, a deleterious allele can fix. This is why conservation of small populations (Ne) is critical. Two types of extreme drift: Bottleneck Effect and Founder Effect.
Bottleneck EffectFounder Effect

Genetic Drift vs. Natural Selection — Population Size Effect
⚠ Trap — Drift vs. Selection

Drift is RANDOM — it can increase OR decrease ANY allele's frequency regardless of fitness. Selection is NON-RANDOM — it consistently favors higher-fitness genotypes. In a SMALL population, drift can overwhelm selection and fix a deleterious allele or lose a beneficial one. In a LARGE population, selection dominates. Population size determines which force wins.

The thing to remember is:

Drift = nonadaptive evolution. It can cause fixation of deleterious alleles in small populations — this is why genetic diversity matters for conservation. Drift reduces genetic variation; selection uses it.

Q4
The Amish community in Lancaster, PA has an unusually high frequency of Ellis-van Creveld syndrome (a recessive condition). This is BEST explained by:
✅ C. This is the textbook founder effect example. A small founding group of ~200 settlers in the 1700s happened to include several carriers of the EVC allele. Because the community remained genetically isolated, this high allele frequency was maintained and expressed. No selection FOR the allele — it's purely a historical accident of who founded the colony.
Q5
What is the key difference between the Bottleneck Effect and Founder Effect?
✅ B. Both are extreme genetic drift events. Bottleneck = existing population dramatically shrinks (then may recover, but with reduced diversity). Founder Effect = small group LEAVES and starts new isolated population elsewhere (e.g., island colonization). Both result in reduced genetic variation and potentially non-representative allele frequencies compared to the original large population.

Gene Flow & Mutation

Gene Flow
Gene flow = movement of alleles between populations via migration. Effect: homogenizes populations (makes them more similar). High gene flow → low FST (genetic differentiation). Low gene flow → populations diverge by drift/selection. Gene flow can PREVENT local adaptation by constantly importing alleles from other environments.
Mutation
Mutation = changes in DNA sequence. The ONLY source of NEW alleles that did not previously exist. Mutations are RANDOM with respect to fitness — not directed by need. Selection acting on mutations is NON-RANDOM. Mutations are rare events, so mutation pressure alone changes allele frequencies slowly.
The 5 Forces That Change Allele Frequencies
⚠ Trap — Only Mutation Creates NEW Alleles

Gene flow, drift, selection, and non-random mating only REDISTRIBUTE existing variation. Only MUTATION introduces truly new alleles. On multiple choice: "which force is the ultimate source of all new genetic variation?" = MUTATION. Every other force works with pre-existing alleles.

⚠ Trap — Mutations Are Random; Selection Is Not

Mutations are random with respect to their EFFECTS on fitness. They do NOT occur “because” the organism needs them. Antibiotic resistance: bacteria don't mutate IN RESPONSE to antibiotics — pre-existing random mutations are selected when antibiotics kill susceptibles. The mutation was random; the selection is non-random.

Q6
FST values near zero between two populations indicate:
✅ B. FST = fixation index for subpopulations. FST near 0 = little genetic differentiation = populations have similar allele frequencies. This happens when gene flow is HIGH (migrants constantly move alleles between populations, preventing divergence). High FST would indicate restricted gene flow + independent drift/selection in each population.
Q7
A population of bacteria is treated with a new antibiotic. Within 2 weeks, some bacteria survive. The CORRECT interpretation is:
✅ C. Mutations are RANDOM. Resistance alleles existed before the antibiotic was applied (as pre-existing variation from prior random mutations). The antibiotic didn't create them — it SELECTED for them by eliminating susceptibles. This is also why stopping antibiotics early is dangerous: resistant variants that were always there now have no competition.

Inbreeding & Non-Random Mating

What it is
Inbreeding = mating between closely related individuals. Effect: increases HOMOZYGOSITY (both alleles identical by descent) and decreases heterozygosity. KEY: inbreeding changes GENOTYPE frequencies (more homozygotes, fewer heterozygotes) but does NOT directly change ALLELE frequencies (p and q are unchanged).
Why it matters
Inbreeding depression: increased expression of deleterious recessive alleles in homozygotes. Population shows excess homozygotes compared to HWE expectations. Inbreeding does NOT cause evolution directly (no allele frequency change) but EXPOSES hidden recessive variation to selection.
⚠ Trap — Inbreeding Changes Genotypes, NOT Allele Frequencies

Inbreeding shifts GENOTYPE frequencies (more AA and aa, fewer Aa) but DOES NOT change p or q. You could calculate p from genotype counts before and after inbreeding and get the SAME p. This is why inbreeding violates HWE (changes genotype distribution) but is NOT itself a force that drives allele frequency change. However, inbreeding exposes recessives to selection, which THEN can change allele frequencies indirectly.

Q8
A population shows EXCESS homozygotes compared to Hardy-Weinberg expectations. Which mechanism is MOST likely NOT responsible?
✅ C is NOT responsible. Heterozygote advantage (e.g., sickle cell) produces EXCESS HETEROZYGOTES compared to HWE, not excess homozygotes. Selection maintaining both alleles (balancing selection) favors Aa over AA and aa, resulting in more Aa than predicted by HWE. ALL other options (A, B, D) produce excess homozygotes. On Robbins' exams, “Which is NOT” questions like this are common.
Q9
Inbreeding in a population changes:
✅ B. Inbreeding increases the probability of homozygosity (identical alleles by descent) without changing which alleles exist or at what frequencies. If p = 0.7 before inbreeding, it's still 0.7 after. But the 2pq heterozygote class shrinks and the p² + q² homozygote classes grow. This violates HWE assumptions but doesn't change allele frequencies directly.

Unit II Genetics & Genomics

From DNA to phenotype — and back to allele frequencies.

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium — At a Glance
What it is
The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) is the null hypothesis of population genetics: in a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation, gene flow, or drift, genotype frequencies p² + 2pq + q² = 1 remain constant across generations. Used to DETECT evolution by testing for deviations.
Calculation Steps
  1. Count genotypes (AA, Aa, aa) in the population
  2. Calculate allele frequencies: p = (2×AA + Aa) / 2N ; q = (2×aa + Aa) / 2N ; or simply p + q = 1
  3. Calculate EXPECTED genotype counts: p²×N (AA), 2pq×N (Aa), q²×N (aa)
  4. Compare OBSERVED vs. EXPECTED — excess homozygotes suggests inbreeding/population structure; excess heterozygotes suggests heterozygote advantage
⚠ Trap — HWE at One Locus ≠ No Evolution

HWE at one locus means that ONE locus, at ONE moment, shows no deviation. It does NOT mean the entire population is not evolving at other loci. Also: if a population meets HWE, you CANNOT conclude “no evolutionary forces are acting” — only that no forces are DETECTABLY altering genotype frequencies at that locus at that moment.

Q10
In a population of 500 individuals, you observe: AA = 245, Aa = 210, aa = 45. What is the frequency of the A allele (p)?
✅ C. p = 0.70. p = (2×AA + Aa) / (2×N) = (2×245 + 210) / (2×500) = (490 + 210) / 1000 = 700/1000 = 0.70. Then q = 1 - 0.70 = 0.30. Expected HWE counts: AA = 0.49×500 = 245, Aa = 0.42×500 = 210, aa = 0.09×500 = 45. These match observed! This population IS in HWE.
Q11
Using p = 0.7 and q = 0.3, what is the expected frequency of HETEROZYGOTES under HWE?
✅ B. 2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42. This is always the biggest genotype frequency class when both alleles exist at intermediate frequencies. Remember: p² + 2pq + q² = 0.49 + 0.42 + 0.09 = 1.0 ✓. If you observe FEWER heterozygotes than 0.42, suspect inbreeding. If MORE, suspect heterozygote advantage.
Q12
A recessive disorder occurs at frequency 1/10,000 in a large randomly mating population. What is the carrier (heterozygote) frequency?
✅ C. ~1/50. q² = 1/10,000, so q = 1/100 = 0.01. Carrier frequency = 2pq ≈ 2×(0.99)×(0.01) ≈ 0.0198 ≈ 1/50. CRITICAL insight: carriers are ~200× more common than affected individuals! (1/50 vs 1/10,000). Most recessive disease alleles in a population are hiding in heterozygote carriers, not in affected homozygotes.

Quantitative Genetics & Heritability

What it is
Quantitative traits are continuous (height, weight, beak size). Their phenotypic variance is partitioned: VP = VA + VD + VI + VE. Only VA (additive genetic variance) responds predictably to natural selection. Narrow-sense heritability h² = VA/VP.
ComponentSymbolDefinitionResponds to Selection?
Additive genetic varianceVAVariance due to additive effects of alleles (each copy adds a fixed amount)YES — determines h² and R
Dominance varianceVDVariance from allele interactions within a locus (heterozygote vs. homozygote)Less predictably
Epistatic/Interaction varianceVIVariance from interactions between different loci (gene × gene)Less predictably
Environmental varianceVEVariance from environmental effects on phenotype (non-genetic)NO
Estimating h²
  1. Measure trait values of parents and offspring
  2. Plot offspring phenotype (y-axis) vs. midparent phenotype (x-axis)
  3. Fit a regression line — the SLOPE of this parent-offspring regression = narrow-sense heritability (h²)
  4. Slope near 1.0 = highly heritable (offspring closely resemble parents). Slope near 0 = mostly environmental.
⚠ Trap — h² = VA/VP, NOT VG/VP

Broad-sense heritability H² = VG/VP (includes all genetic variance). NARROW-sense heritability h² = VA/VP (only ADDITIVE variance). The breeder's equation uses h², not H². Only additive variance produces predictable parent-offspring resemblance. Dominance and epistasis don't transfer cleanly across generations.

⚠ Trap — High Heritability Does NOT Mean Environment Is Unimportant

h² = 0.9 means 90% of VARIATION in a population is explained by additive genetics — it does NOT mean environment is unimportant for the trait. Human height h² ≈ 0.8, yet adequate nutrition is essential for achieving genetic potential. Heritability describes variation within a population, not whether a trait can respond to environmental change.

Q13
Which component of phenotypic variance is MOST directly responsible for the response to natural selection (R in the breeder's equation)?
✅ C. VA — additive variance is the component that parent-offspring regression captures. h² = VA/VP. R = h² × S = (VA/VP) × S. Dominance and epistasis (VD, VI) don't transfer as predictably across generations because the allele combinations that create them may not be preserved.
Q14
A parent-offspring regression for beak length in finches has a slope of 0.65. This means:
✅ B. The SLOPE of a parent-offspring regression IS narrow-sense heritability h². A slope of 0.65 means h² = 0.65. 65% of phenotypic variance is attributable to additive genetic effects. This is actually the reported heritability for beak length in the Grants' Galápagos finch study.

Phenotypic Plasticity & Reaction Norms

What it is
Phenotypic plasticity = one genotype produces different phenotypes in different environments. A reaction norm = a plot of phenotype (y-axis) vs. environment (x-axis) for a single genotype. Sloped reaction norm = plasticity. Non-parallel slopes between genotypes = genotype-by-environment interaction (G×E, VG×E).
Reading Reaction Norm Graphs
  1. FLAT line for a genotype = NO plasticity for that genotype (same phenotype in all environments)
  2. SLOPED line = plasticity (phenotype changes with environment)
  3. PARALLEL sloped lines for different genotypes = plasticity but NO G×E (all genotypes respond equally)
  4. NON-PARALLEL lines (crossing or different slopes) = G×E interaction (genotypes differ in HOW they respond to environment)
⚠ Trap — Polyphenic vs. Continuous Plasticity

Polyphenic traits = discrete phenotypes from one genotype (e.g., army ant castes, wasp queens vs. workers — same genes, completely different bodies based on larval conditions). Continuous plasticity = gradual phenotypic change with environment (e.g., plant height increasing with water). Both are phenotypic plasticity, but polypheny produces DISCRETE forms.

Q15
A reaction norm graph shows genotype A as a flat horizontal line and genotype B as a steep positive slope across environments 1-4. Which statement is CORRECT?
✅ C. Plasticity = slope of the reaction norm for a given genotype. Genotype A (flat) = no plasticity. Genotype B (steep) = high plasticity. G×E = non-parallel slopes between genotypes = they respond differently to environment. A flat line vs. a steep line = non-parallel = G×E. Crossing is ONE type of G×E but not the only type.

Genome Structure & Gene Expression Regulation

Genome composition
Eukaryotic genomes (including human) contain: protein-coding genes (~1.5% of human genome), pseudogenes (non-functional copies of genes), mobile genetic elements/transposons (~45% of human genome), introns, regulatory sequences, and other non-coding DNA. Genome SIZE does not correlate with organismal complexity (C-value paradox).
Level of RegulationMechanismWhen it Acts
Pre-transcriptionalDNA methylation, histone modification, chromatin remodelingBefore RNA is made — controls DNA accessibility
TranscriptionalTranscription factors binding promoters/enhancersDuring RNA synthesis — affects how much mRNA is made
Post-transcriptionalAlternative splicing, microRNA (miRNA), RNA degradationAfter RNA is made, before/during translation
Post-translationalProtein phosphorylation, ubiquitination, glycosylation, cleavageAfter protein is made — affects protein activity/stability
⚠ Trap — microRNA vs. DNA Methylation Levels

DNA methylation = PRE-transcriptional (controls whether a gene is accessible for transcription). microRNA (miRNA) = POST-transcriptional (binds to mRNA and promotes its degradation or blocks translation). These are TWO DIFFERENT levels. Robbins tests whether you know which regulatory mechanism acts at which stage.

Q16
DNA methylation of a gene's promoter region most directly affects gene expression at which level?
✅ C. DNA methylation at promoter regions causes chromatin condensation (heterochromatin), making the DNA physically inaccessible to transcription factors and RNA polymerase. This is a PRE-transcriptional mechanism — it prevents transcription from even beginning. miRNA acts post-transcriptionally; post-translational mechanisms act after protein is made.
Q17
Pseudogenes in a genome are BEST described as:
✅ B. Pseudogenes are DEAD GENES — sequences with strong homology to functional genes but containing mutations (frameshifts, premature stops) that prevent expression of functional protein. They're evolutionary relics. Humans have thousands of pseudogenes (e.g., olfactory receptor pseudogenes). They're important evidence of evolution (shared pseudogenes in related species = common ancestor had a functional copy).

Q18
Alfred Russel Wallace's key contribution to evolutionary biology was:
✅ C. Wallace independently developed natural selection working in Southeast Asia. His 1858 letter to Darwin describing his theory forced Darwin to act. Darwin had been developing his theory since 1838 but delayed publication. The threat of losing priority prompted the joint Darwin-Wallace presentation to the Linnean Society in 1858 and Darwin's publication of Origin in 1859.
Q19
The 'Great Chain of Being' differs from modern evolutionary theory in which fundamental way?
✅ D. The Great Chain of Being was a pre-scientific, pre-Darwinian concept. It proposed: (A) humans at top of a hierarchical chain of beings, (B) no evolutionary mechanism — species were fixed in their station, (C) species were static, immutable entities created in their current form. Modern evolutionary theory has all three elements: humans are one branch (not top), natural selection is the mechanism, and species change over time.
Q20
William Smith's major contribution to evolutionary thought was:
✅ B. William Smith (1769-1839) was a surveyor who noticed that each rock stratum contained a distinct, characteristic assemblage of fossils. Crucially: organisms in older (deeper) strata no longer existed — they had gone EXTINCT. This established extinction as a geological reality and provided a way to correlate and date rock layers worldwide. Lyell (C) proposed uniformitarianism.

CH5
Natural selection — 3 requirements
Answer
(1) Phenotypic VARIATION exists. (2) Variation is HERITABLE (genetic). (3) Variation affects FITNESS (differential survival/reproduction). ALL 3 required for selection to cause evolution.
CH5
Breeder's Equation
Answer
R = h² × S. R = response to selection (change in mean trait value in offspring). h² = narrow-sense heritability (V_A/V_P). S = selection differential (mean of breeders − mean of population).
CH5
Selection differential (S)
Answer
S = mean of SELECTED BREEDERS − mean of ENTIRE POPULATION. Larger S = more intense selection = faster evolution (if h² > 0).
CH5
h² = 0 means...
Answer
If heritability = 0, then R = 0 regardless of selection strength. No evolutionary change even with intense selection. All variation is environmental — not genetic.
CH5
5 forces that change allele frequencies
Answer
Natural selection, Genetic drift, Mutation, Gene flow (migration), Non-random mating (inbreeding). All 5 absent = Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.
CH5
Which force creates NEW alleles?
Answer
MUTATION ONLY. Selection, drift, gene flow, and non-random mating only REDISTRIBUTE existing alleles. Mutation is the ULTIMATE source of all genetic variation.
CH5
Genetic drift — key features
Answer
Random changes in allele frequency due to chance. Strongest in SMALL populations. Can fix deleterious alleles or lose beneficial ones by chance. Causes nonadaptive evolution.
CH5
Bottleneck vs. Founder Effect
Answer
Bottleneck = existing population dramatically shrinks (disaster). Founder Effect = small group colonizes new area. Both = reduced genetic diversity, non-representative allele frequencies. Both are extreme drift events.
CH5
Gene flow effect on populations
Answer
Homogenizes populations (makes allele frequencies MORE SIMILAR). High gene flow → low FST (little differentiation). Can prevent local adaptation.
CH5
FST interpretation
Answer
FST near 0 = low differentiation = high gene flow between populations. FST near 1 = high differentiation = isolated populations with independent evolutionary histories.
CH5
Inbreeding: what it changes
Answer
Changes GENOTYPE frequencies (increases homozygotes, decreases heterozygotes). Does NOT change allele frequencies (p and q remain the same). Causes inbreeding depression by exposing recessive deleterious alleles.
CH5
Is evolution completely random?
Answer
NO. Mutation and drift are random. Natural selection is NON-RANDOM (favors higher fitness). In large populations, selection dominates. In small populations, drift can overpower selection.

Ch 5-6 • HWE Ch 5-6 • HWE & Genetics

CH6
HWE formula
Answer
p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Where p = freq(A allele), q = freq(a allele), p² = freq(AA), 2pq = freq(Aa), q² = freq(aa). Also p + q = 1.
CH6
HWE 5 assumptions
Answer
(1) No natural selection, (2) No mutation, (3) No gene flow, (4) No genetic drift (infinite population), (5) Random mating. ALL must hold for equilibrium.
CH6
HWE: excess homozygotes — 3 causes
Answer
(1) Inbreeding (non-random mating), (2) Population subdivision/Wahlund effect, (3) Selection AGAINST heterozygotes. Excess homozygotes ≠ excess heterozygotes.
CH6
HWE: excess heterozygotes — cause
Answer
Heterozygote advantage (overdominance/balancing selection). Example: sickle cell HbA/HbS has higher fitness than either homozygote in malaria-endemic regions.
CH6
Narrow-sense heritability h²
Answer
h² = V_A / V_P. Additive genetic variance / total phenotypic variance. Estimated from SLOPE of parent-offspring regression. Range 0-1. Only this component responds predictably to selection.
CH6
V_P components
Answer
V_P = V_A + V_D + V_I + V_E. Additive + Dominance + Interaction(epistasis) + Environmental. Only V_A drives R in breeder's equation.
CH6
Parent-offspring regression slope = ?
Answer
Narrow-sense heritability h². Steep slope → high h² → offspring resemble parents → trait responds to selection. Flat slope → h² ≈ 0 → no parent-offspring resemblance → no evolutionary response.
CH6
Phenotypic plasticity — definition
Answer
One genotype produces DIFFERENT phenotypes in DIFFERENT environments. Reaction norm = plot of phenotype vs. environment for one genotype. Sloped reaction norm = plasticity present.
CH6
G×E interaction — what is it?
Answer
Genotype-by-Environment interaction: different genotypes respond DIFFERENTLY to environmental change. Seen as NON-PARALLEL slopes in reaction norm plots. Contributes V_G×E to phenotypic variance.
CH6
Polyphenic trait — example
Answer
One genotype can produce DISCRETE phenotypes depending on environment. Example: army ant castes, honeybee queens vs. workers — same genome, completely different phenotypes based on developmental conditions.
CH6
Genome composition — human
Answer
~1.5% protein-coding genes, ~45% mobile elements/transposons, ~8% pseudogenes and remnants, rest = introns, regulatory sequences, non-coding. Genome SIZE ≠ organismal complexity.
CH6
4 levels of gene expression regulation
Answer
(1) Pre-transcriptional: DNA methylation, histone modification. (2) Transcriptional: transcription factors. (3) Post-transcriptional: splicing, miRNA. (4) Post-translational: phosphorylation, cleavage. Robbins tests which is which.

Answer

TRAP: h² = narrow vs. broad sense
Answer
Narrow-sense h² = V_A/V_P (used in breeder's equation). Broad-sense H² = V_G/V_P (all genetic variance). The SLOPE of parent-offspring regression = h² (narrow-sense). Robbins may ask which is which.
TRAP
TRAP: High h² means environment doesn't matter?
Answer
NO. h² describes the proportion of VARIATION explained by additive genetics in a SPECIFIC POPULATION. It does NOT mean the trait is unaffected by environment. Human height h² ≈ 0.8 but adequate nutrition is still essential.

TRAP: HWE at one locus = population not evolving?
Answer
NO. HWE at one locus, at one moment, means no detectable deviation at THAT LOCUS. The population could be evolving at hundreds of other loci. Also: meeting HWE doesn't mean ALL 5 assumptions are satisfied — some forces can cancel each other out.

TRAP: Who was Wallace?
Answer
Alfred Russel Wallace INDEPENDENTLY conceived natural selection. He sent Darwin a letter in 1858 describing the theory. This PROMPTED Darwin to publish. Wallace ≠ just a collector — he was a co-discoverer of natural selection.
TRAP
TRAP: DNA methylation acts at which level?
Answer
PRE-TRANSCRIPTIONAL. Methylation of CpG islands at promoters condenses chromatin, PREVENTING transcription. microRNA acts POST-TRANSCRIPTIONALLY (degrades mRNA or blocks translation). Different levels.
TRAP
TRAP: Drift is always nonadaptive?
Answer
YES. Drift is random — it has no direction based on fitness. It can INCREASE deleterious alleles or DECREASE beneficial ones by chance. In small populations it can overpower adaptive selection completely.
TRAP
TRAP: Excess homozygotes vs. heterozygotes
Answer
Excess HOMOZYGOTES → suspect inbreeding, population subdivision (Wahlund effect), or selection AGAINST heterozygotes. Excess HETEROZYGOTES → suspect heterozygote advantage (balancing selection). Know which pattern goes with which mechanism.
