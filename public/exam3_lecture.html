How Did Life Originate?

Page 9
  • Chemists and physicists are actively working on how life arose from inorganic matter — abiogenesis
  • Conditions of the early Earth
    • No oxygen in the atmosphere — methane, hydrogen, ammonia, water, CO₂
    • Primary inputs of energy — lightning, ultraviolet radiation, volcanic eruptions, cosmic rays (high-energy charged particles from space)

Stanley Miller & Harold Urey — Prebiotic Soup Hypothesis

  • Prebiotic soup hypothesis — pools of molecules that existed in liquid form before life arose
  • Miller–Urey Experiment (1953): simulated early Earth; produced 20+ common amino acids (glycine, alanine, valine) plus purines (adenine, guanine) and pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine, uracil)
  • But how were amino acids joined into polymers?
    • Sydney Fox & colleagues (1977): mixed amino acids at high temperature (120°C) without water, then placed in water
    • Claudia Huber & colleagues (1997): stable peptide bonds form using carbon monoxide

Key Concepts
  • Phylogenetic trees map evolutionary relationships using shared traits (synapomorphies)
Page 12
  • Evolution is represented as a branching pattern, not a ladder of progress
  • Phylogenetic tree — visual representation of the evolutionary history of species, populations, or genes
  • The evolutionary history of a group is called its phylogeny

Components of a Phylogenetic Tree

  • Tips (leaves) — terminal ends of the tree (species, populations, molecules)
  • Branches — lineages evolving through time between divergence events (speciation)
  • Nodes — the point where a lineage splits (a speciation event)
  • Root — the ancestor from which all other nodes descend
  • Clade — an organism and all of its descendants (monophyletic group)
  • Sister taxa — lineages that diverged from the same immediate ancestor
  • Lineages can be removed or collapsed without changing phylogenetic relationships
  • Rotating branches around a node does NOT change the topology

Homoplasy: Character state similarity, but NOT due to shared descent (i.e., not a homology)

Two types of homoplasies:

Evolutionary reversals: the reversal of a derived character state back to its ancestral state

Convergent evolution: The independent origin of similar traits in separate evolutionary lineages, typically due to similar environmental pressures

Page 18

Homoplasy and Convergent Evolution

  • Homoplasy can create the mistaken impression that two species are closely related when they are not
  • Convergent evolution — similar traits evolving independently in unrelated lineages due to similar selective pressures
  • Reversals — a character returns to an ancestral state (also a form of homoplasy)

Parsimony and the Camera Eye

  • Camera eye of vertebrates and octopus — superficially similar but independently evolved
  • Parsimony test: homologous origin would require 4+ independent losses; convergent origin requires only 2 gains — parsimony favors convergence
  • T7 virus infects E. coli — another example of convergent molecular evolution used to test parsimony
  • Key rule: never infer homology from similarity alone; always use phylogenetic context and parsimony

Exaptation

  • Exaptation — a trait that originates performing one function but is later co-opted for a new function
    • Feathers: originally for insulation or display → later co-opted for flight
    • Swim bladder: ancestral air sac (for breathing) → co-opted for buoyancy control in ray-finned fish
  • Swim bladders are homologous with tetrapod lungs — both derived from a ventral outpouching of the gut in an early bony fish ancestor
  • The ancestral lung was co-opted as a buoyancy organ in ray-finned fish; the tetrapod lineage retained it as a breathing organ

What is a species?

~25 different definitions

Differ somewhat in their assumptions, but generally agree that:

  1. Species consist of groups of interbreeding individuals that share a common ancestry
  2. Species are evolutionarily independent units – different species follow different trajectories through time

Phylogenetic species concept

  • The smallest possible groups whose members are descended from a common ancestor (monophyletic)
  • Possess defining or derived characteristics (synapomorphies) that distinguish them from other groups
Page 26

Some problems

  • Traits that are used don’t have to be ecological or physiologically significant
  • Often divides organisms up into fine-grained species categories
  • Established phylogenies are not available for most groups

Morphospecies concept

  • Species defined on basis of measurable morphological differences
  • Difficult to apply in some groups (Bacteria, archaea, fungi)
  • Used mostly with fossils, but paleontologists need to be careful (no color, soft tissue or behavior present)
  • Some problems
  • Differences used to classify species can be defined arbitrarily and can vary among scientists

Biological species concept

  • Groups of interbreeding natural populations
  • Reproductively isolated from other such groups
  • Useful because definition focuses on the speciation process
  • Other concepts help recognize species, but this definition focuses on how populations are kept separate

Some problems

  • Reproductive isolation can't be tested on fossil species
  • This concept doesn't apply to asexual species
  • This concept has difficulties with hybridizing species

General lineage species concept

Species are metapopulations - Group of spatially separated populations that interact at some level (genetic exchange)

Various populations exchange alleles frequently enough to be treated as the same evolutionary lineage

Page 28

Metapopulations and Gene Flow

  • Metapopulation — a group of spatially separated populations connected by gene flow; individuals share the same gene pool, which remains fairly consistent over time
  • Gene flow — transfer of alleles from one population to another via dispersal and interbreeding; homogenizes allele frequencies between populations

Species Concepts Compared

  • Biological Species Concept (BSC) — looks at gene flow; species = groups of interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from others
  • Morphospecies Concept — looks at morphological differences; species = groups defined by measurable physical differences
  • Phylogenetic Species Concept (PSC) — looks at synapomorphies; species = smallest monophyletic group with defining derived characters
  • General Lineage Species Concept — looks at allele exchange frequency; species = separately evolving metapopulation lineages
  • In many cases, all concepts agree on species boundaries — conflicts arise in recently diverged groups, asexual organisms, and polyploids

Section 6 — Human Evolution: Key Concepts

Human Evolution — Key Concepts

  • Hominids vs. Hominins:
    • Hominidae (hominids) = great ape family: humans, chimps, gorillas, orangutans + ancestors
    • Hominini (hominins) = tribe within Hominidae: humans + our direct ancestors only
    • Hominins share: bipedalism, reduced canines, increased brain size relative to body
  • Bipedalism — earliest and most diagnostic hominin adaptation:
    • Began ~6–7 million years ago
    • Skeletal evidence:
      • Foramen magnum shifted to underside of skull (positions head above spine)
      • S-curved spine (absorbs walking shock)
      • Bowl-shaped, wide pelvis (stabilizes upright torso)
      • Valgus angle of femur (knees angled inward, under body's center of mass)
      • Non-opposable big toe (stable platform for push-off)
      • Arched foot (spring mechanism for locomotion)
    • Trade-offs of bipedalism: lower back pain, difficult childbirth (narrow birth canal vs. large brain)
    • Environment: Savanna hypothesis — African climate dried ~8–5 mya, forests retreated → open grassland → selection for efficient bipedal locomotion, heat dissipation, predator detection
  • Hominin Timeline:
    • ~7 mya: Sahelanthropus tchadensis (Chad) — oldest candidate hominin; foramen magnum position suggests bipedalism; near human-chimp divergence time; nicknamed "Toumaï"
    • ~6 mya: Orrorin tugenensis (Kenya) — femur morphology consistent with bipedalism
    • ~4.4 mya: Ardipithecus ramidus — mosaic anatomy; some bipedal, some arboreal traits
    • ~3.9–2.9 mya: Australopithecus afarensis ("Lucy") — definitive bipedalism (Laetoli footprints, valgus knee), brain ~450 cc (≈ modern chimp), long arms, upright pelvis
    • ~2.4 mya: Homo habilis — "handy man"; brain ~650 cc; Oldowan stone tools; first Homo
    • ~1.9 mya: Homo erectus — brain ~900 cc; Acheulean handaxes; first hominin to leave Africa; fire control; reached Europe, Asia (Peking Man, Java Man)
    • ~300 kya: Homo sapiens — arose in Africa; fully modern anatomy ~100 kya; left Africa in multiple waves; some introgression with Neanderthals (1–4% in non-Africans)
  • Key Evidence:
    • Laetoli footprints (Tanzania, 3.6 mya): preserved bipedal footprints in volcanic ash; attributed to A. afarensis; direct behavioral evidence of bipedalism before large brains
    • Brain expansion pattern: bipedalism PRECEDES brain expansion — Lucy walked upright but had chimp-sized brain; large brains came later with Homo genus
    • Molecular clock: humans and chimps diverged ~6–7 mya (consistent with fossil record)
    • Archaic admixture: ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA in non-African humans;

~4–6% Denisovan DNA in Melanesians/Aboriginal Australians (Denisovans: sister to Neanderthals, known mainly from Siberian cave DNA; contributed EPAS1 altitude gene to Tibetans)

  • Models of Modern Human Origins:
    • Out of Africa (replacement): H. sapiens evolved in Africa ~300 kya, spread globally and largely replaced archaic humans — SUPPORTED by evidence
    • Multiregional: H. erectus populations worldwide evolved in parallel — NOT well supported
    • Current consensus: Out of Africa + limited archaic admixture (Neanderthal/Denisovan)
  • Tool Cultures (shows increasing cognitive complexity):
    • Oldowan (~2.6 mya): simple flakes and choppers; associated with H. habilis
    • Acheulean (~1.7 mya): symmetrical bifacial handaxes; associated with H. erectus; remarkably stable for ~1.5 million years
    • Brain size evolution: Australopithecus ~450 cc → H. habilis ~650 cc → H. erectus ~900 cc

→ H. sapiens ~1350–1400 cc; correlated with tool complexity + social group size

  • Cooking Hypothesis (Wrangham): cooking food → higher caloric yield → reduced gut size needed

→ energy freed for brain growth; H. erectus first controlled fire (~800 kya); brain expansion

+ reduced gut size correlated in fossil record

Section 7 — Evolutionary Medicine: Key Concepts

Evolutionary Medicine — Key Concepts

  • Definition: Application of evolutionary biology to understanding, preventing, and treating disease.

Core insight: many diseases result from evolutionary history, trade-offs, mismatches, or co-evolution — NOT from design flaws or random chance.

  • Why Do We Get Sick? (5 Evolutionary Reasons):
  1. Co-evolution with pathogens (arms race — pathogens evolve faster)
  2. Evolutionary mismatch (modern environment differs from ancestral environment)
  3. Trade-offs (alleles beneficial early cause disease later — antagonistic pleiotropy)
  4. Constraints (evolution cannot always produce an optimal design)
  5. Defenses with costs (fever, inflammation, nausea are adaptive but costly)
  • Co-evolution (Host — Pathogen Arms Race):
    • Each species is a selective pressure on the other — reciprocal evolutionary change
    • Host evolves resistance → pathogen evolves immune evasion → cycle repeats
    • Red Queen Hypothesis: hosts must constantly evolve just to maintain current fitness against rapidly evolving pathogens (named after Lewis Carroll's Red Queen)
    • Sexual reproduction advantage: shuffles alleles each generation → novel host genotypes → pathogens cannot adapt to a single common genotype
    • MHC (major histocompatibility complex) genes are most polymorphic in genome; frequency-dependent selection maintains diversity (rare MHC types resist current pathogens)
  • Virulence and Transmission Trade-off:
    • Virulence = degree of harm pathogen causes to host
    • Trade-off: very high virulence → kills host quickly → fewer secondary infections
    • Very low virulence → easily outcompeted by more virulent strains
    • Optimal (evolved) virulence = intermediate — maximizes transmission
    • Exception: if vector transmits pathogen (malaria via mosquito), host can be bedridden → high virulence can evolve; waterborne diseases (cholera) evolved high virulence similarly
  • Antibiotic Resistance — Evolution in Real Time:
    • Mechanism: antibiotics create strong selection pressure; susceptible bacteria die; resistant mutants survive and reproduce (natural selection, textbook example)
    • Resistance spreads via:
      • Vertical transmission (daughter cells inherit resistance)
      • Horizontal gene transfer: conjugation (plasmids), transformation (naked DNA uptake), transduction (bacteriophage-mediated) — spreads resistance across species boundaries
    • Resistance evolves faster with: large bacterial populations, high antibiotic doses, broad-spectrum antibiotics, incomplete treatment courses
    • Key lesson: human overuse of antibiotics accelerates selection for resistance
  • Evolutionary Mismatch:
    • Many modern diseases result from mismatch between evolved adaptations (Pleistocene hunter-gatherer environment) and current modern environment
    • Examples:
      • Obesity/Type 2 diabetes: fat storage was adaptive in food-scarce past; now high-calorie diet + sedentary lifestyle → metabolic disease
      • Hypertension: kidneys evolved to retain salt (rare in Pleistocene); modern high-sodium diet → kidneys retain too much → high blood pressure
      • Myopia: eyes adapted for outdoor light; modern indoor/screen lifestyle
      • Allergies/autoimmune disease: Hygiene hypothesis — reduced pathogen exposure → immune system overactivates against harmless antigens
  • Antagonistic Pleiotropy:
    • Same gene increases fitness early in life but causes disease later in life
    • Evolution favors early-life benefits even at cost of late-life disease (selection is weak after reproductive age)
    • Example: testosterone → enhanced muscle mass + reproduction (early) → prostate cancer + cardiovascular disease (late)
    • Explains aging: genes with positive effects early are selected for even if harmful in old age (Williams 1957)
  • Classic Example — Sickle-Cell Anemia and Malaria:
    • HbS allele (sickle-cell) causes severe anemia in homozygotes (HbS/HbS)
    • Heterozygotes (HbA/HbS) resist Plasmodium falciparum malaria
    • In malaria-endemic regions: heterozygotes have highest fitness → balancing selection maintains both alleles in population
    • Shows: disease-causing allele can be maintained if it provides protection against a more deadly pathogen (antagonistic pleiotropy at population level)
  • Adaptive Symptoms (Not Always Bad):
    • Fever: many pathogens replicate poorly at high temperatures; fever = host defense; suppressing fever may hinder immune response (though extreme fever is harmful)
    • Nausea in pregnancy: peaks during organogenesis (weeks 6–12); protects embryo from dietary toxins; women with morning sickness have LOWER miscarriage rates
    • Cough, diarrhea: may expel pathogens; suppressing may prolong infection
  • Vestigial Structures (medical relevance):
    • Appendix: lymphoid tissue, site of appendicitis; remnant of cecum for leaf digestion
    • Wisdom teeth: jaw too small for modern diet (softer food → less jaw development)
    • Goosebumps: pilomotor reflex (hair-raising); ancestral heat retention/threat display

Perhaps a last call for Illinois’ prairie chicken

Bird known for its booming mating ritual is in danger of disappearing from
low numbers

April 13, 2013 | By Andy Grimm, Chicago Tribune reporter

The greater prairie chickens have begun their
mating ritual on restored grassland habitat
some 220 miles south of Chicago, and this
year the showmanship matches the stakes for
Illinois’ dwindling flock.

For hundreds of years, the pageantry of male
prairie chickens has made for one of the most
entertaining spectacles in Midwest bird-
watching. The pre-dawn din from thousands
of lovesick males making their "booming"
mating call was a rite of spring well into the
1960s.
